Unit 5 · Topic 5.3

Money Growth and Inflation

The quantity theory says M x V = P x Y.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: Interpretation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Germany in 1923 and Zimbabwe in 2008 both printed money to pay government bills, and prices rose so fast that shops changed price tags several times a day. Few economies go that far, but the same rule works at any speed: when the money supply grows much faster than the output it buys, the price level rises.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The money supply grows faster than real output→nominal spending, M x V, grows faster than output
  2. Nominal spending grows faster→AD shifts right
  3. Output above full employment pushes nominal wages up→SRAS shifts left until output returns to full employment
  4. Real output ends where it started→the price level is higher: faster money growth means higher inflation

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The equation of exchange: M×V=P×YM \times V = P \times Y. M is the money supply, V velocity, P the price level and Y real output, so P×YP \times Y is nominal GDP.
  • Velocity is how often the average dollar is spent on final goods and services in a year: V=P×YMV = \frac{P \times Y}{M}.
  • In the long run, V is steady and Y is set by resources and technology, so P moves with M. In growth rates: inflation ≈\approx money growth + velocity growth - real output growth.
  • Money neutrality: in the long run money changes nominal variables (the price level, nominal GDP, nominal wages and interest rates), not real GDP or unemployment. In the short run, sticky wages let money change output.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Printing more money makes a country richer in the long run.

What actually happens: Real output depends on resources and technology. Extra money with no extra output raises the price level, so each dollar buys less; real GDP ends where it started.

3. In the table, the money supply grows 10 percent while velocity and real GDP stay the same. Which statement fits the quantity theory?

  1. Real GDP rises 10 percent, and the price level is unchanged
  2. The price level rises by the same 10 percent, and real output is unchanged
  3. Velocity falls 10 percent, so nothing else changes
  4. The price level falls 10 percent, since each dollar can now buy more output
Show the answer

If VV and YY are fixed, M×V=P×YM \times V = P \times Y means PP moves in proportion to MM.

  • Real GDP rises 10 percent, and the price level is unchanged: The table shows real GDP unchanged; with V fixed, the extra money shows up in P.
  • Correct: The price level rises by the same 10 percent, and real output is unchanged: Right: with V and Y fixed, P must rise in proportion to M so that both sides of the equation stay equal.
  • Velocity falls 10 percent, so nothing else changes: The table holds velocity at 4 in both years.
  • The price level falls 10 percent, since each dollar can now buy more output: More money chasing the same output raises prices; each dollar buys less.

5. A central bank permanently raises the money supply. Which of these changes in the long run?

  1. Real GDP
  2. The natural rate of unemployment
  3. The real interest rate
  4. The price level
Show the answer

The long-run neutrality of money: money changes nominal variables, not real ones.

  • Real GDP: Real GDP returns to full-employment output, which depends on resources and technology.
  • The natural rate of unemployment: The natural rate depends on frictional and structural unemployment, not on the amount of money.
  • The real interest rate: In the long run the real interest rate is set by saving and investment, not by the money supply.
  • Correct: The price level: Right: in the long run the extra money raises the price level and other nominal values.

6. Velocity rises because people start paying with cards and spend money faster. If the money supply and real GDP stay the same, what happens to the price level?

  1. It rises, because M x V is now larger while Y is unchanged
  2. It falls, because money is spent faster and so is worth more
  3. It stays the same, because the money supply did not change
  4. It rises by exactly the rise in the money supply
Show the answer

Both M and V feed nominal spending: with Y fixed, a rise in either raises the price level.

  • Correct: It rises, because M x V is now larger while Y is unchanged: Right: P = (M x V) / Y, so a larger V raises P.
  • It falls, because money is spent faster and so is worth more: Faster spending of the same money raises nominal spending, which raises prices.
  • It stays the same, because the money supply did not change: The price level depends on M x V, not on M alone.
  • It rises by exactly the rise in the money supply: The money supply did not change; the rise comes from velocity.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The quantity theory says M x V = P x Y. With velocity steady and real output fixed by resources and technology in the long run, the price level rises in proportion to the money supply, so sustained inflation comes from money growing faster than output. Money is neutral in the long run: it changes nominal variables, not real GDP or unemployment, although it can change output in the short run while wages are sticky.
