Unit 5 · Topic 5.2

The Phillips Curve

The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: changes in AD trade unemployment for inflation along it.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: InterpretationPractice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In the 1960s, years when jobs were easy to find also tended to be years when prices rose fast. Then in the 1970s, after oil prices jumped, many countries had high inflation and high unemployment at the same time. The Phillips curves explain both: a short-run trade-off that holds while expectations stay put, and a long run with no trade-off at all.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. AD shifts right→real output rises and the price level rises faster
  2. Output rises→unemployment falls below the natural rate
  3. Unemployment falls while inflation rises→the economy moves up and to the left along the SRPC
  4. Inflation stays above what people expected→expected inflation rises and nominal wages rise
  5. Expected inflation rises→the SRPC shifts right, and unemployment returns to the natural rate at a higher inflation rate

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Axes: Inflation rate on the vertical axis, Unemployment rate on the horizontal one. SRPC slopes down; LRPC is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
  • A change in AD is a movement along the SRPC: AD right means lower unemployment and higher inflation; AD left the reverse.
  • The SRPC shifts when SRAS shifts or expected inflation changes. A negative supply shock or higher expected inflation shifts it right; a positive supply shock or lower expected inflation shifts it left.
  • Where SRPC crosses LRPC, actual inflation equals expected inflation. LRPC matches LRAS: a change in the natural rate shifts LRPC.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A rise in AD shifts the short-run Phillips curve.

What actually happens: A rise in AD moves the economy along the SRPC, to lower unemployment and higher inflation. The SRPC itself shifts only when SRAS shifts (a supply shock) or when expected inflation changes.

3. Between which two years did Velor most likely move along a single short-run Phillips curve after a rise in AD?

  1. Year 1 to Year 2
  2. Year 2 to Year 3
  3. Year 3 to Year 4
  4. Year 1 to Year 3
Show the answer

Along a given SRPC, unemployment and inflation move in opposite directions. Only Year 1 to Year 2 fits.

  • Correct: Year 1 to Year 2: Right: unemployment fell and inflation rose, the inverse short-run trade-off a rise in AD produces.
  • Year 2 to Year 3: Both unemployment and inflation rose; a movement along one SRPC changes them in opposite directions.
  • Year 3 to Year 4: Both rose again, which points to a shift of the SRPC, not a movement along it.
  • Year 1 to Year 3: Unemployment is the same in both years while inflation is higher, so the economy is on a different SRPC.

4. In Year 3, unemployment is back at the natural rate, but inflation is 4 percent instead of the 2 percent of Year 1. What best explains this?

  1. The natural rate of unemployment fell, so LRPC shifted left and inflation rose along it
  2. AD fell, so the economy moved down along the SRPC toward higher inflation
  3. Expected inflation rose, so the SRPC shifted right and the economy returned to LRPC at a higher inflation rate
  4. A positive supply shock shifted the SRPC left, raising inflation
Show the answer

In the long run the economy is on LRPC at the natural rate, and inflation equals expected inflation. A higher inflation rate at the natural rate means expected inflation rose.

  • The natural rate of unemployment fell, so LRPC shifted left and inflation rose along it: Unemployment is still 5 percent, the stated natural rate, so LRPC did not move.
  • AD fell, so the economy moved down along the SRPC toward higher inflation: A fall in AD moves the economy down the SRPC to lower inflation and higher unemployment.
  • Correct: Expected inflation rose, so the SRPC shifted right and the economy returned to LRPC at a higher inflation rate: Right: once people expect higher inflation, the SRPC crosses LRPC at that higher rate, so unemployment returns to 5 percent with inflation at 4 percent.
  • A positive supply shock shifted the SRPC left, raising inflation: A leftward SRPC shift lowers inflation at each unemployment rate.

5. From Year 3 to Year 4, both unemployment and inflation rose. Which event fits that change?

  1. A rise in government spending on roads that shifted AD right
  2. A sharp rise in energy prices that shifted SRAS left
  3. A fall in consumer confidence that shifted AD left
  4. A fall in the natural rate of unemployment
Show the answer

When inflation and unemployment rise together, the SRPC has shifted right; a negative supply shock is the usual cause.

  • A rise in government spending on roads that shifted AD right: More AD lowers unemployment while it raises inflation.
  • Correct: A sharp rise in energy prices that shifted SRAS left: Right: a negative supply shock raises the price level and lowers output, so inflation and unemployment rise together.
  • A fall in consumer confidence that shifted AD left: Less AD lowers inflation while it raises unemployment.
  • A fall in the natural rate of unemployment: A lower natural rate would let unemployment fall, not rise, in the long run.

6. Select every event that shifts the short-run Phillips curve to the right.

  1. A rise in expected inflation
  2. A rise in the price of oil used across the economy
  3. A rise in government spending
  4. A fall in consumer spending
Show the answer

Supply shocks and changes in expected inflation shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.

  • Correct: A rise in expected inflation: Right: higher expected inflation raises inflation at each unemployment rate.
  • Correct: A rise in the price of oil used across the economy: Right: a negative supply shock shifts SRAS left and the SRPC right.
  • A rise in government spending: That shifts AD right: a movement along the SRPC, not a shift.
  • A fall in consumer spending: That shifts AD left: a movement down the SRPC.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: changes in AD trade unemployment for inflation along it. Supply shocks and changes in expected inflation shift it. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment, matching LRAS, so in the long run unemployment returns to the natural rate and only inflation depends on what people expect.
