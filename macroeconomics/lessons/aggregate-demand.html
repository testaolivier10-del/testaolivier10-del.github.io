Unit 3 · Topic 3.1

Aggregate Demand (AD)

Aggregate demand is total planned spending on real output at each price level, C + I + G + (X - M).

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: InterpretationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In the spring of a strong year, car dealers sell out, factories hire, and new houses go up on every edge of town. Then the stock market drops and the mood flips: families put off buying, firms cancel orders. Nothing about the country's workers or machines changed overnight. What changed was how much everyone planned to spend. Aggregate demand is that total plan, for the whole economy at once.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The price level falls→savings buy more, interest rates fall and domestic goods get cheaper than foreign goods
  2. Real wealth rises, borrowing gets cheaper and exports rise→consumption, investment and net exports rise
  3. Spending rises because the price level fell→real GDP demanded rises: a movement down along AD
  4. Spending changes for any other reason (wealth, expectations, taxes, foreign incomes)→the whole AD curve shifts

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Aggregate demand (AD) is the total real output that households, firms, the government and foreigners plan to buy at each price level: AD=C+I+G+(X−M)AD = C + I + G + (X - M).
  • AD slopes down because of three effects of a lower price level: the real wealth effect (savings buy more), the interest rate effect (lower interest rates raise investment) and the exchange rate effect (domestic goods are cheaper than foreign goods).
  • A change in the price level moves the economy along AD. A change in C, I, G or X - M for any other reason shifts AD.
  • Shifters: wealth, consumer and business expectations, household debt, taxes, interest rates at a given price level, government purchases, foreign incomes.
  • The axes are always Price level (vertical) and Real GDP (horizontal), never "price" and "quantity".

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: AD slopes down for the same reason a demand curve for pizza does: people switch to it when it gets cheaper.

What actually happens: When the whole price level falls, there is no cheaper domestic good to switch to. AD slopes down because of the real wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the exchange rate effect.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. Which pair of labels belongs on the axes of an aggregate demand graph?

  1. Price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis
  2. Price level on the vertical axis and real GDP on the horizontal axis
  3. Inflation rate on the vertical axis and unemployment rate on the horizontal axis
  4. Nominal GDP on the vertical axis and real GDP on the horizontal axis
Show the answer

The aggregate demand graph has the price level (PL) on the vertical axis and real GDP (Y) on the horizontal axis.

  • Price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis: Those are the labels for one market, such as the market for coffee. AD is the whole economy.
  • Correct: Price level on the vertical axis and real GDP on the horizontal axis: Right: AD shows total planned spending on real output at each price level.
  • Inflation rate on the vertical axis and unemployment rate on the horizontal axis: Those axes belong to a different graph. AD plots the price level against real GDP.
  • Nominal GDP on the vertical axis and real GDP on the horizontal axis: The vertical axis is a price index for the whole economy, the price level, not nominal GDP.

2. The price level falls while household wealth in dollars stays the same. Through the real wealth effect, what happens?

  1. Households feel poorer, so consumption falls and AD shifts left
  2. Consumption rises, so the AD curve shifts to the right
  3. The same savings buy more, so consumption rises and real GDP demanded rises along AD
  4. Nothing happens, because wealth measured in dollars did not change
Show the answer

The real wealth effect: a lower price level raises the purchasing power of wealth, so real spending rises, a movement down along AD.

  • Households feel poorer, so consumption falls and AD shifts left: A lower price level makes the same dollars worth more, so households feel richer, not poorer.
  • Consumption rises, so the AD curve shifts to the right: The change starts with the price level, so it is a movement along AD, not a shift of the curve.
  • Correct: The same savings buy more, so consumption rises and real GDP demanded rises along AD: Right: a lower price level raises the real value of savings, so households buy more; that is a movement down along AD.
  • Nothing happens, because wealth measured in dollars did not change: What matters is what the dollars can buy. With lower prices, the same dollars buy more.

3. Why does the aggregate demand curve slope down for a different reason than the demand curve for one good?

  1. When the price of one good falls, buyers switch to it from other goods, and the same holds for the whole economy
  2. A lower price level raises nominal incomes, so people buy more of everything
  3. Aggregate demand counts government spending, which rises when prices fall
  4. A lower price level changes real wealth, interest rates and the price of a country's goods against foreign goods
Show the answer

One good's demand slopes down partly because buyers switch goods. For the whole economy three effects do the work: the real wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the exchange rate effect.

  • When the price of one good falls, buyers switch to it from other goods, and the same holds for the whole economy: Switching between goods explains one market's demand. When the whole price level falls there is no other domestic good to switch from.
  • A lower price level raises nominal incomes, so people buy more of everything: A lower price level does not raise nominal incomes; it raises what a given income or savings can buy.
  • Aggregate demand counts government spending, which rises when prices fall: Government spending is set by policy, not by the price level, and it is not why AD slopes down.
  • Correct: A lower price level changes real wealth, interest rates and the price of a country's goods against foreign goods: Right: AD slopes down because of the real wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the exchange rate effect, which act on the whole economy.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Aggregate demand is total planned spending on real output at each price level, C + I + G + (X - M). It slopes down because a lower price level raises real wealth, lowers interest rates and makes domestic goods cheaper than foreign ones. A change in the price level moves along AD; a change in spending for any other reason shifts it.
