Unit 3 · Topic 3.3

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

SRAS shows the real output firms supply at each price level while wages and some prices are sticky, so it slopes up.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: InterpretationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

When gas prices jumped, a bakery owner faced a choice: raise the price of bread, bake less, or both. Her workers' wages were set in a contract for the year, and her flour supplier had promised a price. Across the economy, thousands of owners make choices like hers, and they explain why total output responds to prices in the short run and why a jump in costs can cut output everywhere at once.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The price level rises while nominal wages are fixed by contract→profit on each extra unit rises
  2. Profit per unit rises→firms produce more real output: a movement up along SRAS
  3. Input prices (wages, oil) rise at a given price level→costs per unit rise
  4. Costs per unit rise→firms supply less at every price level: SRAS shifts left

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) is the total real output firms supply at each price level in the short run, while nominal wages and some prices are fixed.
  • SRAS slopes up because of sticky wages and prices: when the price level rises, many costs do not, so more output is profitable.
  • A change in the price level moves along SRAS. A change in costs at a given price level shifts it.
  • SRAS shifts left when input prices rise (nominal wages, energy, raw materials), productivity falls, business taxes or costly rules rise, or expected inflation rises (workers come to expect faster price growth and raise wage demands). The reverse shifts it right.
  • A sudden, economy-wide change in input costs is a supply shock.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: When workers get higher wages they spend more, so a rise in nominal wages shifts SRAS to the right.

What actually happens: For firms, wages are a cost. Higher nominal wages with the same productivity raise the cost of each unit, so SRAS shifts left.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. Why does the short-run aggregate supply curve slope upward?

  1. A higher price level makes households richer, so they buy more
  2. Sticky wages make extra output more profitable when the price level rises
  3. Workers demand higher wages whenever output rises
  4. A higher price level brings more workers into the country, so output rises
Show the answer

In the short run, wages set by contract and some sticky prices do not adjust at once, so a higher price level makes more output profitable.

  • A higher price level makes households richer, so they buy more: That is about spending (AD), and a higher price level lowers real wealth rather than raising it.
  • Correct: Sticky wages make extra output more profitable when the price level rises: Right: output prices rise while many costs stay fixed for a while, so firms produce more.
  • Workers demand higher wages whenever output rises: If wages rose as fast as prices, profits would not rise and firms would not expand. It is wage stickiness that gives the slope.
  • A higher price level brings more workers into the country, so output rises: The slope comes from fixed costs and contracts in the short run, not from migration.

2. The price level rises by 5 percent, but workers' wage contracts will not be renegotiated for a year. What happens in the short run?

  1. Firms produce less, because the price level is higher
  2. SRAS shifts right, because firms' profits rose
  3. Firms produce more, since prices rose but wage costs did not
  4. Nothing changes until the wage contracts are renegotiated next year
Show the answer

Sticky nominal wages mean a higher price level raises profit per unit, so firms move up along SRAS to more output.

  • Firms produce less, because the price level is higher: A higher price level with sticky wages makes extra output more profitable, so output rises.
  • SRAS shifts right, because firms' profits rose: The price level is on the axis; its change moves along SRAS rather than shifting it.
  • Correct: Firms produce more, since prices rose but wage costs did not: Right: that is a movement up along SRAS.
  • Nothing changes until the wage contracts are renegotiated next year: Firms respond at once to higher selling prices; only wages wait for the contracts.

5. Which change moves an economy along its SRAS curve instead of shifting it?

  1. A rise in the price level
  2. A rise in nominal wages
  3. A rise in productivity
  4. A fall in the price of oil
Show the answer

Only the variable on an axis moves the economy along the curve. Input prices, productivity and taxes on firms shift it.

  • Correct: A rise in the price level: Right: the price level is on the vertical axis, so its change is a movement along SRAS.
  • A rise in nominal wages: Wages are an input price; a change in them shifts SRAS.
  • A rise in productivity: Productivity changes cost per unit and shifts SRAS.
  • A fall in the price of oil: Oil is an input; a change in its price shifts SRAS.

6. Make it specific: explain how a fall in oil prices affects short-run aggregate supply.

Build the answer part by part, then write it in your own words.

Show the answer

Cheaper oil lowers production costs, so firms supply more at every price level: SRAS shifts right.

  • An answer that earns the point: Oil prices fall, so firms' production costs decrease. Because each unit is cheaper to make, firms supply more real output at every price level, so SRAS shifts to the right.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

SRAS shows the real output firms supply at each price level while wages and some prices are sticky, so it slopes up. A change in the price level moves along SRAS. Changes in input prices, productivity, taxes and rules on firms, or the wages workers bargain for, shift it: higher costs shift SRAS left, lower costs shift it right.
