Unit 1 · Topic 1.5

Supply

The law of supply says that as a good's price rises, the quantity supplied rises, other things equal.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: InterpretationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

When the price of honey doubles, beekeepers find it worth tending extra hives. When a drought hits, the same beekeepers offer fewer jars even at the old price. Sellers respond to the price along one curve, and to their costs and conditions by moving the whole curve.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The price of a good rises→producing more becomes worthwhile even though extra output costs more
  2. Sellers respond to the higher price→the quantity supplied rises: a movement along the supply curve
  3. Input costs rise (or a tax is added, or sellers leave the market)→sellers offer less at every price
  4. Sellers offer less at every price→supply decreases: the whole curve shifts left

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Law of supply: as a good's price rises, the quantity supplied rises, other things equal.
  • A change in the good's own price changes the quantity supplied (a movement along the curve). Anything else changes supply (a shift).
  • Supply shifters: input costs, technology, taxes and subsidies on producers, expectations, and the number of sellers. Lower costs and more sellers shift supply right.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: When the price of a good rises, its supply increases.

What actually happens: A higher price raises the quantity supplied, a movement along the supply curve. Supply, the whole curve, shifts only when something other than the good's own price changes, such as input costs.

6. A drought raises the farm's costs, and it now offers 10 fewer jars at every price in the table. What happened?

  1. Supply decreased: the supply curve shifted left
  2. Quantity supplied fell along the same supply curve
  3. Supply increased, because honey became scarcer
  4. Demand for honey decreased
Show the answer

Fewer jars offered at every price, because of higher costs, is a decrease in supply: a shift left.

  • Correct: Supply decreased: the supply curve shifted left: Right: a cost increase shifts supply left.
  • Quantity supplied fell along the same supply curve: A movement along the curve needs a change in the honey price.
  • Supply increased, because honey became scarcer: Offering less at every price is a decrease in supply.
  • Demand for honey decreased: The drought changed the farm's costs, not what buyers want.

7. A reporter writes, "The price of gasoline rose, so the supply of gasoline increased." How should the sentence be fixed?

  1. "The supply of gasoline decreased"; higher prices lower supply
  2. "The quantity supplied of gasoline increased"; supply itself did not change
  3. "The demand for gasoline increased"; price affects buyers
  4. "Supply and quantity supplied both increased"
Show the answer

A change in the good's own price changes the quantity supplied (a movement along the supply curve). "Supply" means the whole curve.

  • "The supply of gasoline decreased"; higher prices lower supply: A higher price raises the quantity supplied; it does not lower supply.
  • Correct: "The quantity supplied of gasoline increased"; supply itself did not change: Right: a price change is a movement along the curve.
  • "The demand for gasoline increased"; price affects buyers: The sentence is about sellers, and buyers want less at a higher price.
  • "Supply and quantity supplied both increased": Only the quantity supplied changes; the curve stays put.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The law of supply says that as a good's price rises, the quantity supplied rises, other things equal. A change in the good's own price moves sellers along the supply curve; a change in input costs, technology, taxes or subsidies, expectations or the number of sellers shifts the whole curve. Higher costs and taxes shift supply left; lower costs, better technology, subsidies and more sellers shift it right.
