Unit 2 · Topic 2.1

The Circular Flow and GDP

The circular flow shows households selling factors of production to firms and buying their output, so spending on final goods equals income.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: Interpretation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A bakery sells a $300 batch of bread. The flour in it cost $120 and the wheat in the flour cost $50. How much did the economy produce: $300 or $470? A country adds up millions of sales like these to get one number for its output, and getting it right means knowing what to count, what to leave out, and why one person's spending is always another person's income.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Households spend on goods and services in the product market→firms receive that spending as revenue
  2. Firms receive revenue→they pay wages, rent, interest and profit to households in the factor market
  3. Households receive factor income→they spend it again, so total spending on final output equals total income
  4. GDP adds up spending on final goods only→intermediate goods, used goods, financial trades and transfer payments are left out

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year.
  • The expenditure approach: GDPGDP=C+I+G+(X−M)= C + I + G + (X - M). Imports are subtracted because they are already inside C, I and G but were made abroad.
  • In GDP, investment means new capital, new homes and added inventory, not buying stocks.
  • Left out: intermediate goods (double counting), used goods, stocks and bonds, and transfer payments. Counting each stage's value added gives the same total as counting final goods.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Adding up every sale in the economy, including the flour and the wheat, gives GDP.

What actually happens: Only final goods count. The bread's price already includes the flour and wheat, so adding them counts the same production two or three times.

Scenario

From wheat to a loaf of bread

In Country X a farmer grows wheat and sells it to a mill for $50. The mill grinds it into flour and sells the flour to a bakery for $120. The bakery bakes bread and sells all of it to shoppers for $300. Nothing else is produced along this chain this year.

3. How much does this chain add to Country X's GDP, in dollars?

Type a number. A four-function calculator is allowed, as on the exam.

Show the answer

Count only the final good, the bread sold to shoppers: $300. The wheat and the flour are intermediate goods; their value is already inside the price of the bread.

  • Answer: 300

4. Which of these are counted in this year's GDP of the United States? Select all that apply.

  1. A pension check mailed to a retiree
  2. A house built and sold this year
  3. A haircut bought at a local salon
  4. A car built in Ohio by a company based in Japan
  5. A ten-year-old car bought from a neighbor
  6. Shares of stock bought through an app
Show the answer

GDP is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country in a year. New production counts; used goods, financial trades and transfers do not.

  • A pension check mailed to a retiree: A transfer payment: nothing is produced in exchange, so it is left out.
  • Correct: A house built and sold this year: Right: a newly built home is counted, as residential investment.
  • Correct: A haircut bought at a local salon: Right: a service produced this year and sold at market value.
  • Correct: A car built in Ohio by a company based in Japan: Right: GDP counts what is produced inside the country's borders, whoever owns the factory.
  • A ten-year-old car bought from a neighbor: A used good was counted in the year it was made; reselling it produces nothing new.
  • Shares of stock bought through an app: A stock purchase is a financial exchange of ownership, not production of a good or service.

5. A bakery buys a new delivery truck made this year in the same country. In which part of GDP is the truck counted?

  1. It is an intermediate good and is not counted
  2. Investment
  3. Consumption
  4. Government purchases
Show the answer

Investment in GDP means spending on new capital (machines, tools, buildings), new homes and changes in inventories. Capital goods are final goods, not intermediate ones.

  • It is an intermediate good and is not counted: The truck is not used up in making one loaf; it is a capital good used for years, so it is a final good counted as investment.
  • Correct: Investment: Right: new capital goods bought by firms are investment spending.
  • Consumption: Consumption is spending by households on goods and services for their own use, not a firm's capital.
  • Government purchases: The bakery is a private firm, not the government.
