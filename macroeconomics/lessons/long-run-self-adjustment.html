Unit 3 · Topic 3.7

Long-Run Self-Adjustment

Left alone, an economy closes its own output gap through nominal wages and input prices.

Practice 2: InterpretationPractice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Suppose a country slides into a recession and its government does nothing at all. Do the empty factories and long unemployment lines stay forever? Economists say no: over time, as wage contracts come up for renewal and suppliers reset prices, the economy finds its way back to full employment. It is slow, and how it happens is the point the exam rewards most often in this unit.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Real GDP is below full-employment output (recessionary gap)→unemployment is above the natural rate
  2. Unemployment is above the natural rate→nominal wages and input prices fall as contracts are renewed
  3. Nominal wages and input prices fall→SRAS shifts right
  4. SRAS shifts right along AD→real GDP rises to full-employment output and the price level falls

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Long-run self-adjustment: with no policy, the economy returns to full employment because nominal wages and input prices adjust and SRAS shifts.
  • Recessionary gap: unemployment above the natural rate, nominal wages fall, SRAS shifts right; the price level falls and output rises to YF.
  • Inflationary gap: unemployment below the natural rate, nominal wages rise, SRAS shifts left; the price level rises and output falls to YF.
  • The mechanism is never a shift in AD. In the long run, a change in AD changes only the price level.
  • It can be slow, especially downward, because nominal wages resist cuts.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: An economy in a recession recovers on its own because spending picks up and AD shifts back to the right.

What actually happens: Self-adjustment runs through the supply side: high unemployment lowers nominal wages and input prices, so SRAS shifts right until output is back at full employment.

Check yourself

Country H's AD-AS graph today

1. If no policy is used, what happens to nominal wages in Country H over time, and why?

  1. They fall, because output is above full employment
  2. They stay the same, because wages are sticky
  3. They rise, because AD keeps shifting right
  4. They rise, as labor is scarce
Show the answer

Country H is in an inflationary gap: unemployment is below the natural rate, so nominal wages rise over time.

  • They fall, because output is above full employment: Output above full employment means scarce labor, which pushes wages up, not down.
  • They stay the same, because wages are sticky: Wages are sticky only in the short run; given time, contracts are renegotiated.
  • They rise, because AD keeps shifting right: The graph shows no further shift in AD. Wages rise because labor is scarce.
  • Correct: They rise, as labor is scarce: Right: output is above full employment, labor is scarce, and wages are bid up as contracts are renewed.

2. In Country H, which curve shifts as the economy adjusts on its own, and in which direction?

  1. SRAS shifts left
  2. SRAS shifts right
  3. AD shifts left
  4. LRAS shifts right
Show the answer

Rising nominal wages and input prices shift SRAS left: the self-adjustment always runs through SRAS.

  • Correct: SRAS shifts left: Right: higher nominal wages raise costs, so SRAS shifts left until it meets AD on LRAS.
  • SRAS shifts right: Rising wages raise costs, which shift SRAS left, not right.
  • AD shifts left: The adjustment works through wages and costs, which are SRAS, not through spending.
  • LRAS shifts right: LRAS moves with resources and technology, which have not changed.

3. Once Country H has fully adjusted, how do its price level and real GDP compare with today?

  1. The price level is lower and real GDP is lower
  2. The price level is higher and real GDP is lower
  3. The price level is higher and real GDP is higher
  4. Both are the same as today, since the gap closes itself
Show the answer

From an inflationary gap, SRAS shifts left: the price level rises further and real GDP falls back to YF.

  • The price level is lower and real GDP is lower: A leftward SRAS shift raises the price level.
  • Correct: The price level is higher and real GDP is lower: Right: SRAS shifts left along AD until output equals full-employment output.
  • The price level is higher and real GDP is higher: Real GDP falls back to full-employment output from above it.
  • Both are the same as today, since the gap closes itself: Closing the gap moves the economy: output falls to YF and the price level rises.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Left alone, an economy closes its own output gap through nominal wages and input prices. In a recessionary gap, high unemployment pushes wages down, SRAS shifts right, and output returns to full employment at a lower price level; in an inflationary gap, wages rise and SRAS shifts left, raising the price level. AD is not the mechanism, and in the long run AD changes only the price level.
