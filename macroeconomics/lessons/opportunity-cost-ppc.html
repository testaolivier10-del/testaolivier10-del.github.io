Unit 1 · Topic 1.2

Opportunity Cost and the Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)

Opportunity cost is the next-best alternative you give up, and the production possibilities curve shows it for a whole economy: every point on the curve is efficient, points inside mean idle resources, and points outside are out of reach for now.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: InterpretationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Tonight you can study for a test or work a shift that pays $80. Choose the test, and the cost is not zero: it is the $80 you did not earn. Countries face the same kind of choice, only bigger: more tractors means less wheat. The production possibilities curve puts that choice on a graph.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. An economy uses all its resources efficiently→it produces at a point on its PPC
  2. It moves along the PPC to make more of one good→it gives up some of the other good: the opportunity cost
  3. Resources are not equally suited to both goods→each extra unit costs more of the other good, so the PPC bows outward
  4. Resources grow or technology improves→the whole PPC shifts outward, and points once out of reach become possible

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next-best alternative given up. Per unit, it is what you give up divided by what you gain.
  • On the PPC: efficient. Inside: idle or misused resources (a recession). Outside: unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Bowed out means increasing opportunity cost (specialized resources); a straight line means constant opportunity cost.
  • More resources or better technology shift the PPC outward, which is economic growth; a disaster shifts it inward. Moving resources between the two goods is a movement along it.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Putting idle factories back to work shifts the PPC outward.

What actually happens: Idle resources put the economy inside its PPC; using them moves the economy toward the curve. Only more resources or better technology shift the curve itself outward.

5. What does the table show about Dornia's opportunity cost of wheat as it makes more wheat?

  1. It stays the same: each extra 10 million bushels costs 2 thousand tractors
  2. It rises: each extra 10 million bushels costs more tractors than the last
  3. It falls: wheat gets cheaper to make as Dornia makes more
  4. It is impossible to find from a table
Show the answer

The tractors given up for each extra 10 million bushels are 2, 4, 6 and 8 thousand: increasing opportunity cost, so the PPC is bowed out.

  • It stays the same: each extra 10 million bushels costs 2 thousand tractors: Only the first step costs 2 thousand; later steps cost more.
  • Correct: It rises: each extra 10 million bushels costs more tractors than the last: Right: 2, then 4, then 6, then 8 thousand tractors.
  • It falls: wheat gets cheaper to make as Dornia makes more: The cost of wheat rises, not falls, as Dornia moves along.
  • It is impossible to find from a table: The differences between rows give the costs directly.

6. Jordan's Saturday options, in order of preference, are a hike with friends, a paid work shift worth $80, and studying. If Jordan goes on the hike, what is the opportunity cost?

  1. The work shift and the studying, added together
  2. Nothing, because the hike is free
  3. The studying, because it ranks last
  4. The $80 work shift, the next-best choice
Show the answer

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative given up, here the work shift. Jordan could not have done both other options with the same time.

  • The work shift and the studying, added together: Jordan could have done only one of the other two, so only the best of them counts.
  • Nothing, because the hike is free: Even a free activity costs the time that could have earned $80.
  • The studying, because it ranks last: The lowest-ranked option is not what Jordan would have done instead.
  • Correct: The $80 work shift, the next-best choice: Right: only the next-best option counts.

7. A country's PPC is a straight line from 60 thousand shirts (and no jackets) to 20 thousand jackets (and no shirts). What is the opportunity cost of one jacket, in shirts?

Type a number. A four-function calculator is allowed, as on the exam.

Show the answer

Making all 20 thousand jackets means giving up all 60 thousand shirts, so one jacket costs 6020=3\frac{60}{20} = 3 shirts. A straight line means the cost is the same everywhere on it.

  • Answer: 3

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Opportunity cost is the next-best alternative you give up, and the production possibilities curve shows it for a whole economy: every point on the curve is efficient, points inside mean idle resources, and points outside are out of reach for now. A bowed-out PPC means increasing opportunity cost, a straight one constant cost. More resources or better technology shift the curve outward; moving resources from one good to the other is a movement along it.
