Unit 6 · Topic 6.3

The Foreign Exchange Market

The foreign exchange market is a supply and demand market for a currency.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: InterpretationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A carmaker based in Japan sells cars in Ohio and is paid in dollars, but it pays its workers in Japan in yen. A student from Ohio heading to Tokyo needs yen too. Every day millions of trades like these swap one currency for another, and the price they settle on is the exchange rate. That market can be drawn as a supply and demand graph, as long as you label it right.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Americans want more European goods, services or assets→they need more euros, so the demand for euros shifts right
  2. Demand for euros rises with supply unchanged→the price of the euro in dollars rises: the euro appreciates
  3. Americans pay for those euros with dollars→the supply of dollars shifts right in the market for dollars, so the dollar depreciates

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • In the foreign exchange market for a currency, the horizontal axis is the quantity of that currency and the vertical axis is its price in the other currency per unit: dollars per euro in the market for euros.
  • Demand for a currency comes from people abroad who want the country's goods, services and assets. Supply comes from the currency's own holders who want foreign goods, services and assets.
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper euro makes European goods cheaper for Americans, so they want more euros. Supply slopes up: a dearer euro makes American goods cheaper for Europeans, so they offer more euros.
  • One set of trades shows in both markets as mirror changes: more demand for euros is more supply of dollars.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: In the market for euros, the vertical axis is the number of euros per dollar.

What actually happens: In the market for euros, the vertical axis is the price of one euro: dollars per euro. The currency being traded is the one in the denominator of the price.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

Graph

The market for arls (Arland's currency), traded for bels (Belvia's currency)

1. What does the vertical axis of this graph measure?

  1. The price of the bel, in arls per bel
  2. The price of the arl, in bels per arl
  3. The interest rate on Arland bonds
  4. The price level in Arland
Show the answer

Market for arls: price of the arl, in bels per arl.

  • The price of the bel, in arls per bel: That is the flipped ratio. In the market for arls, the price is of one arl, in bels.
  • Correct: The price of the arl, in bels per arl: Right: in the market for a currency, the vertical axis is its price in the other currency per unit of it.
  • The interest rate on Arland bonds: Interest rates go on the money market and loanable funds graphs, not here.
  • The price level in Arland: The price level goes on the AD-AS graph; this graph prices a currency.

2. Who is on the demand side of this market?

  1. Arland residents who want to buy Belvian goods
  2. Belvian firms that sell their goods inside Belvia
  3. Belvians who want Arland goods, services or assets
  4. Arland households saving more in Arland banks
Show the answer

Demand for a currency comes from people abroad who want to buy the country's goods, services and assets.

  • Arland residents who want to buy Belvian goods: They pay with arls to get bels, so they supply arls.
  • Belvian firms that sell their goods inside Belvia: A sale inside Belvia is paid in bels and needs no arls.
  • Correct: Belvians who want Arland goods, services or assets: Right: to pay for anything from Arland, Belvians need arls, so they demand arls (and supply bels).
  • Arland households saving more in Arland banks: Saving at home in arls involves no trade of arls for bels.

3. Who is on the supply side of this market?

  1. Arland residents who want Belvian goods, services or assets
  2. Belvian tourists planning trips to Arland
  3. Arland exporters paid in bels by Belvian buyers
  4. Belvian savers buying Arland government bonds
Show the answer

Supply of a currency comes from its own holders who want to buy foreign goods, services and assets.

  • Correct: Arland residents who want Belvian goods, services or assets: Right: they sell arls to get the bels they need, so they supply arls.
  • Belvian tourists planning trips to Arland: Tourists from Belvia buy arls to spend in Arland, so they demand arls.
  • Arland exporters paid in bels by Belvian buyers: They trade the bels they earn for arls, so they add to the demand for arls.
  • Belvian savers buying Arland government bonds: Buying an Arland asset needs arls, so these savers demand arls.

4. Suppose the price of the arl were above its equilibrium on this graph. What would happen, and why?

  1. A shortage of arls would push the price of the arl up further
  2. The demand for arls would shift right until it met the price
  3. A surplus of arls would push the price of the arl down
  4. The supply of arls would shift left until it met the price
Show the answer

Above equilibrium, a surplus of the currency pushes its price down toward equilibrium.

  • A shortage of arls would push the price of the arl up further: Above equilibrium, the quantity supplied is larger than the quantity demanded: a surplus, not a shortage.
  • The demand for arls would shift right until it met the price: A price away from equilibrium causes a movement along the curves, not a shift.
  • Correct: A surplus of arls would push the price of the arl down: Right: at a price above equilibrium, more arls are offered than wanted, and sellers accept fewer bels per arl until the market clears.
  • The supply of arls would shift left until it met the price: The curves stay put; the price adjusts along them to the crossing.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The foreign exchange market is a supply and demand market for a currency. Draw it with the quantity of the currency across the bottom and its price in the other currency per unit up the side. Foreigners buying the country's goods and assets demand its currency; its own residents buying foreign goods and assets supply it. The same trades show up as the mirror change in the market for the other currency, so when one currency appreciates, the other depreciates.
