Unit 4 · Topic 4.1

Financial Assets

Financial assets are claims on money or future income: money, bonds and stocks.

Practice 1: Principles and ModelsPractice 2: Interpretation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Your grandparents bought a company bond years ago that pays a fixed amount every year. Then interest rates on new bonds jump. Their bond still pays exactly what it promised, yet if they tried to sell it today, buyers would offer less than they paid. Nothing about the bond changed. What changed is what else a saver can get, and that is the key to every financial asset.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Market interest rates rise→new bonds pay more than an existing bond with a fixed payment
  2. New bonds pay more than the existing bond→fewer buyers want the existing bond at its old price
  3. Fewer buyers want the existing bond→its price falls until its yield matches the new market rate

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • A financial asset is a claim on money or future income: money, bonds (loans to a government or firm) and stocks (shares of ownership).
  • Assets trade off three things: liquidity (how easily they become money at full value), risk and return. Money is the most liquid and pays little or no interest.
  • The interest rate is the price of borrowing: the yearly charge as a percent of the amount borrowed, and the reward a lender earns.
  • A bond pays a fixed amount, so its price moves opposite to market interest rates: rates up, bond prices down.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: When interest rates rise, bonds become more valuable because "bonds pay interest."

What actually happens: New bonds pay more, but existing bonds keep their old, lower payment, so their prices fall. Higher interest rates mean lower prices for existing bonds.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What does liquidity measure?

  1. How much interest an asset pays each year
  2. How likely an asset is to lose value
  3. How easily an asset becomes money without losing value
  4. How many people own the asset
Show the answer

Liquidity is the ease of turning an asset into money, the medium people spend, without a loss in value.

  • How much interest an asset pays each year: That is its return. Liquidity is about turning an asset into money.
  • How likely an asset is to lose value: That is risk. Liquidity is about how easily it becomes spendable money.
  • Correct: How easily an asset becomes money without losing value: Right: money is perfectly liquid; a house is far less so.
  • How many people own the asset: Ownership counts are not liquidity. Liquidity is about converting the asset to money.

2. Which statement compares stocks and bonds correctly?

  1. A stock is a loan to a firm; a bond is a share of ownership
  2. A stock is a share of ownership; a bond is a loan to the issuer
  3. Both are loans that pay a fixed interest rate
  4. Both are shares of ownership in the issuer
Show the answer

Stocks are equity (ownership); bonds are debt (loans), which pay set interest and repay the amount borrowed.

  • A stock is a loan to a firm; a bond is a share of ownership: That is reversed: a stock is ownership and a bond is a loan.
  • Correct: A stock is a share of ownership; a bond is a loan to the issuer: Right: stockholders own part of the firm; bondholders are lenders.
  • Both are loans that pay a fixed interest rate: A stock is not a loan and pays no fixed rate; its dividends and price can change.
  • Both are shares of ownership in the issuer: A bond gives no ownership. It is a promise to repay a loan with interest.

3. Market interest rates fall sharply. What happens to people who already own bonds paying a fixed 6%?

  1. They lose, because their payments are cut
  2. They gain, because their bonds' prices rise
  3. Nothing happens, because their rate is fixed
  4. They gain, because their bonds now pay more interest
Show the answer

Falling interest rates raise the prices of existing bonds, so current bondholders gain.

  • They lose, because their payments are cut: Their fixed payments do not change; the market value of their bonds rises.
  • Correct: They gain, because their bonds' prices rise: Right: their 6% bonds now pay more than new ones, so buyers pay more for them.
  • Nothing happens, because their rate is fixed: The fixed rate is why the price changes: their bonds become more valuable.
  • They gain, because their bonds now pay more interest: The yearly payment stays the same; the gain is a higher price for the bond.

4. Which of these are financial assets? Select all that apply.

  1. Shares of stock in a company
  2. A house a family lives in
  3. A delivery truck a bakery owns
  4. A meal eaten at a restaurant
Show the answer

Financial assets are claims on money or future income, such as money, bonds and stocks; trucks and buildings are physical assets.

  • Correct: Shares of stock in a company: Right: stock is a claim on a firm's profits and assets.
  • A house a family lives in: A house is a physical (real) asset: it gives shelter, not a claim on someone else's income.
  • A delivery truck a bakery owns: A truck is a physical (real) asset used to produce goods, not a financial claim.
  • A meal eaten at a restaurant: A meal is a good that is used up; it is not an asset at all.

5. In which year would the owner of the old bond most likely have to sell it for less than $1,000?

  1. Year 2
  2. Year 3
  3. Year 4
  4. Year 1
Show the answer

When the interest rate on new bonds rises above what an old bond pays, the old bond is worth less, so its price falls.

  • Correct: Year 2: Right: new bonds pay 8%, more than the old bond's 5%, so buyers offer less than $1,000 for it.
  • Year 3: New bonds pay 4% in Year 3, less than the old bond, so its price rises above $1,000.
  • Year 4: New bonds pay 5% again, the same as the old bond, so its price returns to about $1,000.
  • Year 1: In Year 1 the bond pays the market rate, so it sells for about the $1,000 it cost.

6. In Year 3, what most likely happens to the price of the old bond, and why?

  1. It falls, because the interest rate fell
  2. It rises, because it pays more than new bonds
  3. It stays at $1,000, because the payment is fixed
  4. It rises, because the bond pays more each year
Show the answer

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions: lower rates on new bonds raise the price of an old bond with a higher payment.

  • It falls, because the interest rate fell: A fall in market interest rates makes the old, higher payment more attractive, so its price rises.
  • Correct: It rises, because it pays more than new bonds: Right: $50 a year beats the 4% new bonds pay, so buyers bid its price above $1,000.
  • It stays at $1,000, because the payment is fixed: The payment is fixed, but its price is not: buyers compare it with what new bonds pay.
  • It rises, because the bond pays more each year: The yearly payment stays $50; the price rises because new bonds pay less, not because the payment grew.

Show the answer

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Financial assets are claims on money or future income: money, bonds and stocks. A bond is a loan to its issuer and a stock is a share of ownership. Assets trade off liquidity, risk and return, and money is the most liquid. The interest rate is the price of borrowing. Because a bond pays a fixed amount, its price moves opposite to market interest rates.
