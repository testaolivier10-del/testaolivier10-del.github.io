Unit 4 · Topic 4.6

Monetary Policy

Monetary policy is the central bank changing interest rates to shift aggregate demand.

Practice 2: InterpretationPractice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

When the news says "the Fed raised rates a quarter point," no one at the Federal Reserve sets your car loan rate. What it changes is the interest it pays banks on the reserves they keep there. Within days, rates on loans, mortgages and savings accounts move too, and over the next year, so does spending across the whole economy.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. With ample reserves, the central bank lowers its administered rates (interest on reserve balances)→the flat part of reserve demand moves down, so the policy rate falls
  2. The policy rate falls→other interest rates fall, so investment and interest-sensitive consumption rise
  3. Investment and consumption rise→aggregate demand shifts right
  4. Aggregate demand shifts right→real output and the price level rise and unemployment falls in the short run

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Monetary policy is the central bank changing interest rates to shift AD: expansionary (lower rates) for a recessionary gap, contractionary (higher rates) for an inflationary gap.
  • Limited reserves (money market graph): open market operations (buy bonds to raise the money supply, sell to lower it), the discount rate and the reserve requirement shift MS.
  • Ample reserves (reserve market graph): the central bank sets administered rates, mainly interest on reserves; the flat part of reserve demand moves with them, and so does the policy rate.
  • With ample reserves, shifting reserve supply in the flat part leaves the policy rate alone. Always say which framework a question sets.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: With ample reserves, the central bank lowers the policy rate by buying bonds and drawing a money supply shift on the money market graph.

What actually happens: With ample reserves, it lowers its administered rates and you draw the reserve market: the flat part of reserve demand moves down. Buying bonds and the money market belong to the limited-reserves framework.

3. What is the federal funds rate?

  1. The rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves
  2. The rate the government pays on its bonds
  3. The rate banks charge their best business customers
  4. The rate of inflation the central bank aims for
Show the answer

The federal funds rate, the rate on overnight loans of reserves between banks, is the Federal Reserve's policy rate.

  • Correct: The rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves: Right: it is the Federal Reserve's policy rate.
  • The rate the government pays on its bonds: That is a Treasury bond rate, which the policy rate influences but is not.
  • The rate banks charge their best business customers: That is a bank lending rate, which moves with the policy rate.
  • The rate of inflation the central bank aims for: That is an inflation target, not an interest rate.

4. The central bank operates with ample reserves. The graph shows the flat part of reserve demand moving down from DR1 to DR2. Which action caused this?

  1. Buying government bonds from banks
  2. Lowering the interest rate it pays on reserve balances
  3. Raising the required reserve ratio
  4. Lowering taxes on bank profits
Show the answer

With ample reserves, the central bank moves the policy rate by changing its administered rates, mainly the interest on reserve balances, which sets the height of the flat part of reserve demand.

  • Buying government bonds from banks: Buying bonds adds reserves, which would move the vertical supply curve; with ample reserves that leaves the rate alone.
  • Correct: Lowering the interest rate it pays on reserve balances: Right: banks will not lend reserves for less than the central bank pays them, so a lower administered rate lowers the flat part.
  • Raising the required reserve ratio: Reserve requirements are not how an ample-reserves central bank sets its rate.
  • Lowering taxes on bank profits: Taxes are fiscal policy; the shift shows a change in an administered rate.

5. In the graph, what happens to the policy rate (the federal funds rate) and the quantity of reserves?

  1. The policy rate falls and the quantity of reserves rises
  2. Both stay the same, since reserve supply did not move
  3. The policy rate falls and the quantity of reserves is unchanged
  4. The policy rate rises and the quantity of reserves falls
Show the answer

Lowering the administered rate lowers the flat part of reserve demand, so the federal funds rate falls; reserve supply, and so the quantity, stay put.

  • The policy rate falls and the quantity of reserves rises: Reserve supply did not move, so the quantity of reserves is unchanged.
  • Both stay the same, since reserve supply did not move: The flat part of demand moved down, so the rate where supply crosses it fell.
  • Correct: The policy rate falls and the quantity of reserves is unchanged: Right: the vertical supply now crosses demand at a lower flat part.
  • The policy rate rises and the quantity of reserves falls: The flat part moved down, so the rate fell.

6. After the change in the graph, what happens to business investment in the short run?

  1. It falls, because banks have fewer reserves
  2. It rises, because borrowing costs fall
  3. It does not change, because investment depends on taxes
  4. It falls, because savers earn less interest
Show the answer

A lower policy rate lowers other interest rates, which raises interest-sensitive spending such as business investment.

  • It falls, because banks have fewer reserves: The quantity of reserves did not change; interest rates fell.
  • Correct: It rises, because borrowing costs fall: Right: a lower policy rate pulls other interest rates down, so more investment projects pay off.
  • It does not change, because investment depends on taxes: Investment is interest sensitive: lower rates make borrowing cheaper.
  • It falls, because savers earn less interest: Lower rates encourage borrowing and spending; investment rises.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Monetary policy is the central bank changing interest rates to shift aggregate demand. With limited reserves, open market operations, the discount rate and the reserve requirement change the money supply, shown on the money market graph. With ample reserves, the Federal Reserve sets administered rates, mainly the interest on reserve balances; on the reserve market graph the flat part of reserve demand moves with them and the policy rate follows, while shifts in reserve supply leave the rate alone. Lower rates raise investment and interest-sensitive consumption, so AD rises; higher rates do the reverse.
