Unit 6 · Topic 6.5

Changes in the Foreign Exchange Market and Net Exports

When a currency appreciates, everything the country sells costs foreign buyers more and everything it buys from abroad costs its residents less.

Practice 2: InterpretationPractice 3: Manipulation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A farm-equipment maker in Iowa can have a great year at home and still lose sales abroad, without changing a single price. If the dollar gains value, each of its tractors costs a buyer in Brazil or Germany more of their own money. Multiply that by every exporter and importer, and a stronger currency moves a whole economy.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The dollar appreciates→US goods cost foreign buyers more of their currency, and foreign goods cost Americans fewer dollars
  2. US goods are dearer abroad and foreign goods cheaper at home→US exports fall and US imports rise
  3. Exports fall and imports rise→net exports (X - M) fall
  4. Net exports, a component of AD, fall→AD shifts left, so real GDP and the price level fall and unemployment rises in the short run

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Appreciation makes a country's exports dearer abroad and its imports cheaper at home, so net exports fall and AD shifts left, other things equal.
  • Depreciation does the opposite: exports get cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home, net exports rise and AD shifts right.
  • In a two-country story, one country's exports are the other's imports: when Arland's net exports fall, Belvia's rise.
  • Net exports are in the current account, so a fall in net exports lowers the current account balance and raises the capital and financial account balance by the same amount.
  • Higher domestic income raises imports, so net exports fall; income is not the same as competitiveness.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A stronger currency helps a country's exporters, because what they earn is worth more.

What actually happens: A stronger currency makes their goods cost more for foreign buyers, so they sell less abroad. Exports fall, imports rise and net exports fall.

3. Use the table. Other things equal, what happens to Arland's aggregate demand?

  1. It shifts right, because exports are still large
  2. It does not shift, because imports are not part of GDP
  3. It shifts left, because net exports fell
  4. Short-run aggregate supply shifts left instead
Show the answer

Lower net exports shift AD left.

  • It shifts right, because exports are still large: What moves AD is the change in net exports, and net exports fell.
  • It does not shift, because imports are not part of GDP: Net exports, exports minus imports, are part of AD; a rise in imports lowers net exports.
  • Correct: It shifts left, because net exports fell: Right: net exports are a component of AD (C + I + G + X - M), and they fell by 70 billion arls, so AD shifts left.
  • Short-run aggregate supply shifts left instead: A change in net exports is a change in spending, so it shifts AD.

5. Arland's real income rises during an expansion. A classmate predicts that Arland's net exports will rise because Arland is producing more. Which response is best?

  1. Right: more output means more goods to sell abroad
  2. Right, as long as the arl does not change in value
  3. Wrong: net exports do not depend on income
  4. Wrong: higher income raises imports, so net exports tend to fall
Show the answer

Higher domestic income raises imports, so net exports fall.

  • Right: more output means more goods to sell abroad: Exports depend mainly on foreign incomes and prices; higher Arland income mainly raises Arland's imports.
  • Right, as long as the arl does not change in value: Even with an unchanged exchange rate, higher Arland income raises imports, which lowers net exports.
  • Wrong: net exports do not depend on income: Income matters: higher Arland income raises imports.
  • Correct: Wrong: higher income raises imports, so net exports tend to fall: Right: Arland households spend part of their extra income on imported goods, so imports rise and net exports fall, other things equal.

6. After the arl appreciates, Arland's net exports fall while its income and transfers with the rest of the world do not change. What happens to its balance of payments?

  1. Its current account balance falls, and its capital and financial account balance rises
  2. Both balances fall by the same amount
  3. Its current account balance rises, because the arl is worth more
  4. Nothing changes, because the exchange rate is not in the accounts
Show the answer

Lower net exports lower the current account balance; the capital and financial account balance rises by the same amount.

  • Correct: Its current account balance falls, and its capital and financial account balance rises: Right: net exports are part of the current account, which falls; the two accounts sum to zero, so the capital and financial account balance rises by the same amount.
  • Both balances fall by the same amount: The two balances sum to zero, so when one falls the other rises.
  • Its current account balance rises, because the arl is worth more: Net exports fell, and they are part of the current account, so its balance falls.
  • Nothing changes, because the exchange rate is not in the accounts: The exchange rate is not an entry, but the trade it changes is.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

When a currency appreciates, everything the country sells costs foreign buyers more and everything it buys from abroad costs its residents less. Exports fall, imports rise and net exports fall, so aggregate demand shifts left and output and the price level fall in the short run. A depreciation works the other way round. Always state the link through net exports and AD, and remember that the trading partner sees the mirror change.
