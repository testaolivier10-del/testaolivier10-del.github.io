Unit 1 practice test: Basic Economic Concepts

twelve questions, every option explained

12 exam-style questions from the unit, free, with an explanation for every option. Most are multiple choice, as on the real exam; a few ask for a number or have you shift a curve on a graph, and sets share one data table, graph or scenario. Covers: Scarcity; Opportunity Cost and the Production Possibilities Curve (PPC); Comparative Advantage and Gains from Trade; Demand; Supply; Market Equilibrium, Disequilibrium, and Changes in Equilibrium.

Start at question 1

Data

Tables and chairs: hours per unit

Hours of labor needed to make one table or one chair in two invented countries (input data)
CountryHours per tableHours per chair
Ellis21
Fenwick62

1. Which country has the absolute advantage in chairs?

  1. Fenwick, because it needs more hours per chair
  2. Ellis, because it needs 1 hour per chair to Fenwick's 2
  3. Fenwick, because it has the lower opportunity cost of chairs
  4. Neither, because both need hours to make chairs
Show the answer

With input data, the absolute advantage goes to whoever needs fewer resources per unit: 1 hour beats 2.

  • Fenwick, because it needs more hours per chair: More hours per unit means less efficient, not more.
  • Correct: Ellis, because it needs 1 hour per chair to Fenwick's 2: Right: fewer hours per chair.
  • Fenwick, because it has the lower opportunity cost of chairs: Lower opportunity cost is comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.
  • Neither, because both need hours to make chairs: Absolute advantage compares the hours needed, and Ellis needs fewer.

2. Which country has the comparative advantage in chairs?

  1. Fenwick, because a chair costs it a third of a table and costs Ellis half a table
  2. Ellis, because it needs fewer hours per chair than Fenwick
  3. Ellis, because it is faster at making both goods
  4. Fenwick, because it needs more hours to make a table
Show the answer

A chair costs Ellis 12\frac{1}{2} of a table and Fenwick 26\frac{2}{6}, a third of a table, so Fenwick has the lower opportunity cost.

  • Correct: Fenwick, because a chair costs it a third of a table and costs Ellis half a table: Right: a third of a table is less than half.
  • Ellis, because it needs fewer hours per chair than Fenwick: Fewer hours per chair is absolute advantage.
  • Ellis, because it is faster at making both goods: Being faster at both gives Ellis the absolute advantage in both, not the comparative advantage in both.
  • Fenwick, because it needs more hours to make a table: Hours per table alone do not give the cost of a chair.

3. A student says, "Ellis needs fewer hours for both goods, so trade with Fenwick cannot help Ellis." What is wrong with the claim?

  1. Nothing: a country with the absolute advantage in both goods gains nothing from trading with others
  2. Ellis gains if Fenwick also has an absolute advantage in something
  3. Trade helps Ellis if it switches to making chairs
  4. Ellis gains by making tables and buying chairs at a price below its own opportunity cost
Show the answer

Gains from trade come from comparative advantage. Ellis gives up 2 chairs per table; if it can get a chair for less than half a table from Fenwick, it ends up with more of both.

  • Nothing: a country with the absolute advantage in both goods gains nothing from trading with others: Both sides can gain whenever their opportunity costs differ.
  • Ellis gains if Fenwick also has an absolute advantage in something: Absolute advantage is not needed for gains from trade.
  • Trade helps Ellis if it switches to making chairs: Ellis's comparative advantage is in tables, so it gains by making tables.
  • Correct: Ellis gains by making tables and buying chairs at a price below its own opportunity cost: Right: comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, drives the gains.

Scenario

A year in Hollis's electric scooter market

Hollis is an invented city where many people rent electric scooters by the ride. Over one year: (1) the price of gasoline jumps, making car trips more expensive; (2) a new university campus opens, bringing 10,000 students to the city; (3) the scooter company announces that ride prices will drop sharply next month; (4) the scooter company raises its own price per ride from $3 to $4.

4. Event (1): gasoline prices jump. What happens to the demand for scooter rides in Hollis?

  1. It decreases, because gasoline and scooter rides are complements for riders
  2. The quantity demanded rises along the same curve
  3. It increases, because scooter rides are a substitute for car trips
  4. It does not change, because scooters do not use gasoline
Show the answer

When car trips cost more, some riders switch to scooters, a substitute: demand for scooter rides shifts right.

  • It decreases, because gasoline and scooter rides are complements for riders: Complements are used together; scooters replace car trips instead.
  • The quantity demanded rises along the same curve: The scooter price did not change, so this is a shift, not a movement.
  • Correct: It increases, because scooter rides are a substitute for car trips: Right: a dearer substitute raises demand.
  • It does not change, because scooters do not use gasoline: The link is through car trips, which compete with scooter rides.

5. Event (2): 10,000 students arrive. How does this affect the scooter market?

  1. Demand decreases, because students have low incomes
  2. Demand increases, because there are more buyers
  3. Supply increases, because more scooters are needed
  4. Quantity demanded rises because the price falls
Show the answer

The number of buyers is a determinant of demand: more buyers shift demand right.

  • Demand decreases, because students have low incomes: Even students with low incomes add riders the market did not have.
  • Correct: Demand increases, because there are more buyers: Right: more buyers, more rides wanted at every price.
  • Supply increases, because more scooters are needed: The scenario is about riders, the buyers; it says nothing about scooter makers.
  • Quantity demanded rises because the price falls: No price change is described for this event.

6. Event (3): riders learn that ride prices will drop sharply next month. What happens to demand for rides this month?

  1. It decreases, because riders wait for the lower price
  2. It increases, because riders expect a bargain
  3. It is unchanged until the price of a ride actually falls
  4. The quantity demanded rises along the curve
Show the answer

Expectations are a determinant of demand. Expecting a lower price soon, buyers put off rides now: today's demand shifts left.

  • Correct: It decreases, because riders wait for the lower price: Right: buyers delay purchases until the price falls.
  • It increases, because riders expect a bargain: The bargain is next month, so buyers wait for it.
  • It is unchanged until the price of a ride actually falls: Expectations shift demand before the price changes.
  • The quantity demanded rises along the curve: The price has not changed yet, so there is no movement along the curve.

7. Event (4): the company raises its own ride price from $3 to $4. How does the graph of the scooter market show this?

  1. A shift of the demand curve to the left
  2. A shift of the demand curve to the right
  3. A movement down along the demand curve to a larger quantity demanded
  4. A movement up along the demand curve to a smaller quantity demanded
Show the answer

The good's own price is on the vertical axis, so a higher price moves riders up along the same demand curve.

  • A shift of the demand curve to the left: The ride price is on the axis, so it does not shift demand.
  • A shift of the demand curve to the right: A higher price does not raise demand; it lowers the quantity demanded.
  • A movement down along the demand curve to a larger quantity demanded: Down along the curve would be a lower price.
  • Correct: A movement up along the demand curve to a smaller quantity demanded: Right: a movement along the curve.

8. A country discovers a large new field of natural gas. Which factor of production has it gained more of?

  1. Labor
  2. Land
  3. Capital
  4. Entrepreneurship
Show the answer

Natural resources count as land in economics.

  • Labor: Labor is human effort; no workers were added.
  • Correct: Land: Right: natural resources are land.
  • Capital: Capital is made goods; the gas is natural.
  • Entrepreneurship: Entrepreneurship is organizing and risk-taking; nothing here adds it.

9. Why must mutually beneficial terms of trade lie between the two countries' opportunity costs?

  1. The richer country sets the price
  2. Prices in trade are set at the exact average of the two costs
  3. Countries trade goods they are unable to make
  4. Each country must get a good for less than it costs to make at home
Show the answer

A country buys a good from abroad only if it pays less than its own opportunity cost, and sells only if it receives more than its own; both conditions hold only between the two costs.

  • The richer country sets the price: Wealth does not set the terms; the opportunity costs do.
  • Prices in trade are set at the exact average of the two costs: Any price between the costs works; the average is one choice.
  • Countries trade goods they are unable to make: Countries often trade goods they could make, as the examples show.
  • Correct: Each country must get a good for less than it costs to make at home: Right: both must beat their own opportunity cost.

10. In Marnovia one worker makes 24 shirts or 8 hats a day. Marnovia trades for hats at 1 hat for 2.5 shirts. How many shirts does Marnovia save on each hat it buys instead of making it?

Type a number. A four-function calculator is allowed, as on the exam.

Show the answer

Making a hat costs Marnovia 248=3\frac{24}{8} = 3 shirts; buying one costs 2.5 shirts, so Marnovia saves 3−2.5=0.53 - 2.5 = 0.5 shirt per hat.

  • Answer: 0.5

11. Which is a service rather than a good?

  1. A pair of scissors
  2. A bottle of shampoo
  3. A haircut
  4. A barber's chair
Show the answer

A service is an action done for someone; a good is a physical item.

  • A pair of scissors: Scissors are a good (and capital for a barber).
  • A bottle of shampoo: Shampoo is a good.
  • Correct: A haircut: Right: the haircut is work done for you.
  • A barber's chair: The chair is a good (and capital for a barber).

12. Jordan's Saturday options, in order of preference, are a hike with friends, a paid work shift worth $80, and studying. If Jordan goes on the hike, what is the opportunity cost?

  1. The work shift and the studying, added together
  2. Nothing, because the hike is free
  3. The studying, because it ranks last
  4. The $80 work shift, the next-best choice
Show the answer

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative given up, here the work shift. Jordan could not have done both other options with the same time.

  • The work shift and the studying, added together: Jordan could have done only one of the other two, so only the best of them counts.
  • Nothing, because the hike is free: Even a free activity costs the time that could have earned $80.
  • The studying, because it ranks last: The lowest-ranked option is not what Jordan would have done instead.
  • Correct: The $80 work shift, the next-best choice: Right: only the next-best option counts.
