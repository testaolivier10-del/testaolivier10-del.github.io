Unit 6 · Topic 6.8

Urban Sustainability

Urban sustainability policies try to meet a city's needs without wasting land, energy or money.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 2: Spatial Relationships

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1979, the Portland, Oregon, region drew a line on the map around its built-up area and said: houses and offices go inside, farms and forests stay outside. Decades later, farms and forests still reach close to the city, and many people ride light rail. Home prices also rose fast in the 2010s. Was the line a success? It depends on whom you ask, and that is what this topic is about.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A region limits building on farmland with a growth boundary or greenbelt→new homes and offices go into land already inside the city, so density rises
  2. Homes, shops and offices are built close together near transit stations→more trips can be made on foot, by bike or by train, so car use and emissions fall
  3. Land for building becomes scarcer inside the boundary→land and home prices can rise, so lower-income families may be priced out
  4. New, denser buildings go up in older neighborhoods→some residents welcome shops and housing choices, while others feel the area loses its historic character

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Smart growth is a set of planning policies that steer growth into compact, walkable areas instead of sprawl. Tools include mixed-use development (homes, shops and offices together) and transit-oriented development (dense building around transit stations).
  • Walkability is how easy and safe it is to reach daily needs on foot. New Urbanism designs neighborhoods with walkable streets, porches, nearby shops and a mix of homes.
  • A greenbelt is a ring of protected open land around a city; an urban growth boundary is a line beyond which building is limited. Slow-growth cities cap how many new homes may be built each year.
  • Praise: less sprawl, less driving, more housing choice, livelier streets. Criticism: higher housing costs, possible de facto segregation, loss of historic character.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Smart growth policies stop a city from growing.

What actually happens: Smart growth redirects growth rather than stopping it: up and inward, onto infill sites and near transit, instead of outward onto farmland. Slow-growth caps, such as Boulder, Colorado's yearly limit on new homes, are a stricter tool, and they tend to push prices up.

1. Which feature of the left panel shows mixed-use development?

  1. Shops below apartments
  2. The curving dead-end streets lined with houses
  3. The wide road joining the mall and the office park
  4. The strip mall set far away from the homes
Show the answer

Mixed use puts homes, shops and offices in the same building or block. Shops below apartments are a classic example.

  • Correct: Shops below apartments: Right: different uses stacked in one building.
  • The curving dead-end streets lined with houses: Dead-end streets belong to the right panel and separate uses.
  • The wide road joining the mall and the office park: A wide road links separated uses; it is not mixed use.
  • The strip mall set far away from the homes: A strip mall far from homes separates uses.

2. Which term best describes the design in the left panel?

  1. Urban sprawl
  2. An edge city
  3. A squatter settlement
  4. Transit-oriented development
Show the answer

Dense, mixed-use building within a short walk of a rail station is transit-oriented development.

  • Urban sprawl: Sprawl is the low-density, car-based pattern in the right panel.
  • An edge city: An edge city is a suburban office center at a highway junction.
  • A squatter settlement: The buildings are planned, not self-built on land residents do not own.
  • Correct: Transit-oriented development: Right: density and mixed use around a station.

3. Compared with the right panel, how would daily travel in the left panel most likely differ?

  1. More car trips, because the station is crowded
  2. Longer trips, because buildings are taller
  3. More trips on foot and by train
  4. About the same, since both have roads
Show the answer

In the left panel, homes, shops, jobs and the station are within a short walk, so many trips need no car. In the right panel, every destination is far, so people drive.

  • More car trips, because the station is crowded: A busy station means people ride trains instead of driving.
  • Longer trips, because buildings are taller: Height puts more destinations close by, so trips get shorter.
  • Correct: More trips on foot and by train: Right: nearby destinations and transit cut car trips.
  • About the same, since both have roads: The right panel's layout makes the car the only practical option.

6. Seaside, Florida, begun in the early 1980s, has a grid of narrow streets, houses close to the sidewalk with porches, and a town center within walking distance. Which planning movement does it represent?

  1. Urban sprawl
  2. Galactic city
  3. Zone in transition
  4. New Urbanism
Show the answer

Designing neighborhoods like older walkable towns, with porches, a street grid and a nearby center, is New Urbanism.

  • Urban sprawl: Sprawl spreads homes far from shops on wide roads.
  • Galactic city: The galactic city model describes a whole metro area with edge cities.
  • Zone in transition: The zone in transition belongs to the concentric zone model.
  • Correct: New Urbanism: Right: walkable streets and a town center are New Urbanism.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Urban sustainability policies try to meet a city's needs without wasting land, energy or money. Smart growth tools (mixed use, walkability, transit-oriented development, New Urbanism, greenbelts and growth boundaries, slow-growth caps) can cut sprawl and driving, but critics point to higher housing costs, possible segregation and loss of historic character.
