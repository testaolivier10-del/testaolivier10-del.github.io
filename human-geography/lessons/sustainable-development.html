Unit 7 · Topic 7.8

Sustainable Development

Sustainable development improves lives now without harming future generations.

Practice 2: Spatial RelationshipsPractice 5: Scale Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

For centuries the cod off Newfoundland, Canada, seemed endless. Then factory trawlers caught more each year than the fish could replace. In 1992 Canada closed the northern cod fishery, and about 30,000 people lost their jobs. Growth that uses up what it depends on cannot last. How can places grow richer, healthier and better schooled without wrecking the environment for the next generation?

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Incomes rise and people buy more (mass consumption)→demand for energy, materials and food grows
  2. Resources are used faster than they renew, as the Newfoundland cod were→they run out, and the jobs that depend on them go too
  3. Fossil fuels are burned for growth→pollution and climate change harm places, often poorer ones most
  4. Ecotourism and the SDGs link income to protecting nature→development can improve lives while protecting the future, if it is well managed

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Sustainable development improves lives now without harming future generations' ability to meet their needs: economy, society and environment together.
  • Threats: resource depletion, mass consumption, pollution and climate change.
  • The 17 Sustainable Development Goals (UN, 2015, for 2030) include no poverty, gender equality, clean energy and climate action.
  • Ecotourism (Costa Rica, the Galápagos) can make protecting nature pay, but crowding, flights and outside ownership limit it.
  • Responses work at every scale: global goals, national energy policy, local reserves.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Sustainable development means stopping development to protect nature.

What actually happens: It means improving people's lives (income, health, schooling) in ways that protect resources and the environment for the future: all three parts together.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

Text

A reserve office writes about visitors (invented passage for practice)

From the 2026 visitor report of the Southern Forest Reserve, an invented protected forest in Country A.

Before the reserve opened in 2012, loggers cleared part of the forest each year. Since then, villagers who once cut trees work as guides, cooks and lodge staff, and every visitor pays a park fee that funds rangers. We allow 400 visitors a day and keep them on marked trails. Last year, however, three lodges owned by firms from the capital took most of the bookings, and some villagers say little of the money reaches them. Most visitors fly in from abroad.

1. What kind of tourism does the passage describe?

  1. Mass tourism at a beach resort
  2. Ecotourism in a protected natural area
  3. Tourism to a special economic zone
  4. Heritage tourism to an old city
Show the answer

Tourism to a protected forest, with visitor limits, guides and fees that fund protection, is ecotourism.

  • Mass tourism at a beach resort: The passage limits visitors to 400 a day in a forest, the opposite of mass tourism.
  • Correct: Ecotourism in a protected natural area: Right: tourism to a natural area that aims to protect it and employ local people.
  • Tourism to a special economic zone: A special economic zone draws factories, not forest visitors.
  • Heritage tourism to an old city: The passage is about a forest reserve, not a historic city.

2. According to the passage, how does the reserve help keep the forest standing?

  1. It pays villagers to cut trees more slowly
  2. It bans visitors from the forest
  3. Jobs and fees make the forest pay
  4. It moves the villagers to the capital
Show the answer

Ecotourism turns a standing forest into income: jobs for villagers and park fees for rangers make protection pay more than logging.

  • It pays villagers to cut trees more slowly: The passage says villagers now work in tourism, not logging.
  • It bans visitors from the forest: It allows 400 visitors a day.
  • Correct: Jobs and fees make the forest pay: Right: guiding, lodge jobs and park fees give people a reason to protect the trees.
  • It moves the villagers to the capital: Nothing says villagers moved; they work in the reserve.

3. Which detail in the passage shows a limit of ecotourism as sustainable development?

  1. Visitors keep to marked trails
  2. Park fees fund rangers
  3. Outside lodges take most of the money
  4. Villagers work as guides and cooks
Show the answer

Sustainable development needs economic, social and environmental gains; outside firms keeping the income weakens the social side.

  • Visitors keep to marked trails: Trails protect the forest: that is a strength.
  • Park fees fund rangers: Fees that pay for protection are a strength.
  • Correct: Outside lodges take most of the money: Right: if outside firms keep the income, local people gain little, which weakens the social side.
  • Villagers work as guides and cooks: Local jobs are a strength.

4. The passage says most visitors fly in from abroad. Why does this matter for judging the reserve's sustainability?

  1. Flights add to climate change
  2. Foreign visitors pay lower park fees than locals
  3. Flights bring loggers into the forest
  4. Foreign visitors are barred from the trails
Show the answer

Ecotourism can protect a place at the local scale while flights add greenhouse gases at the global scale.

  • Correct: Flights add to climate change: Right: a local gain for the forest comes with a global cost from flights.
  • Foreign visitors pay lower park fees than locals: The passage gives no fee difference.
  • Flights bring loggers into the forest: Nothing links flights to logging.
  • Foreign visitors are barred from the trails: Visitors walk the marked trails.

5. Which choice best describes sustainable development?

  1. Growing the economy as fast as possible
  2. Better lives now, without harm to the future
  3. Stopping development to protect nature
  4. Moving factories from core countries to the periphery
Show the answer

Sustainable development improves people's lives now while protecting the environment and resources for the future.

  • Growing the economy as fast as possible: Speed by itself ignores the environment and future generations.
  • Correct: Better lives now, without harm to the future: Right: development that meets needs now without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
  • Stopping development to protect nature: Sustainable development still improves lives; it does not stop development.
  • Moving factories from core countries to the periphery: That is outsourcing, not sustainable development.

6. In 2015, all UN member states adopted 17 goals, such as no poverty, gender equality and climate action, to be reached by 2030. What are these called?

  1. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
  2. The Human Development Index
  3. Structural adjustment programs
  4. The Gender Inequality Index
Show the answer

The 17 Sustainable Development Goals were adopted by UN member states in 2015, with 2030 as the target year.

  • Correct: The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs): Right: the SDGs, adopted in 2015 for 2030.
  • The Human Development Index: The HDI is a measure, not a set of goals.
  • Structural adjustment programs: Those are IMF loan conditions, not UN goals.
  • The Gender Inequality Index: The GII measures gaps between women and men; it is not a set of goals.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Sustainable development improves lives now without harming future generations. Resource depletion, mass consumption, pollution and climate change make it hard. The UN Sustainable Development Goals (2015, for 2030) set shared targets, and ecotourism can make protecting nature pay, though it has limits. Responses work at global, national and local scales.
