Unit 6 · Topic 6.6

Density and Land Use

Housing and land use reflect culture, technology and the cycles of building and decline that every city goes through.

Practice 2: Spatial RelationshipsPractice 4: Source Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In Hong Kong, millions of people live in apartment towers 30 or more stories high. In Houston, Texas, most homes are houses with their own yards. In Detroit, whole blocks that were packed with homes in 1950 are grassy fields in the 2020s. Why do cities house people so differently, and why does the same land fill up, empty out and fill up again?

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Land near the center costs the most→builders put more homes on each lot, so residential density is highest near downtown
  2. New technology (elevators, streetcars, then cars) changes how people move and build→cities grow up in some eras and spread out in others
  3. Local governments pass zoning rules about what can be built where→some areas allow apartments and stores, others allow only single houses, which locks in a density pattern
  4. An area ages, loses jobs or residents, and leaves empty lots→later investors and cities may fill the gaps with new buildings: infilling

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Residential density is the number of homes (or people) on a given area of land. High-density housing means apartments; low-density housing means detached houses on large lots.
  • Housing reflects culture (courtyard homes for family privacy in old Fez, Morocco; single-family houses with yards in many US suburbs) and technology (elevators and steel frames made towers possible; cars made low-density suburbs possible).
  • Zoning is a set of local laws that says what kind of building and land use is allowed on each piece of land. It shapes density for decades.
  • Land goes through cycles of development: building, aging, decline and rebuilding. Infilling is building on vacant or underused lots inside an area that is already built up.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A city with a large population must have a high residential density.

What actually happens: Density compares homes or people with land area. A large city spread over a huge area, such as metro Atlanta, can have a low residential density, while a smaller city of apartment blocks can have a high one. Always ask "per how much land?"

Data

Residential density by distance from the center in two cities

Invented data for practice.

1. Which statement best describes the data in the chart?

  1. Both fall with distance; City 1 stays denser
  2. Density rises with distance from the center in both of the cities
  3. City 2 is denser than City 1 near the center but not farther out
  4. The two cities reach the same density by about 10 km from the center
Show the answer

Both lines slope down, and City 1's line is above City 2's at every distance (120 vs 60 at the center, 40 vs 5 at 10 km).

  • Correct: Both fall with distance; City 1 stays denser: Right: both fall, and City 1 stays higher.
  • Density rises with distance from the center in both of the cities: Both lines slope downward.
  • City 2 is denser than City 1 near the center but not farther out: At the center, City 1 has 120 homes per hectare and City 2 has 60.
  • The two cities reach the same density by about 10 km from the center: At 10 km, City 1 has 40 and City 2 has 5.

2. Between the center and 4 km out, by how much does City 2's density fall compared with City 1's?

  1. City 2 falls by 15 homes per hectare, City 1 by 85
  2. Both fall by the same amount, about 40 homes per hectare
  3. City 2 rises over this stretch while City 1 falls
  4. City 2 falls by 45, City 1 by 35
Show the answer

City 2: 60 to 15, a fall of 45. City 1: 120 to 85, a fall of 35. City 2 drops faster near the center.

  • City 2 falls by 15 homes per hectare, City 1 by 85: Those are the values at 4 km, not the falls.
  • Both fall by the same amount, about 40 homes per hectare: The falls are 45 and 35, not equal.
  • City 2 rises over this stretch while City 1 falls: Both lines fall over this stretch.
  • Correct: City 2 falls by 45, City 1 by 35: Right: 60 - 15 = 45 and 120 - 85 = 35.

3. City 2 grew mostly after 1950, when most families owned cars. City 1 grew mostly before 1900. Which best explains the difference in the chart?

  1. City 1 had more cars than City 2, so it could afford to build higher
  2. Streetcars made City 2 denser at its edge than near its center
  3. Cars let City 2 spread out on cheap land
  4. Elevators lowered density in City 1's center by spacing out its towers
Show the answer

Before cars, people had to live within walking or streetcar distance of work, so City 1 packed homes close together. Cars let City 2 spread out at low density.

  • City 1 had more cars than City 2, so it could afford to build higher: City 1 grew before most people owned cars.
  • Streetcars made City 2 denser at its edge than near its center: Streetcars raise density along their lines, and City 2 grew after the car.
  • Correct: Cars let City 2 spread out on cheap land: Right: car-era growth spreads homes out at low density.
  • Elevators lowered density in City 1's center by spacing out its towers: Elevators raise density by allowing taller buildings.

Text

A city planning notice

Invented notice for practice, 2026. "The city will hold a public hearing on a plan to build a five-story building with 60 apartments and a corner grocery on the empty lot at 4th Street and Oak Avenue, a parking lot since a factory closed there in 1998. The lot is zoned for factories and must be rezoned for homes and shops. The block is lined with two-story houses. Supporters say the plan will add homes near the bus line. Some neighbors say a five-story building is too tall for the block and will bring more traffic."

4. Why must the lot be rezoned before the building can go up?

  1. It is zoned for factories
  2. Its zoning sets a minimum height of five stories for any building
  3. Its zoning keeps the lot as a parking lot for the old factory
  4. The grocery store on the corner needs a farm permit first
Show the answer

Zoning says what can be built on each lot. This lot is zoned for factories, so homes and a grocery are not allowed until the zone changes.

  • Correct: It is zoned for factories: Right: the planned uses do not match the lot's zone.
  • Its zoning sets a minimum height of five stories for any building: The notice says nothing about a height minimum.
  • Its zoning keeps the lot as a parking lot for the old factory: The lot is zoned for factories, not kept empty by law.
  • The grocery store on the corner needs a farm permit first: A grocery is a shop, not a farm.

5. What would the plan most likely do to the block's residential density?

  1. Lower it, by replacing houses with a store
  2. Leave it unchanged, because the lot was empty
  3. Lower it, by adding a parking lot
  4. Raise it, by adding 60 homes on one lot
Show the answer

Sixty apartments on a lot with no homes adds many housing units to the same area, so homes per hectare rise.

  • Lower it, by replacing houses with a store: No houses are torn down; the lot is a parking lot.
  • Leave it unchanged, because the lot was empty: Adding homes to an empty lot changes the density of the block.
  • Lower it, by adding a parking lot: The plan replaces a parking lot with homes.
  • Correct: Raise it, by adding 60 homes on one lot: Right: more homes on the same land means higher density.

6. Houses in the old medina of Fez, Morocco, face inward onto private courtyards and have few windows on the street. Which influence on housing does this best show?

  1. Culture, in a value placed on family privacy
  2. Technology, in the use of steel and elevators
  3. Zoning, in a rule that sets building heights
  4. Cycles of development, in the decline of an old area
Show the answer

The inward-facing courtyard form expresses a cultural value: privacy for the family from the street.

  • Correct: Culture, in a value placed on family privacy: Right: the form of the home reflects cultural values.
  • Technology, in the use of steel and elevators: Courtyard homes are old, low buildings, not steel towers.
  • Zoning, in a rule that sets building heights: The design comes from culture, not from a modern height rule.
  • Cycles of development, in the decline of an old area: The question is about the form of homes, not decline.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Housing and land use reflect culture, technology and the cycles of building and decline that every city goes through. Residential density is usually highest near the center and falls toward the edge. Zoning rules lock in density patterns, and infilling adds new buildings on empty lots inside the built-up area.
