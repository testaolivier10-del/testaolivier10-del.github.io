Unit 6 · Topic 6.1

The Origin and Influences of Urbanization

Cities first grew where farming produced a food surplus, mostly in river valleys.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 2: Spatial Relationships

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1950, about one person in five lived in a city. By 2025, UN DESA counted 45 percent of the world's 8.2 billion people in cities (World Urbanization Prospects 2025). Cities did not appear at random. The first ones grew where rivers watered rich farmland, and the biggest ones grew where routes met. This lesson asks two questions: why does a city start where it does, and why do people keep moving to cities?

Part 4 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Farmers in river valleys grow more food than they need→some people can stop farming and become builders, traders, priests and rulers
  2. Those people gather in one place to trade and govern→the first cities form, often on a site that is easy to defend and near water
  3. Routes by river, sea, road and later rail meet at a city→its situation draws trade and jobs, so it keeps growing
  4. Jobs and services gather in cities while farm work needs fewer hands→people move from rural areas to cities, and the urban share of the population rises: urbanization

Part 5 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • A city is a large, dense settlement where most people work in jobs other than farming. Urbanization is the growth in the share of people who live in cities.
  • The first cities grew in river valleys such as the Tigris and Euphrates in Mesopotamia (Iraq), the Nile in Egypt, the Indus in Pakistan and the Huang He in China, and separately in Mesoamerica. A food surplus came first.
  • Site is the place itself: the land, water and shape of the ground. Situation is where it sits among other places. A site rarely changes unless people reshape the land; a situation changes when routes change.
  • Urbanization is driven by transport, communication, population growth, migration, economic development and government policy, often several at once.

Part 6 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Site and situation both mean "where the city is", so either word fits any location detail.

What actually happens: Site is about the place itself (Paris began on an island in the Seine). Situation is about its links to other places (Singapore sits on a main sea route at the end of the Strait of Malacca). Ask: does the detail need another place to make sense? Then it is situation.

Part 7 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. Which of these is an example of urbanization?

  1. A family moves from Nairobi, the capital, back to a farm village
  2. The share of Kenyans in cities rises
  3. A village in western Kenya keeps the same population for 50 years
  4. Farmers in Kenya plant more maize on the same amount of land
Show the answer

Urbanization is a rise in the share of a population that lives in cities.

  • A family moves from Nairobi, the capital, back to a farm village: That is a move from city to countryside, the reverse.
  • Correct: The share of Kenyans in cities rises: Right: a rising urban share is urbanization.
  • A village in western Kenya keeps the same population for 50 years: No change in where people live is not urbanization.
  • Farmers in Kenya plant more maize on the same amount of land: Higher farm output may help cities grow, but it is not urbanization itself.

2. Mexico City was built on the bed of a drained lake. Parts of the city sink each year as water is pumped from under it. Which idea explains this problem?

  1. The city's site: soft lake soil settles
  2. The city's situation: it lies far inland from the coast and its sea ports
  3. Rural-to-urban migration: newcomers build homes on the edge of the city
  4. Distance decay: trade and contact fall with distance from the city center
Show the answer

The problem comes from the ground itself. A soft lake-bed site compresses when groundwater is pumped out, so the land sinks.

  • Correct: The city's site: soft lake soil settles: Right: the physical character of the place causes the sinking.
  • The city's situation: it lies far inland from the coast and its sea ports: Distance from the coast is situation, and it does not make land sink.
  • Rural-to-urban migration: newcomers build homes on the edge of the city: Migration adds people but does not explain why the ground sinks.
  • Distance decay: trade and contact fall with distance from the city center: Distance decay is about interaction, not the ground.

3. Which best explains why Singapore grew into a great port city?

  1. Its site of wide, flat farmland that could feed a large population
  2. Its rich oil fields, which drew shipping and trading companies from abroad
  3. Its cool, dry climate, which suited the long storage of trade goods
  4. Its situation on a main sea route
Show the answer

Singapore is a small island with little farmland. Its strength is where it sits: ships between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific pass by it.

  • Its site of wide, flat farmland that could feed a large population: Singapore has very little farmland; its food mostly comes from elsewhere.
  • Its rich oil fields, which drew shipping and trading companies from abroad: Singapore has no large oil fields of its own.
  • Its cool, dry climate, which suited the long storage of trade goods: Singapore is near the equator, warm all year.
  • Correct: Its situation on a main sea route: Right: a location on a main sea route is a strong situation.

Text

A history of an invented river town

Invented passage for practice. "Our town began about 900 years ago on a rocky island where the river narrows, so it was easy to defend and to cross. For centuries it grew because the coast road met the river route inland at our bridge, and every merchant paid a toll. In the 1880s the harbor filled with silt, and the new railroad was laid 30 kilometers to the north, through the town of Hartfield. Trade moved there. Our island is as safe and as pretty as ever, but our population has not grown since."

From a local history society booklet (invented), 1952.

4. Which detail from the passage describes the town's site?

  1. The coast road met the river route inland at the town bridge
  2. It stood on a rocky island
  3. The new railroad was laid 30 kilometers to the north, through Hartfield
  4. Merchants passing through on the roads paid a toll at the bridge
Show the answer

Site is the physical character of the place itself: a rocky island at a narrow point in a river.

  • The coast road met the river route inland at the town bridge: A meeting of routes links the town to other places: that is situation.
  • Correct: It stood on a rocky island: Right: an island and a narrow river are features of the place itself.
  • The new railroad was laid 30 kilometers to the north, through Hartfield: Where the railroad ran is about links to other places: situation.
  • Merchants passing through on the roads paid a toll at the bridge: Tolls came from traffic between other places, a result of situation.

5. Which detail from the passage describes the town's situation before the 1880s?

  1. Two trade routes met at its bridge
  2. It stood on a rocky island in the river that was easy to defend
  3. Its island setting was described as safe and pretty to look at
  4. The river narrowed right beside the island, making it easy to cross
Show the answer

Situation is a place's location relative to other places. A crossing of two trade routes links the town to the coast and the inland region.

  • Correct: Two trade routes met at its bridge: Right: the meeting of two routes is about links to other places.
  • It stood on a rocky island in the river that was easy to defend: The island and its defenses are features of the place: site.
  • Its island setting was described as safe and pretty to look at: Safety and beauty describe the place itself, not its links.
  • The river narrowed right beside the island, making it easy to cross: The narrow river is a physical feature of the place: site.

6. According to the passage, why did the town stop growing after the 1880s?

  1. Its site worsened when the rocky island slowly eroded away, while its situation improved
  2. Its population aged, so births fell below deaths year after year
  3. Its regional government moved the capital and its offices to Hartfield
  4. Its situation worsened while its site stayed the same
Show the answer

The silted harbor and the railroad through Hartfield cut the town out of the main routes. Its island was "as safe as ever", so the site did not change; its situation did.

  • Its site worsened when the rocky island slowly eroded away, while its situation improved: The passage says the island is as safe as ever; nothing eroded.
  • Its population aged, so births fell below deaths year after year: The passage gives no data on births, deaths or ages.
  • Its regional government moved the capital and its offices to Hartfield: The passage says trade moved, not the government.
  • Correct: Its situation worsened while its site stayed the same: Right: situation can change when routes change, and that ends growth.

Part 8 · Summary

Summary

Cities first grew where farming produced a food surplus, mostly in river valleys. Site (the physical place) explains where a city starts; situation (its links to other places) explains much of how it grows, and situation can change. Urbanization, a rising share of people in cities, is driven by transport, communication, population growth, migration, development and policy.
