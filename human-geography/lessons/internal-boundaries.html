Unit 4 · Topic 4.6

Internal Boundaries

Internal boundaries divide a state into smaller units, including voting districts.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 5: Scale Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1812, Massachusetts drew a voting district so twisted that a newspaper said it looked like a salamander. The governor who signed it was Elbridge Gerry, and the "Gerry-mander" got its name. The same voters can elect very different representatives depending on where the lines fall. Internal boundaries, the lines inside a state, decide who wins.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A census shows that people have moved→seats are shared out again (reapportionment) and district lines are redrawn (redistricting)
  2. Whoever draws the lines packs or cracks the other party's voters→that party's votes are wasted in a few districts or spread too thin to win
  3. Many votes are wasted→a party can win fewer seats than its share of the votes

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Internal boundaries divide a state into units such as provinces, counties and voting districts.
  • Redistricting redraws voting districts, in the United States after each census, so each holds about the same number of people.
  • Gerrymandering draws lines to favor one party or group. Packing crowds a party's voters into a few districts; cracking splits them across many.
  • Always say the effect on votes: packing wastes votes in lopsided wins, cracking leaves votes too spread out to win.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A party that gets most of the votes always wins most of the seats.

What actually happens: Seats depend on where the district lines fall. With packing and cracking, a party with about 60% of the votes can win only 40% of the seats.

3. In Plan 2, the two left columns are each a district made only of Party X blocks. Which gerrymandering method does this show?

  1. Cracking
  2. Reapportionment
  3. Packing
  4. Demarcation
Show the answer

In Plan 2, Party X wins its two packed districts by huge margins. Every X vote beyond what it needed to win there is wasted.

  • Cracking: Cracking splits a party's voters across many districts.
  • Reapportionment: Reapportionment shares out seats among states after a census.
  • Correct: Packing: Right: Packing puts as many of a party's voters as possible into a few districts.
  • Demarcation: Demarcation is marking a boundary on the ground.

4. In Plan 2, the five Party X blocks in the middle column are spread over three districts that Party Y wins. What effect does this have on Party X votes?

  1. Those X votes are packed together, so they win one more seat
  2. Those X votes are split up, so they lose in every district
  3. Those X votes count twice, because they sit in bent districts
  4. Those X votes decide which party wins the two left districts
Show the answer

Cracking spreads a party's voters across many districts so they are outvoted in each. Naming cracking is not enough on the exam: say what it does to the votes.

  • Those X votes are packed together, so they win one more seat: They are split among three districts, not packed.
  • Correct: Those X votes are split up, so they lose in every district: Right: This is cracking: the votes are spread so thin that they elect no one.
  • Those X votes count twice, because they sit in bent districts: Each voter votes once, in one district.
  • Those X votes decide which party wins the two left districts: Those districts are made of other X blocks.

5. What is gerrymandering?

  1. Drawing district lines to favor one party or group
  2. Counting votes in the order they arrive
  3. Giving each district about the same number of people
  4. Letting each region elect its own leader
Show the answer

Gerrymandering is drawing voting district boundaries to give one party or group an advantage. The name comes from a Massachusetts district of 1812, signed into law by Governor Elbridge Gerry, whose shape looked like a salamander.

  • Correct: Drawing district lines to favor one party or group: Right: Gerrymandering shapes districts to help one side win seats.
  • Counting votes in the order they arrive: That is not about district lines.
  • Giving each district about the same number of people: That is a basic rule of fair districting.
  • Letting each region elect its own leader: That describes regional government, not district lines.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Internal boundaries divide a state into smaller units, including voting districts. Redistricting redraws those districts, in the United States after each census, at the national, state and local scales. Gerrymandering draws them to favor one side: packing wastes a party's votes in a few lopsided districts, and cracking spreads them too thin to win. Independent commissions and rules for compact districts make it harder.
