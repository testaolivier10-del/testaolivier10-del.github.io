Unit 2 · Topic 2.6

Malthusian Theory

Thomas Malthus argued in 1798 that population grows faster than food, so checks such as famine, disease and war would follow.

Practice 1: Concepts and Processes

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1798, an English clergyman named Thomas Malthus warned that people would always have more children than the land could feed. World population was then about 1 billion. By 2024 it was about 8.2 billion (UN, 2024), and food output per person had risen, not fallen. Was Malthus wrong, or just early?

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically→population outruns the food supply
  2. There is not enough food for everyone→famine, disease and war (checks) raise deaths
  3. New seeds, fertilizer and machines raise crop yields→food grows faster than Malthus expected, which weakens his theory
  4. Families choose to have fewer children as countries change→population growth slows, another critique of Malthus

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Malthusian theory (Thomas Malthus, 1798): population grows faster than food, so famine, disease or war (checks) follow unless people have fewer children.
  • Neo-Malthusians, such as Paul Ehrlich (1968), apply the worry to water, energy and the environment.
  • Critiques: technology raised food output; fertility fell as countries changed; hunger comes mostly from poverty, conflict and how food is shared; trade moves food between places.
  • The idea links to carrying capacity: technology and trade can raise how many people an area can support.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Hunger in a country proves that its population has outgrown its food supply, as Malthus predicted.

What actually happens: Hunger often comes from poverty, prices, conflict and how food is shared. In the Bengal famine of 1943, the economist Amartya Sen found that food was available, but many people could not afford it.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

Text

From Thomas Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798)

"Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. [The food supply] increases only in an arithmetical ratio."

Plain words: left alone, a population multiplies (1, 2, 4, 8, 16), while the food supply only adds a fixed amount each time (1, 2, 3, 4, 5). Malthus argued that hunger, disease and war, which he called checks, would keep population in line with food.

1. According to the passage, how did Malthus think population and food supply grow?

  1. Population multiplies; food adds a fixed amount each time
  2. Food multiplies, while population adds a fixed amount each time
  3. Both grow at the same steady rate, so they stay balanced
  4. Population shrinks over time while food supply keeps growing
Show the answer

Malthus said population grows geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8) and food arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4), so population would outrun food.

  • Correct: Population multiplies; food adds a fixed amount each time: Right: a geometric ratio doubles; an arithmetic ratio adds the same step.
  • Food multiplies, while population adds a fixed amount each time: this reverses the two ratios in the passage.
  • Both grow at the same steady rate, so they stay balanced: Malthus argued the opposite: population pulls ahead of food.
  • Population shrinks over time while food supply keeps growing: Malthus said population grows unless checks stop it.

2. Which evidence most weakens the claim in the passage?

  1. Some countries had famines in the 1900s
  2. World population grew from about 1 billion in 1800 to 8.2 billion in 2024
  3. World food output grew faster than population from the 1960s to the 2010s
  4. Food prices rose in some years after bad harvests
Show the answer

Malthus assumed food could grow only by fixed steps. In fact, new seeds, fertilizer and machines raised food output faster than population for decades (FAO data), so the world did not hit his limit.

  • Some countries had famines in the 1900s: famines fit Malthus’s idea of checks; they do not weaken it.
  • World population grew from about 1 billion in 1800 to 8.2 billion in 2024: fast growth fits his first claim; it does not show food kept up.
  • Correct: World food output grew faster than population from the 1960s to the 2010s: Right: that shows food can grow faster than an arithmetic ratio.
  • Food prices rose in some years after bad harvests: a bad year fits his worry; long-run food growth is the stronger test.

4. How do the data for Country W relate to Malthusian theory?

  2. They support it: population outran food after 1960
  3. They support it: food grew in a steady arithmetic ratio

Malthus predicted population would outgrow food. Country W’s food output grew faster than its population, which is the main critique of his theory: technology raised output.

5. In 1943, millions died of famine in Bengal, then part of British India, although, by the economist Amartya Sen’s count, the food supply that year was not unusually low. Which critique of Malthus does this support?

  1. Population grows faster than food in each region of a country
  2. Technology raised food output faster than population there
  3. Hunger is often about who can get food, not the total
  4. Fertility fell, so fewer people needed food
Show the answer

The economist Amartya Sen (1981) showed that in the Bengal famine, food was there but many poor people could not afford it. Distribution and access, not just supply, decide who goes hungry.

  • Population grows faster than food in each region of a country: this repeats Malthus; it is not a critique.
  • Technology raised food output faster than population there: the case is about access to food, not rising output.
  • Correct: Hunger is often about who can get food, not the total: Right: prices, war and poor relief left people unable to buy food.
  • Fertility fell, so fewer people needed food: the famine was not caused by fertility.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Thomas Malthus argued in 1798 that population grows faster than food, so checks such as famine, disease and war would follow. Neo-Malthusians extend the worry to other resources. Three critiques stand out: technology raised food output, fertility fell as countries changed, and hunger depends on access to food more than total supply.
