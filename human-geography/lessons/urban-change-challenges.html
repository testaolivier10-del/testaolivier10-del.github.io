Unit 6 · Topic 6.10

Challenges of Urban Changes

US cities were shaped by redlining and blockbusting, which drained investment from some neighborhoods; by urban renewal, which cleared many of them; and by gentrification, which brings investment and displacement.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 2: Spatial Relationships

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In the 1930s, a federal agency colored some neighborhoods of US cities red on its maps and called them "hazardous" for lending. In the 1950s, bulldozers cleared whole districts for highways and new towers. In the 2010s, coffee shops and new condos arrived in some of the same places, and rents doubled. Each wave changed who could live there. This topic follows those waves and the policies cities use in response.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Lenders refuse loans or set worse terms in certain neighborhoods (redlining)→owners cannot buy, repair or sell, so buildings decline
  2. Buildings decline and some are abandoned→land becomes cheap, and the city clears it (urban renewal) or investors buy it
  3. Higher-income newcomers and investors arrive (gentrification)→buildings are repaired and new shops open, but rents and taxes rise
  4. Rents rise faster than long-time residents' incomes→many renters are displaced, so cities respond with inclusionary zoning, rent help and land trusts

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Redlining is refusing loans, insurance or other services, or offering worse terms, because of a neighborhood's location. In US history it targeted mostly Black and immigrant neighborhoods. Blockbusting was agents scaring white owners into selling cheaply by claiming Black families were moving in, then reselling at high prices.
  • Urban renewal is government clearing and rebuilding of areas it judges run-down. Gentrification is higher-income people moving into a lower-income area and raising its property values. Both bring investment and both can displace residents.
  • Other challenges: housing costs, unequal access to services, crime, disamenity zones (the poorest, least-served areas), squatter settlements without secure land rights, and environmental injustice (pollution falling hardest on poor and minority communities).
  • Responses include inclusionary zoning (new buildings must include affordable homes), fair-lending laws, land titles for squatters, local food projects and regional cooperation across fragmented local governments.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Redlining means any kind of racism in housing, or simply being poor.

What actually happens: Redlining is a specific practice: refusing services such as loans or insurance, or setting worse terms, based on where a property is. Its US history is tied to race, because the redlined areas were mostly Black and immigrant neighborhoods, but the mechanism is about location.

Text

A public hearing on a riverfront plan

Invented passage for practice, 2026. At a city council hearing on a city redevelopment plan to clear and replace a closed rail yard and 200 older rental homes with 1,500 new apartments, offices and a riverside park:

A developer: "This plan brings 2,000 jobs, a new park and 10 million dollars a year in taxes."

A renter of 22 years: "My building is on your map. Where will my family go? Nothing in this city is affordable to us."

A city planner: "We propose that 20% of the new apartments be priced for families earning less than the city's median income."

1. Which term best describes the plan in the passage?

  1. Redlining
  2. Blockbusting
  3. Suburbanization
  4. Urban renewal
Show the answer

Clearing an area of older buildings and rebuilding it under a city plan is urban renewal.

  • Redlining: Redlining is refusing loans or services by location.
  • Blockbusting: Blockbusting was agents stirring fear to buy homes cheaply.
  • Suburbanization: Suburbanization is movement out to the suburbs.
  • Correct: Urban renewal: Right: clearing and rebuilding an area.

2. Which speakers give a benefit and a cost of the plan?

  1. The renter names jobs; the developer names displacement
  2. The planner names a cost; the developer names no benefit
  3. The developer names jobs and taxes; the renter names displacement
  4. The three speakers name costs and no benefits
Show the answer

The developer lists jobs, a park and taxes (benefits); the renter fears losing a home (a cost). Strong answers give both sides.

  • The renter names jobs; the developer names displacement: This swaps the two speakers.
  • The planner names a cost; the developer names no benefit: The planner proposes a response; the developer names benefits.
  • Correct: The developer names jobs and taxes; the renter names displacement: Right: one benefit, one cost.
  • The three speakers name costs and no benefits: The developer names benefits.

3. Which policy does the city planner propose?

  1. Blockbusting
  2. Inclusionary zoning
  3. An urban growth boundary
  4. Redlining
Show the answer

Requiring a share of new homes priced for lower-income households is inclusionary zoning.

  • Blockbusting: Blockbusting was an illegal sales practice.
  • Correct: Inclusionary zoning: Right: affordable units inside new buildings.
  • An urban growth boundary: A growth boundary limits building at the edge of a metro area.
  • Redlining: Redlining refuses services by location.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

US cities were shaped by redlining and blockbusting, which drained investment from some neighborhoods; by urban renewal, which cleared many of them; and by gentrification, which brings investment and displacement. Around the world, squatter settlements face insecure land rights and poor services, and pollution often falls hardest on poorer communities. Cities respond with inclusionary zoning, fair-lending laws, land titles, local food projects and regional planning.
