Unit 2 · Topic 2.8

Women and Demographic Change

More schooling, paid work, health care and contraception for women lower fertility and child deaths in most places, as in Bangladesh and Kerala, India.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 2: Spatial Relationships

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In the early 1970s, a woman in Bangladesh had more than six children on average. By 2022 the average was 2.3 (Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey, 2022). Bangladesh did not become rich overnight. What changed were the lives of its women: more girls in school, millions of women in paid jobs, and clinics in every district.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. More girls stay in school longer→they marry and have their first child later
  2. Women gain paid jobs and know more about family planning→families choose to have fewer children, so the TFR falls
  3. Mothers know more about health and use clinics→fewer babies and young children die
  4. Women’s roles change→more women move for work, within their country and abroad

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Female education (schooling for girls and women), paid jobs, health care and access to contraception lower fertility in most places.
  • Women’s changing roles also lower mortality: children of mothers with more schooling are less likely to die young.
  • Ravenstein’s laws of migration (1885, 1889): most moves are short, many in steps; long-distance migrants go to big cities; each flow has a counterflow; within a country women moved more than men.
  • Ravenstein’s limits: 1800s British data, before air travel; many more women and long-distance migrants move abroad now.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Fertility falls only when a country becomes rich.

What actually happens: Fertility often falls with changes in women’s lives even before incomes rise much: Bangladesh’s TFR fell from more than 6 to 2.3 as girls’ schooling, women’s jobs and family planning spread.

Data

Women’s schooling and fertility in 13 countries

Invented data for practice.

1. Which statement describes the relationship on the scatter plot?

  1. Where women have more years of school, the TFR tends to be lower
  2. Where women have more years of school, the TFR tends to be higher
  3. There is no relationship between the two measures
  4. The TFR is the same in each of the 13 countries on the plot
Show the answer

The pattern is negative: more years of school for women goes with fewer children per woman. Readers want the direction stated, not just "a relationship".

  • Correct: Where women have more years of school, the TFR tends to be lower: Right: the points slope down from left to right.
  • Where women have more years of school, the TFR tends to be higher: that would be an upward slope; the points slope down.
  • There is no relationship between the two measures: most points lie close to a downward trend line.
  • The TFR is the same in each of the 13 countries on the plot: the TFR ranges from about 1.5 to 6.6.

2. One country has 11 years of school for women but a TFR of 4.3, far above the trend. Which is the most likely explanation?

  1. Women in that country have no access to schools
  2. The trend shows that schooling raises fertility
  3. Strong cultural or policy support for large families
  4. The country’s data are probably wrong and can be ignored in the trend
Show the answer

Schooling is one force among many. Religious teaching, family customs or pronatalist policies can keep fertility high. Explain an outlier rather than ignore it.

  • Women in that country have no access to schools: the point shows 11 years of school on average.
  • The trend shows that schooling raises fertility: the trend slopes down; this point is the exception.
  • Correct: Strong cultural or policy support for large families: Right: other factors can keep fertility high despite schooling.
  • The country’s data are probably wrong and can be ignored in the trend: outliers often have real causes worth explaining.

Text

Ravenstein’s laws of migration, summarized

LevlPrep summary of E. G. Ravenstein, "The Laws of Migration" (1885 and 1889), based on British census data:

  • Most migrants move only a short distance.
  • Many move in steps, from village to town to city.
  • People who move long distances usually head for large cities.
  • Each flow of migrants produces a smaller flow back.
  • People from the countryside move more than people from towns.
  • Within their own country, women move more than men; men more often move long distances and abroad.
  • Most migrants are adults, and most move for economic reasons.

3. A young woman moves from her village to a nearby town, and five years later to the capital. Which of Ravenstein’s laws does her path fit?

  1. Many migrants move in steps toward larger places
  2. Each flow of migrants produces a smaller flow back
  3. People from towns move more than people from the countryside
  4. Men move more than women within their own country
Show the answer

Ravenstein found many migrants move in stages: village to town to city.

  • Correct: Many migrants move in steps toward larger places: Right: village, then town, then city is a step-by-step path.
  • Each flow of migrants produces a smaller flow back: she moves forward each time; nothing here flows back.
  • People from towns move more than people from the countryside: the summary says the reverse: rural people move more.
  • Men move more than women within their own country: the summary says women move more within their country.

4. Each year many young people move from rural areas of a country to its capital, and a smaller number of retired people move from the capital back to the countryside. Which law does the second group fit?

  1. Most migrants move a short distance from home
  2. Each flow of migrants produces a smaller flow back
  3. People who move long distances head for large cities
  4. Most migrants are adults who move for economic reasons
Show the answer

Ravenstein said every migration stream has a counterflow: a smaller movement the other way.

  • Most migrants move a short distance from home: the question is about the direction of the second flow, not its distance.
  • Correct: Each flow of migrants produces a smaller flow back: Right: the retirees are a smaller flow in the opposite direction.
  • People who move long distances head for large cities: the retirees are leaving the capital.
  • Most migrants are adults who move for economic reasons: retirees moving to the countryside are not moving for work.

5. Children of mothers who finished secondary school are less likely to die before age 5, in most countries. Which link best explains this?

  1. Schooled mothers have more children, so each child gets more care
  2. Schooled mothers learn more about health and use clinics more
  3. Mothers who stay in school live farther from hospitals
  4. Mothers with more schooling move abroad, so their children are counted elsewhere
Show the answer

Women’s changing roles affect mortality too. Mothers with more schooling tend to know more about clean water, nutrition and vaccines and to use health care, so infant and child deaths fall.

  • Schooled mothers have more children, so each child gets more care: schooled mothers usually have fewer children, not more.
  • Correct: Schooled mothers learn more about health and use clinics more: Right: knowledge of hygiene, nutrition and vaccines lowers child deaths.
  • Mothers who stay in school live farther from hospitals: distance is not the link here.
  • Mothers with more schooling move abroad, so their children are counted elsewhere: the pattern holds where families stay put.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

More schooling, paid work, health care and contraception for women lower fertility and child deaths in most places, as in Bangladesh and Kerala, India. Changing roles also change who migrates. Ravenstein’s laws, drawn from 1800s British data, say most moves are short and step by step, every flow has a counterflow, and women moved more than men within their own country.
