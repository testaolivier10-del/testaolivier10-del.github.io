Unit 5 · Topic 5.4

The Second Agricultural Revolution

The Second Agricultural Revolution, from the 1700s in Britain and the 1800s in North America, brought crop rotation, selective breeding, enclosure and mechanization.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 2: Spatial Relationships

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1831, a crew in Virginia cut wheat with hand tools while a horse-drawn machine beside them did the work of many workers. Within a few decades, farms across Britain and North America were growing more food with fewer people, and the people who were no longer needed on farms moved to towns.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. New rotations, breeding and machines spread on British farms in the 1700s and 1800s→each farm worker produced much more food
  2. Each worker produced more and farms were enclosed→farms needed fewer workers
  3. Fewer farm jobs pushed people off the land while factories offered work→people moved to towns and cities
  4. More and better food reached more people→death rates fell and the population grew

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The Second Agricultural Revolution began in Britain and western Europe in the 1700s and spread to North America in the 1800s, alongside the rise of factories.
  • Its changes: crop rotation (the Norfolk four-field system), selective breeding, enclosure and mechanization (seed drill, steel plow, reaper).
  • Effects: more food per worker, better diets and lower death rates, and fewer farm jobs, which pushed people to towns and factory work.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The Second Agricultural Revolution means farmers began growing crops for the first time.

What actually happens: That was the First Agricultural Revolution. The second changed how existing farms worked: rotation, breeding, enclosure and machines, from the 1700s.

3. Which explanation best links the farming columns to the rise in the share of people living in towns?

  1. Farms needed more workers, so towns shrank as people moved out
  2. Town growth caused each farm worker to grow less food
  3. Fewer workers were needed on farms, so many moved to town jobs
  4. Food ran short, so people left farms to find food in towns
Show the answer

This is the Second Agricultural Revolution's key effect: more food from fewer farmers freed labor for town and factory work.

  • Farms needed more workers, so towns shrank as people moved out: The table shows towns growing, not shrinking.
  • Town growth caused each farm worker to grow less food: Food per farm worker rose, not fell.
  • Correct: Fewer workers were needed on farms, so many moved to town jobs: Right: as each worker grew more food, farms needed fewer hands, and people moved to towns to work, often in factories.
  • Food ran short, so people left farms to find food in towns: Food per farm worker rose sharply; the table shows no shortage.

Text

A new machine for the harvest, 1831

LevlPrep summary, 2026, based on US farm histories. In 1831, Cyrus McCormick showed a horse-drawn reaper in Virginia. Before it, workers cut wheat by hand with a scythe or cradle, and a crew could harvest only a few acres a day. Pulled by horses, the reaper cut grain as it moved, so a few people could harvest many times more land in a day. By the 1850s, reapers were being sold across the wheat lands of the US Midwest.

4. The reaper in the passage is an example of what?

  1. Domestication
  2. Mechanization
  3. Shifting cultivation
  4. The Columbian Exchange
Show the answer

Mechanization means replacing human and animal labor with machines, a core part of the Second Agricultural Revolution.

  • Domestication: Domestication changes wild plants and animals into crops and livestock; the reaper is a machine.
  • Correct: Mechanization: Right: a machine took over work that people had done by hand.
  • Shifting cultivation: Shifting cultivation is clearing forest plots in turn, not using machines.
  • The Columbian Exchange: The Columbian Exchange moved crops and animals between hemispheres.

5. According to the passage, how did the reaper change the work of the harvest?

  1. Wheat could be grown in places with no rain
  2. Farmers no longer needed horses to farm
  3. A small crew could harvest many more acres in a day
  4. Each acre of wheat produced twice as much grain
Show the answer

The reaper raised output per worker at harvest time. It saved labor rather than growing more grain on each acre.

  • Wheat could be grown in places with no rain: The passage is about harvesting, not water.
  • Farmers no longer needed horses to farm: The reaper was pulled by horses.
  • Correct: A small crew could harvest many more acres in a day: Right: the passage says a few people could harvest many times more land in a day.
  • Each acre of wheat produced twice as much grain: The reaper made cutting faster; it did not change how much grain grew on an acre.

6. Turnips and clover in the new rotations fed more animals through the winter. What was a likely effect on people's diets in England?

  1. More meat, milk and other animal foods
  2. Fewer animals, because land went to crops
  3. A diet of grain alone, with no animal foods
  4. Less food overall, because rotation wore out the soil
Show the answer

Better feed meant larger herds and better diets, one reason people in Britain grew healthier and lived longer.

  • Correct: More meat, milk and other animal foods: Right: more animals kept through winter meant more meat and dairy.
  • Fewer animals, because land went to crops: Feed crops let farmers keep more animals, not fewer.
  • A diet of grain alone, with no animal foods: Animal foods increased.
  • Less food overall, because rotation wore out the soil: Rotation protected the soil and raised output.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The Second Agricultural Revolution, from the 1700s in Britain and the 1800s in North America, brought crop rotation, selective breeding, enclosure and mechanization. Each farm worker grew more food, diets and life spans improved, and people no longer needed on farms moved to towns.
