Unit 5 · Topic 5.6

Agricultural Production Regions

Subsistence agriculture feeds the farm family; commercial agriculture sells for profit and often uses monocropping.

Practice 1: Concepts and ProcessesPractice 2: Spatial Relationships

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A hectare of farmland just outside Rotterdam can cost many times what a hectare costs on the cattle lands of Patagonia. That price gap shapes what farmers grow: costly land near a market must earn a lot, so it is farmed intensively, while cheap, distant land is used for grazing.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Many land users want land with easy access to a market→land near the market costs more
  2. Land near the market costs more→only uses that earn a lot per hectare can afford it
  3. Market gardening earns the most per hectare but loses value fast with distance→market gardens take land closest to the market
  4. Ranching earns little per hectare but ships cheaply→ranching takes the cheapest, most distant land

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Subsistence agriculture feeds the farm family (rice plots in Bangladesh); commercial agriculture sells for profit (soybeans in Mato Grosso, Brazil).
  • Monocropping grows the same crop on a field year after year, and monoculture one crop over a large area: efficient, but risky if pests, drought or prices strike.
  • Bid-rent theory: land near a market costs more, so the use that can pay most wins it. Intensive uses take the center, extensive uses the edges. It assumes one market and equal transport.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Subsistence means extensive, and commercial means intensive.

What actually happens: Subsistence and commercial describe purpose (eat or sell); intensive and extensive describe inputs per hectare. A Bangladesh rice paddy is subsistence and intensive; an Argentine ranch is commercial and extensive.

What three kinds of farming can pay for land

Invented data for practice.

1. According to the chart, which kind of farming can pay the highest rent for land 10 km from the market?

  1. Market gardening
  2. Grain
  3. Ranching
  4. The three can pay about the same
Show the answer

Read up from 10 km on the distance axis: the market gardening line is highest, so that use would outbid the others for this land.

  • Correct: Market gardening: Right: at 10 km market gardening can pay about 700 dollars a hectare, more than grain or ranching.
  • Grain: Grain can pay about 340 dollars a hectare at 10 km, less than market gardening.
  • Ranching: Ranching can pay the least near the market, about 140 dollars a hectare.
  • The three can pay about the same: The lines are far apart at 10 km.

2. Between 40 and 50 km from the market, which kind of farming can pay the most for land?

  1. Market gardening
  2. Ranching
  3. Grain
  4. Ranching and grain equally
Show the answer

Bid-rent theory says land goes to the use that can pay the most. In the middle band that is grain, so a ring of grain farms forms there.

  • Market gardening: Market gardening falls fast and reaches zero at 45 km.
  • Ranching: Ranching overtakes grain past about 56 km, beyond this band.
  • Correct: Grain: Right: past about 36 km the grain line is above the market gardening line, and it stays above ranching until about 56 km.
  • Ranching and grain equally: Grain is well above ranching between 40 and 50 km; they cross only past about 56 km.

3. Why does the market gardening line fall faster than the ranching line?

  1. Ranchers sell their animals at a much higher price per hectare
  2. Vegetables grow faster far away from the market
  3. Market gardens need less labor for each hectare
  4. Its fresh produce costs more to ship and spoils on long trips
Show the answer

The slope shows how fast transport costs eat into what a use can earn. Perishable produce has the steepest slope.

  • Ranchers sell their animals at a much higher price per hectare: Ranching earns little per hectare; that is why its line starts lowest.
  • Vegetables grow faster far away from the market: Growth rate does not change with distance; shipping cost and spoilage do.
  • Market gardens need less labor for each hectare: Market gardens need much more labor per hectare, not less.
  • Correct: Its fresh produce costs more to ship and spoils on long trips: Right: perishable, bulky produce loses value fast with distance, so the rent it can pay drops quickly.

4. A new highway and refrigerated trucks cut the cost of shipping fresh produce. How would the market gardening line most likely change?

  1. It would fall more steeply, so gardening would move closer in to town
  2. It would fall less steeply, so gardening could extend farther out
  3. It would match the ranching line exactly
  4. It would disappear from the chart
Show the answer

Transport technology changes bid-rent patterns: when distance costs less, intensive uses can spread farther from the market.

  • It would fall more steeply, so gardening would move closer in to town: Cheaper shipping lowers the cost of distance, which flattens the line.
  • Correct: It would fall less steeply, so gardening could extend farther out: Right: lower transport cost flattens the line, so market gardening can outbid grain over a wider area.
  • It would match the ranching line exactly: Cheaper shipping helps produce most, so its line does not drop to ranching's.
  • It would disappear from the chart: Market gardening remains profitable near the market.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Subsistence agriculture feeds the farm family; commercial agriculture sells for profit and often uses monocropping. Bid-rent theory explains why intensive uses take costly land near a market and extensive uses take cheap, distant land, though real regions with many markets and routes depart from it.
