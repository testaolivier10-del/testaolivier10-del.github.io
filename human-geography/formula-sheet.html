AP® Human Geography

Formulas you must know

The exam gives you no formula sheet and no calculator. These are the rates, densities and ratios it asks you to work out by hand or read from a table, a pyramid or a map, in our own words: what each means, when to use it, and the trap that costs points.

Three habits that save points

  • Interpret, do not define. "Physiological density is people per farmland" earns little; "Egypt's physiological density is far above its arithmetic density, so its farmland along the Nile is under heavy pressure" earns the point.
  • Name the place and the scale. Say where a value is high or low, and at what scale the data are.
  • Say what the number means. A doubling time of 35 years means the population needs twice the schools and jobs in a generation.

Scale of Analysis and Map Scale

Ideas to read off a source

  • Scale of analysis. The scale of the data shown (global, regional, national, local), not the size of the area on the map. Provinces of one country are local (subnational) data. Scale of Analysis and Map Scale
  • Pyramid shapes. A wide base (expansive) means many children and fast growth; straight sides (stationary) slow growth; a narrow base (constrictive) an aging, shrinking population. Population Composition
  • Demographic transition stages. A pair of rates is consistent with a stage of the model; a country is never in a stage as a fact. Negative natural increase fits stage 5. The Demographic Transition Model
  • Say what a source cannot show. A national map hides differences inside the country; one year of data shows no trend. Reading Maps and Data

On exam day

  • No calculator. The AP® Human Geography exam allows no calculator. Every number it asks for (a rate, a density, a ratio, a doubling time) can be worked out by hand.
  • No formula sheet. Nothing on this page is printed in Bluebook: know what each measure means and how to read it from a table, a pyramid or a map.
