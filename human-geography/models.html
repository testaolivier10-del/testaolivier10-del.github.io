Models you must know

The 26 models the course and exam description names, each drawn, with what it claims, what it assumes, one limit and a real, dated example. A model is a simplified idea, not a fact about a place: on the exam, apply it to a named place and say where it fits and where it fails. 22 are flagged with the model students most often mix them up with.

Three habits that save points

  • Apply it to a named place. "Chicago in the 1920s fits the concentric zone model" earns more than a definition of the model.
  • Say "consistent with", not "is". A country's data are consistent with stage 3 of the demographic transition; the country is not in stage 3 as a fact.
  • Give a limit with its reason. "It was built on one US city before cars spread homes in every direction" is a limit; "it is old" is not.

Unit 1: Thinking Geographically

Environmental determinism and possibilism

Learn it in Human-Environmental Interaction

  1. 1Determinism: the land decidesView 1 (old, rejected)
  2. 2Possibilism: people chooseView 2
What it claims
Determinism, an old and rejected idea, says the physical environment decides how people live. Possibilism says the environment sets limits and offers options, and people choose among them with their culture and technology.
It assumes
  • Determinism: climate and land shape behavior directly.
  • Possibilism: people can change or work around their environment.
One limit
Determinism ignores culture, technology and choice, and it was used to justify European rule over other peoples; possibilism can understate real limits such as drought.
A real example
Possibilism: people in the Netherlands have drained land from the sea since the Middle Ages, and the Flevoland polders, drained in the 1950s and 1960s, became a province in 1986.

Easy to confuse with Demographic transition model. Not a stage model: these are two views of how much the environment controls people.

Unit 2: Population and Migration Patterns and Processes

Demographic transition model

A model built mostly from the history of Europe and North America in the 1800s and 1900s. · Learn it in The Demographic Transition Model

  1. 1High birth rate, high death rateStage 1
  2. 2High birth rate, falling death rateStage 2
  3. 3Falling birth rate, low death rateStage 3
  4. 4Low birth rate, low death rateStage 4
  5. 5Birth rate below the death rateStage 5
What it claims
Birth and death rates fall in sequence as a society changes, so population growth starts slow, speeds up, then slows again. This course shows five stages; some versions of the model show only four.
It assumes
  • Death rates fall first, as food, clean water and medicine improve.
  • Birth rates fall later, as children survive, more people live in cities and more women go to school and work for pay.
One limit
It was built mostly from European and North American history. Countries that changed later, such as many in Asia and Africa, moved through the stages faster and in different ways.
A real example
In 2023 Japan recorded about 727,000 births and about 1.58 million deaths (Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, 2024): rates consistent with stage 5, a population shrinking from natural decrease.

Easy to confuse with Epidemiological transition. The demographic transition tracks birth and death rates; the epidemiological transition tracks what people die of.

Epidemiological transition

Abdel Omran first described three stages in 1971; other researchers later added a fourth. · Learn it in The Demographic Transition Model

  1. 1Pestilence and famineStage 1
  2. 2Receding pandemicsStage 2
  3. 3Degenerative and human-made diseasesStage 3
  4. 4Delayed degenerative diseasesStage 4
What it claims
As a society changes, the main causes of death shift from infections and famine to chronic diseases such as heart disease and cancer.
It assumes
  • Better food, clean water and medicine cut deaths from infection first.
  • People then live long enough for chronic diseases to become the main causes of death.
One limit
New and returning infections break the sequence. Pandemics and new or drug-resistant infections can make infectious disease a leading cause of death again.
A real example
In the United States, the leading causes of death in 1900 were pneumonia and influenza, tuberculosis and diarrhea; in 2022 they were heart disease and cancer (CDC, National Center for Health Statistics): a move into the degenerative stage.

Easy to confuse with Demographic transition model. Its stages are about causes of death, not the birth and death rates the demographic transition shows.

Malthusian theory

Thomas Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798. · Learn it in Malthusian Theory

  1. 1Population (geometric growth)Spot 1: the line that curves upward
  2. 2Food supply (arithmetic growth)Spot 2: the straight line
  3. 3Point where food runs shortSpot 3: where the lines cross
  4. 4Checks: famine, disease, warSpot 4: the gap after they cross
What it claims
Population grows faster than the food supply, so in time food runs short and checks such as famine and disease follow.
It assumes
  • Population grows geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8, ...).
  • Food supply grows arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4, ...).
One limit
He did not foresee how much technology would raise food output. Machines, fertilizer and new crop varieties raised food output far faster than a straight line.
A real example
Malthus wrote in 1798. World population grew from about 1 billion around 1800 to about 8.2 billion in 2024 (UN WPP 2024), and food supply per person still rose, largely through new farm technology such as the Green Revolution.

Ravenstein’s laws of migration

E. G. Ravenstein, papers of 1885 and 1889, built largely on British census data. · Learn it in Women and Demographic Change

  1. 1Most migrants move a short waySpot 1: the many short arrows into a nearby town
  2. 2Migration often happens in stepsSpot 2: the chain from village to town to city
  3. 3Each flow has a counterflowSpot 3: the dashed arrow out of the city
  4. 4Long-distance moves go to big citiesSpot 4: the long arrow straight to the city
What it claims
Patterns in who moves, how far and where: most moves are short, many happen in steps, each flow has a counterflow, and long-distance migrants head for big cities.
It assumes
  • People move mostly for economic reasons.
  • Distance makes moving harder, so short moves are most common.
One limit
It was built on 19th-century British data. Its laws describe Britain in the 1800s, and patterns differ in other places and times.
A real example
Ravenstein drew his laws mainly from the British census of 1881, in papers published in 1885 and 1889: most migrants moved a short way, many of them toward growing industrial towns such as Manchester and Liverpool.

Unit 3: Cultural Patterns and Processes

Types of diffusion

A way geographers sort how ideas, goods and practices spread. · Learn it in Types of Diffusion

  1. 1RelocationSpot 1: spreads as the people who carry it move
  2. 2ExpansionSpot 2: spreads while it stays strong in its hearth
  3. 3ContagiousSpot 3: spreads person to person to nearby places
  4. 4HierarchicalSpot 4: spreads from big, connected places down
  5. 5StimulusSpot 5: the idea spreads, but the trait changes
What it claims
A trait spreads from its hearth in two main ways: with people who move (relocation) or while it stays strong at home (expansion), which can be contagious, hierarchical or stimulus.
It assumes
  • Every trait starts at a hearth, a place where it began.
  • How it spreads depends on who carries it and through which places.
One limit
Real spread usually mixes several types. A trait can spread through cities first and then to their neighbors, which is hierarchical and contagious at once.
A real example
Relocation: Spanish spread to the Americas with colonists after 1492. Contagious: COVID-19 spread from person to person after the first cases were reported in Wuhan, China, in December 2019 (WHO).

Easy to confuse inside this model. Relocation diffusion moves with migrants, so the source loses some of its carriers; expansion diffusion (contagious, hierarchical, stimulus) spreads while the source keeps the trait.

Unit 4: Political Patterns and Processes

Boundary types by origin

A classification geographers use for how political boundaries began. · Learn it in Defining Political Boundaries

  1. 1GeometricSpot 1: a straight line along a parallel
  2. 2AntecedentSpot 2: drawn before many people settled the area
  3. 3SubsequentSpot 3: drawn as the area grew, after people settled
  4. 4ConsequentSpot 4: drawn to separate two language groups
  5. 5SuperimposedSpot 5: drawn by an outside power through a group
  6. 6RelictSpot 6: the dashed line, no longer a boundary
What it claims
Boundaries can be sorted by how and when they were drawn: antecedent, subsequent, consequent, superimposed, relict and geometric.
It assumes
  • A boundary’s type comes from its history: who drew it, when, and around what.
One limit
One boundary can fit more than one type. A straight line drawn by an outside power is both geometric and superimposed.
A real example
The 49th parallel between Canada and the United States, agreed in 1818 and 1846 before most settlers arrived, is geometric and antecedent; the pieces of the Berlin Wall (1961 to 1989) left standing are a relict boundary.

Easy to confuse inside this model. Antecedent is drawn before most settlers arrive; subsequent is drawn after, as the cultural landscape takes shape; a consequent boundary is a subsequent one drawn along a cultural line; superimposed is forced on by an outside power.

Centripetal and centrifugal forces

Learn it in Consequences of Centrifugal and Centripetal Forces

  1. 1Centripetal: a shared languageTier 1: pulls together
  2. 2Centrifugal: a deep regional divideTier 2: pushes apart
What it claims
Centripetal forces pull a state's people together; centrifugal forces push them apart. The balance between them shapes whether a state holds together or devolves power.
It assumes
  • A shared trait can unite and a sharp divide can split.
  • The same trait can work in either direction at different scales.
One limit
It names forces but does not measure them, so it cannot say on its own whether a state will break apart.
A real example
In Belgium, the language divide between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia is a centrifugal force; Belgium became a federal state in 1993, partly to manage it.

Easy to confuse with Boundary types by origin. These are forces within a state, not types of boundary lines.

Unit 5: Agriculture and Rural Land-Use Patterns and Processes

Von Thünen model

Johann Heinrich von Thünen, The Isolated State, 1826, drawn from farming on his estate in northern Germany. · Learn it in Von Thünen Model

  1. 1Market gardening and dairyingRing 1 (next to the market)
  2. 2Forest (wood and fuel)Ring 2
  3. 3Grain and field cropsRing 3
  4. 4Livestock ranchingRing 4 (outermost)
What it claims
Farmers choose what to grow by the cost of moving it to one market, so land use forms rings around the market town.
It assumes
  • One market town on a flat, uniform plain with the same soil and climate everywhere.
  • Farmers carry goods to market themselves, so cost grows with distance.
One limit
It assumes a flat, uniform plain with one market. Real land has hills, rivers, different soils and many markets.
A real example
Von Thünen published The Isolated State in 1826, drawn from his own estate near Rostock, Germany. Perishable farming still clusters near big markets: the greenhouses of the Westland area near Rotterdam and The Hague supply fresh vegetables and flowers.

Easy to confuse with Bid-rent theory. Von Thünen rings are farm land uses around a market town; bid-rent is land uses in a city, from the CBD outward.

Commodity dependence

Learn it in The Global System of Agriculture

  1. 1One main raw exportStage 1
  2. 2Its world price fallsStage 2
  3. 3Export earnings and jobs fallStage 3
  4. 4Debt rises and services are cutStage 4
What it claims
A country that earns most of its export income from one or a few raw goods rises and falls with their world prices.
It assumes
  • Raw goods prices swing more than prices of factory goods.
  • The country has few other exports to fall back on.
One limit
Some countries that export mostly raw goods, such as Norway with oil, stay wealthy because they save and invest the earnings.
A real example
In 2015, as world copper prices fell, Zambia, whose exports are mostly copper, saw its currency, the kwacha, lose much of its value against the dollar.

Easy to confuse with Dependency theory. Commodity dependence is about relying on a few exports; dependency theory is a theory of why poorer countries stay poor.

Unit 6: Cities and Urban Land-Use Patterns and Processes

Bid-rent theory

An economic theory used for both farmland (Unit 5) and cities (Unit 6). · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Retail and officesSpot 1: the steepest part, at the CBD
  2. 2IndustrySpot 2: the next part out
  3. 3HousingSpot 3: the flatter part farther out
  4. 4FarmingSpot 4: the flattest part, farthest out
What it claims
Land users bid more for land with the best access to the center, so the price of land, and how intensely it is used, falls with distance from the CBD.
It assumes
  • One center with the best access.
  • Each land user pays only as much as access is worth to it.
One limit
Many cities have several centers, not one CBD. Edge cities and suburban centers create more than one peak in land value.
A real example
In Chicago, the tallest office towers, such as the Willis Tower (finished in 1973), stand in the Loop, where land costs the most; housing and then farms take over as land gets cheaper with distance.

Easy to confuse with Von Thünen model. Both are rings set by distance to a center, but bid-rent is about who can pay the most for city land; von Thünen is about farm products and transport costs.

Rank-size rule and primate city

A rule geographers use to compare a country’s cities by size. · Learn it in The Size and Distribution of Cities

  1. 1City of 1,200,000Rank 1 (largest city)
  2. 2City of 600,000Rank 2
  3. 3City of 400,000Rank 3
  4. 4City of 300,000Rank 4
What it claims
In some countries the city ranked n has about 1/n the people of the largest city. Where one city is far larger than the rest, it is a primate city.
It assumes
  • City sizes follow a regular pattern down the ranks.
One limit
It fits some countries, not those with a primate city. Where one city is far larger, the rule does not hold.
A real example
In the 2020 US census, New York City had about 8.8 million people, Los Angeles about 3.9 million (about half) and Chicago about 2.7 million (about a third): close to the rank-size rule.

Easy to confuse with Central place theory. Rank-size is about how big each city is by rank; central place theory is about the spacing and market areas of centers. A primate city breaks the rank-size rule: it is far more than twice the second city.

Gravity model

A model borrowed from physics; the version used here is population × population ÷ distance squared. · Learn it in The Size and Distribution of Cities

  1. 1Pair B: 7 and 4 million, 200 kmMost interaction
  2. 2Pair C: 5 and 2 million, 300 kmMiddle
  3. 3Pair A: 2 and 1 million, 400 kmLeast interaction
What it claims
Interaction between two places (trade, travel, migration) grows with their populations and falls with the distance between them.
It assumes
  • Bigger places attract more interaction.
  • Distance makes interaction harder (distance decay).
One limit
Borders, language and culture also shape interaction. Two big, close cities across a closed border may interact very little.
A real example
In 2019 Dublin to London was one of the busiest international air routes in Europe: two large cities a short flight apart, as the gravity model expects.

Easy to confuse with Rank-size rule and primate city. The gravity model predicts interaction between two places from size and distance; rank-size predicts city sizes alone.

Central place theory

Walter Christaller, 1933, from towns in southern Germany. · Learn it in The Size and Distribution of Cities

  1. 1City (high-order center)Spot 1: the center of the largest hexagon
  2. 2Town (middle-order center)Spot 2: a corner of the largest hexagon’s inner ring
  3. 3Village (low-order center)Spot 3: a small dot between those corners
  4. 4Market area of a villageSpot 4: the small shaded hexagon
What it claims
Settlements of different sizes serve market areas shaped like hexagons; bigger places offer higher-order goods that people travel farther for.
It assumes
  • A flat plain with people and buying power spread evenly.
  • People shop at the nearest place that offers what they need.
One limit
It assumes a flat plain with people spread evenly. Real land has hills, rivers and uneven population.
A real example
Christaller published the theory in 1933 from southern Germany, where cities such as Munich, Nuremberg and Stuttgart served as high-order centers with market towns spaced between them.

Easy to confuse with Gravity model. Central place theory explains where centers sit and how far people travel for each good (range and threshold); the gravity model explains how much two places interact.

Burgess concentric zone model

Ernest Burgess, 1925, built on Chicago. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Zone in transition (factories)Ring 1 (next to the CBD)
  2. 2Working-class homesRing 2
  3. 3Middle-class homesRing 3
  4. 4Commuter zoneRing 4 (outermost)
What it claims
A city grows outward from its CBD in rings, with newer and wealthier housing farther out.
It assumes
  • One CBD at the center.
  • People move outward as they can afford to, and newcomers settle near the center.
One limit
It was built on one city, Chicago, in the 1920s. Other cities, and later times, differ.
A real example
Chicago in the 1920s: the Loop at the center, factories and immigrant neighborhoods in the zone around it, and commuter towns along the rail lines beyond the city.

Easy to confuse with Hoyt sector model. Burgess draws rings around the CBD; Hoyt draws wedges along transport lines.

Hoyt sector model

Homer Hoyt, 1939, from housing and rent data for 142 US cities. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Industry and warehousesSpot 1: the wedge along the rail line
  2. 2Low-income housingSpot 2: the wedges on both sides of the rail wedge
  3. 3Middle-income housingSpot 3: the wedges next to those
  4. 4High-income housingSpot 4: the wedge along the boulevard to the lake
What it claims
Land use grows outward from the CBD in wedges along transport routes; high-income housing follows the most attractive corridor, and low-income housing sits beside industry.
It assumes
  • One CBD at the center.
  • A land use that starts along a route keeps growing outward along it.
One limit
It still assumes one CBD at the center. Many cities grow around several centers.
A real example
Hoyt's 1939 study of 142 US cities: in Chicago, high-rent housing ran north along the Lake Michigan shore, away from the industry along the rail lines.

Easy to confuse with Burgess concentric zone model. Hoyt's land uses run out from the CBD in wedges along roads and rails, not in even rings.

Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model

Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman, 1945. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Central business districtSpot 1: the old center where the main roads meet
  2. 2Wholesale and light manufacturingSpot 2: beside it, along the rail line
  3. 3Heavy industrySpot 3: by the river port and the rail line
  4. 4Outlying business districtSpot 4: a second center of shops and offices
  5. 5Residential suburbSpot 5: far from industry, on the quiet side
  6. 6Industrial suburbSpot 6: out along the rail line, beyond the city
What it claims
A city grows around several centers (nuclei), not one CBD; some activities cluster together, and others keep apart.
It assumes
  • Some activities need special sites (a port, a rail yard).
  • Similar activities gain by clustering; some, like heavy industry and high-income housing, repel each other.
One limit
It does not predict where new centers will form. It shows that a city has several nuclei, not where the next one goes.
A real example
Metro Los Angeles in the 2020s, with separate centers at Downtown, the Port of Los Angeles, the airport district around LAX, university campuses and the film studios.

Easy to confuse with Galactic city model. Multiple nuclei is several specialized centers inside one city; the galactic city is a car-based metro area with edge cities ringing an old downtown.

Galactic city model

Described for US metropolitan areas in the late 1900s; also called the peripheral model. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Old central city (CBD)Spot 1: the old center
  2. 2Edge city (offices and malls)Spot 2: where a highway meets the ring road
  3. 3Suburban housingSpot 3: the areas between highways
  4. 4Airport and business parkSpot 4: the big site with runways
What it claims
In a car-based metropolitan area, the old central city is one center among many; offices, shops and homes spread along highways and a beltway, with edge cities at the junctions.
It assumes
  • Most people travel by car.
  • A beltway and highways make places far from the old CBD easy to reach.
One limit
It fits US car-based metros better than other places. Cities built around walking or transit do not spread this way.
A real example
Tysons, Virginia, outside Washington, D.C., grew from a rural crossroads in the 1960s into a cluster of offices and malls along highways, and was described as an edge city in 1991.

Easy to confuse with Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model. The galactic city adds edge cities and beltways around a metro area; multiple nuclei describes separate centers inside one city.

Latin American city model

A model drawn from many cities in Latin America in the late 1900s. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1CBDSpot 1: the center
  2. 2Spine and elite housingSpot 2: the wedge along the main avenue
  3. 3Zone of maturitySpot 3: the ring next to the center
  4. 4Zone of in situ accretionSpot 4: the middle ring
  5. 5Squatter settlementsSpot 5: the outer ring
  6. 6Disamenity sectorSpot 6: the wedge along the river
What it claims
A CBD and a commercial spine with elite housing run out along a main avenue; quality of housing falls toward the edge, where squatter settlements grow.
It assumes
  • Colonial planning centered the city on a plaza and its CBD.
  • Rapid growth from rural migrants happens at the edge.
One limit
Cities in the region differ a lot from one another. Each city’s site and history shape it in its own way.
A real example
In Mexico City, the Paseo de la Reforma, laid out in the 1860s, became a spine of offices and wealthy homes running west from the old colonial center.

Easy to confuse with African city model. Both have squatter or informal settlements at the edge, but only the Latin American model has a commercial spine with elite housing.

Your class may name its authors, Ernst Griffin and Larry Ford (1980).

Southeast Asian city model

A model drawn from port cities in Southeast Asia in the 1960s. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Old colonial port zoneSpot 1: at the waterfront
  2. 2Western and immigrant commerceSpot 2: next to the waterfront
  3. 3Squatter settlementsSpot 3: scattered at the edge of the city
  4. 4Market gardeningSpot 4: the band beyond the city
What it claims
Many port cities in Southeast Asia focus on the old colonial port, not one strong CBD, with commercial zones beside it and squatter areas and farmland at the edge.
It assumes
  • The colonial port was the reason the city grew.
  • Trade, not one CBD, organizes the city.
One limit
Cities in the region differ a lot from one another. Not every city is a port, and each has its own history.
A real example
Jakarta, Indonesia, grew around the port of Batavia, founded by the Dutch in 1619; the old port zone sits on the coast, with commercial zones inland and informal settlements on the edges.

Easy to confuse with Latin American city model. The Southeast Asian model centers on the old colonial port, not a plaza and CBD with a spine.

Your class may name its author, T. G. McGee (1967).

African city model

A model drawn from cities in Sub-Saharan Africa in the late 1900s. · Learn it in The Internal Structure of Cities

  1. 1Colonial CBDSpot 1: the center laid out in a grid
  2. 2Traditional CBDSpot 2: the center with winding streets
  3. 3Market zoneSpot 3: the area below the two centers
  4. 4Ethnic and mixed neighborhoodsSpot 4: the ring of neighborhoods
  5. 5Mining and manufacturingSpot 5: the wedge along the rail line
  6. 6Informal satellite townshipsSpot 6: beyond the city edge
What it claims
Many cities in Sub-Saharan Africa have three central areas side by side (a colonial CBD, a traditional CBD and a market zone), with neighborhoods around them and informal settlements beyond the edge.
It assumes
  • Colonial rule built a separate Western-style center beside an older one.
  • Rapid growth adds informal settlements at the edge.
One limit
Cities across Africa differ a lot. A model of a whole region’s cities cannot fit every city.
A real example
Nairobi, Kenya, founded in 1899 as a railway depot, has a colonial-era CBD, a busy market zone and informal settlements such as Kibera.

Easy to confuse with Latin American city model. The African model has three CBDs (colonial, traditional and market) instead of one CBD with a spine.

Your class may name its author, Harm de Blij.

Unit 7: Industrial and Economic Development Patterns and Processes

Least cost theory

A theory of where factories locate, from the early 1900s. · Learn it in Economic Sectors and Patterns

  1. 1Copper smelter (loses weight)Spot 1: next to the mine
  2. 2Soft-drink bottler (gains weight)Spot 2: in the market city
  3. 3Refinery for imported crude oilSpot 3: at the port, where cargo is unloaded
What it claims
A factory locates where its costs of transport, labor and clustering are lowest; a process that loses weight locates near its raw materials, one that gains weight near its market.
It assumes
  • Firms only try to keep costs low.
  • Transport cost depends on weight and distance.
One limit
It assumes firms only try to keep costs low. Owners also choose places for markets, skills or quality of life.
A real example
Copper is smelted near the mines in Zambia's Copperbelt, where smelters opened in the 1930s: smelting loses weight, so it pays to process the ore before shipping it.

Easy to confuse with Central place theory. Least cost theory places one factory by transport, labor and agglomeration costs; central place theory places markets and services.

Your class may name Alfred Weber (1909).

Rostow’s stages of economic growth

W. W. Rostow, The Stages of Economic Growth, 1960. · Learn it in Theories of Development

  1. 1Traditional societyStage 1
  2. 2Preconditions for take-offStage 2
  3. 3Take-offStage 3
  4. 4Drive to maturityStage 4
  5. 5High mass consumptionStage 5
What it claims
Countries develop by passing through five stages, from a traditional farming society to an economy of high mass consumption.
It assumes
  • Every country follows the same path that Western Europe and North America took.
  • Investment and industry drive growth.
One limit
It assumes every country follows one Western path. Countries develop in different ways and orders.
A real example
Rostow published The Stages of Economic Growth in 1960, drawing on the histories of Britain and the United States; South Korea's fast growth from the 1960s on is often read as a take-off.

Easy to confuse with Wallerstein’s world system theory. Rostow has each country climb the same five stages on its own; Wallerstein sees one world economy with core, semiperiphery and periphery.

Wallerstein’s world system theory

Immanuel Wallerstein, from 1974. · Learn it in Theories of Development

  1. 1High wages, high-tech goods, capitalTier 1: Core
  2. 2A mix of core and periphery activitiesTier 2: Semiperiphery
  3. 3Low wages, raw materials for exportTier 3: Periphery
What it claims
One world economy links core, semiperiphery and periphery; the core gains most from the links. Dependency theory and commodity dependence explain why the periphery can stay poor.
It assumes
  • Countries are tied together in one capitalist world economy.
  • Tiers are positions in that system, not stages; countries can move between them.
One limit
Countries are hard to place, since tiers are not measured one way. Different measures can put a country in different tiers.
A real example
Wallerstein set out the theory in 1974. Countries often classed as semiperiphery include Brazil, China, India and Mexico, but the tiers are a model, not an official list.

Easy to confuse with Rostow’s stages of economic growth. World-system theory has tiers in one connected economy, not stages a country passes through alone.

Easy to confuse with Dependency theory. Dependency theory has two groups, rich and poor, and blames trade ties for poverty; world-system theory adds the semiperiphery.

Dependency theory

Learn it in Theories of Development

  1. 1Sells factory goods, lends moneyTier 1: the richer core
  2. 2Sells cheap raw materialsTier 2: the poorer periphery
What it claims
Poorer countries stay poor because of how they are tied to richer ones: since colonial times they export cheap raw materials and import costly factory goods.
It assumes
  • The world economy has a rich center and a poor edge.
  • Trade ties built under colonial rule continue after independence.
One limit
Some former colonies, such as South Korea and Singapore, became wealthy through trade with richer countries.
A real example
In the 1950s, economists at the UN Economic Commission for Latin America argued that exporting raw goods and importing factory goods kept the region poor.

Easy to confuse with Wallerstein’s world system theory. Dependency theory has two groups; world-system theory adds a semiperiphery and treats the world as one system.

Easy to confuse with Commodity dependence. Dependency is a theory of poverty; commodity dependence is one pattern of trade it points to.

Complementarity and comparative advantage

Learn it in Trade and the World Economy

  1. 1Each has what the other needsStage 1
  2. 2Each makes what costs it leastStage 2
  3. 3They trade and both gainStage 3
What it claims
Trade happens when one place has what another needs (complementarity), and each place gains by making what it gives up the least to make (comparative advantage).
It assumes
  • Goods can be moved at a cost worth paying.
  • Each place can shift workers and land into the good it makes best.
One limit
It leaves out who wins and loses inside a country: workers in a sector that shrinks under trade can lose their jobs.
A real example
Costa Rica grows bananas in its warm lowlands and Canada grows wheat and makes machines, so each supplies what the other needs; Ricardo explained comparative advantage in 1817 with English cloth and Portuguese wine.

Easy to confuse with Gravity model. Complementarity says why two places trade at all; the gravity model says how much, from size and distance. Comparative advantage is not absolute advantage: a country better at everything still gains by trading.
