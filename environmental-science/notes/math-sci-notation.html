Environmental science is full of numbers that are too big or too small to write comfortably: the mass of carbon dioxide in the air, the water a country uses, a trace of a chemical in a glass of water. Scientific notation and metric prefixes are two ways to write those numbers compactly. On the exam you have only a scientific calculator and no formula sheet, so you need to move between the forms quickly and without losing a zero.

Scientific notation

A number in scientific notation has two parts: a front number from 1 up to (but not including) 10, and a power of ten.

2,500,000=2.5×1062{,}500{,}000 = 2.5 \times 10^{6}

To write a number this way, move the decimal point until exactly one nonzero digit is to its left, then count how many places you moved it. Moving it left gives a positive exponent (a big number); moving it right gives a negative exponent (a small number): 0.00042=4.2×10−40.00042 = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}.

Scale words are the same powers of ten in words. Write each number all three ways at least once until it feels automatic:

Scale wordPower of tenWritten out
thousand10310^{3}1,000
million10610^{6}1,000,000
billion10910^{9}1,000,000,000
trillion101210^{12}1,000,000,000,000

So 2.5 million = 2.5×1062.5 \times 10^{6} = 2,500,000, and 40 billion = 4.0×10104.0 \times 10^{10} = 40,000,000,000.

Multiplying and dividing

Powers of ten follow two rules you can check with zeros.

  • Multiply: multiply the front numbers and add the exponents. 104×103=10710^{4} \times 10^{3} = 10^{7} (10,000 × 1,000 has 4 + 3 = 7 zeros).
  • Divide: divide the front numbers and subtract the exponents. 109÷106=10310^{9} \div 10^{6} = 10^{3}.

If the front number ends up outside 1 to 10, fix it at the end: 0.5×104=5.0×1030.5 \times 10^{4} = 5.0 \times 10^{3}, and 12×106=1.2×10712 \times 10^{6} = 1.2 \times 10^{7}. Moving the point one place left adds 1 to the exponent; one place right subtracts 1.

Worked example: per person. A country of 2.4×1082.4 \times 10^{8} people throws away 1.2×10111.2 \times 10^{11} kg of trash in a year. How much is that per person?

Setup: 1.2×1011kg2.4×108people\frac{1.2 \times 10^{11}\,\text{kg}}{2.4 \times 10^{8}\,\text{people}}

Fronts: 1.2÷2.4=0.51.2 \div 2.4 = 0.5. Exponents: 11−8=311 - 8 = 3. So 0.5×1030.5 \times 10^{3}, which is 5.0×1025.0 \times 10^{2} kg per person = 500 kg per person.

On a calculator, type the powers with brackets or the EE key: (1.2 × 10^11) / (2.4 × 10^8). Without the brackets the calculator divides by 2.4 and then multiplies by 10810^{8}, which is a very different number.

Metric prefixes

A metric prefix in front of a unit is a power of ten in disguise. A kilogram is 10310^{3} grams; a megaliter is 10610^{6} liters; a milligram is 10−310^{-3} grams.

To change a prefix, replace it with its power of ten. 750 ML = 750×106750 \times 10^{6} L = 7.5×1087.5 \times 10^{8} L. Going to a smaller unit makes the number bigger (2.5 g = 2,500 mg); going to a bigger unit makes it smaller (2,500 g = 2.5 kg).

Worked example: a daily total. A landfill takes in 4.0×1054.0 \times 10^{5} kg of trash a day. How much in 30 days?

Setup: 4.0×105kg/day×30days4.0 \times 10^{5}\,\text{kg/day} \times 30\,\text{days}. The days cancel, leaving kg.

4.0×30=1204.0 \times 30 = 120, so 120×105120 \times 10^{5} kg =1.2×107= 1.2 \times 10^{7} kg = 12 million kg.

Comparing numbers

Before comparing, put every number in the same form and the same unit. 5×1085 \times 10^{8} L looks larger than 3×1093 \times 10^{9} L because 5 is bigger than 3, but the power of ten matters far more: 3×1093 \times 10^{9} is six times larger. And 2 GL (gigaliters) is 2×1092 \times 10^{9} L, so compare it only after you rewrite it in liters.

Exam habits

  • Write the setup with units, then the answer with its unit and its scale: "6.0×1076.0 \times 10^{7} kg", not "6.0".
  • If a question says "in millions", give the number of millions with the word: "12 million kg".
  • Check the size: a city's water use in liters will be billions, not thousands.
