Energy chain

Pick a fuel and follow its energy to the outlet.

About this tool

Pick a power source and follow 100 units of energy from the fuel (or the wind, the sun, the water) to your outlet. Each step keeps only part of what it is given; the rest leaves as heat. Then compare coal, natural gas, nuclear, hydro, wind and solar on carbon dioxide, how often they run and whether they can follow demand, and work the kWh, BTU and CO2 numbers the exam asks for.

Where the numbers come from

Efficiency of each plant. From the U.S. Energy Information Administration's average heat rates for 2024 (Electric Power Annual, table 8.1): coal 10,777, natural gas 7,754 and nuclear 10,443 BTU of fuel per kWh of electricity. Since 1 kWh = 3,412 BTU, efficiency = 3,412 ÷ heat rate: about 32% for coal, 44% for natural gas (mostly combined-cycle plants) and 33% for nuclear. The split between boiler, turbine and generator inside each plant is a typical one, chosen so the steps multiply to within half a point of that figure. Hydro, wind and solar stage efficiencies are typical published values (a large hydro plant about 90%, a modern wind turbine about 40% of the wind's energy, below the 59% Betz limit, and solar panels about 20%).

Power lines. About 5% of electricity is lost in transmission and distribution in the United States (EIA).

Carbon dioxide. At the plant: 2.31 lb per kWh for coal and 0.96 for natural gas (EIA, 2023). Life cycle (mining, building, fuel and decommissioning included): the medians in the IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report (Working Group III, Annex III), in grams of CO2-equivalent per kWh: coal 820, gas 490, solar 48, hydro 24, nuclear 12, wind 11.

Capacity factor. The share of the year a plant's full output is actually produced, from EIA's Electric Power Monthly (rounded U.S. averages of recent years): nuclear above 90%, natural gas combined cycle near 60%, coal about 40% (falling), hydro and wind in the 30s, solar about 25%.

Every calculation in the drills uses ApEnvCalc, the same recipes as the question bank. 1 kWh = 3,412 BTU and 1 short ton = 2,000 lb, as the course rounds them.
