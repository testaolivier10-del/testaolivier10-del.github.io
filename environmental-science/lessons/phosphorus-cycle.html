Unit 1 · Topic 1.6

The Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus is stored mostly in rock and ocean sediments and has no significant gas phase, so it cycles slowly.

Practice 1: Concept ExplanationPractice 2: Visual Representations

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A clear lake in spring, a green, cloudy lake by August: the difference is often a few kilograms of phosphate washed in from lawns, farms and wastewater. Phosphorus is scarce in nature because it never travels through the air. It creeps out of rock, rides in water and on soil, and settles to the bottom of the sea.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Weathering breaks down rock→phosphate enters the soil
  2. Plants take up phosphate and animals eat plants→decomposers return phosphate to the soil when they die
  3. Rain washes phosphate-carrying soil into water→it settles as sediment and returns to land only when rock is uplifted, over millions of years

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The main reservoirs are rock and ocean sediments; the phosphorus cycle has no significant gas phase, so it is slow.
  • Weathering releases phosphate; plants take it up; decomposers return it; sedimentation stores it.
  • Phosphorus builds DNA, ATP, cell membranes, bones and teeth.
  • Phosphate is often the limiting nutrient in fresh water, so extra phosphate (fertilizer, detergents, wastewater) makes algae grow fast.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Phosphorus cycles through the air like carbon and nitrogen.

What actually happens: Phosphorus has no significant gas form. It moves in rock, soil, water and living things, which is why it cycles slowly and is so often in short supply.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. Why does phosphorus usually move through ecosystems more slowly than carbon or nitrogen?

  1. It has no significant gas phase.
  2. Plants do not need phosphorus to grow and reproduce.
  3. It is released mainly by volcanoes, which erupt rarely.
  4. Decomposers do not release it from dead plants and animals.
Show the answer

Carbon and nitrogen move quickly through the atmosphere. Phosphorus must be weathered from rock and moved by water, which is slow.

  • Correct: It has no significant gas phase.: Right: no trip through the air makes it slow.
  • Plants do not need phosphorus to grow and reproduce.: Plants need phosphorus for DNA, ATP and cell membranes.
  • It is released mainly by volcanoes, which erupt rarely.: Weathering of rock, not volcanoes, is the main source.
  • Decomposers do not release it from dead plants and animals.: Decomposers do return phosphate to the soil.

7. Which action would most directly lower phosphate in the farmland stream?

  1. Winter cover crops on the fields
  2. Adding more phosphate fertilizer just before heavy rain
  3. Removing the trees that grow along the stream banks
  4. Plowing the fields right before the winter storms
Show the answer

Plant cover holds soil, and phosphate moves on soil. Less soil lost means less phosphate in the stream.

  • Correct: Winter cover crops on the fields: Right: keeping soil in place keeps phosphate out of the water.
  • Adding more phosphate fertilizer just before heavy rain: More fertilizer before rain adds phosphate.
  • Removing the trees that grow along the stream banks: Trees along the bank catch soil; removing them adds phosphate.
  • Plowing the fields right before the winter storms: Bare plowed soil washes away in storms.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Phosphorus is stored mostly in rock and ocean sediments and has no significant gas phase, so it cycles slowly. Weathering releases phosphate, plants take it up, animals eat plants, and decomposers return it to the soil. Rain carries phosphate on soil into water, where it settles as sediment. Phosphorus often limits growth in fresh water, so extra phosphate makes algae grow.
