Unit 9 · Topic 9.9

Endangered Species

Endangered species face extinction now; threatened species likely will soon.

Practice 1: Concept ExplanationPractice 7: Environmental Solutions

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1963, fewer than 500 pairs of bald eagles nested in the lower 48 states. DDT had thinned their eggshells, and hunting and the clearing of nesting trees had done the rest. By 2007 there were nearly 10,000 pairs, and the national bird came off the endangered list. Protection worked, but only after the cause was found and stopped.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Specialist, K-selected species with small ranges→cannot easily shift to new food or habitat and replace losses slowly
  2. Shrinking habitat, hunting, trade, pollution or invaders reduce their numbers→they become threatened, then endangered
  3. Small populations lose genetic diversity→they are more vulnerable to disease and change
  4. The Endangered Species Act, CITES, protected areas and captive breeding→can stop the decline and let numbers recover

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Endangered species: in danger of extinction in all or much of its range. Threatened species: likely to become endangered soon.
  • Most at risk: specialists, K-selected species, small or isolated populations, species needing large territories, those hunted or traded.
  • The Endangered Species Act (US, 1973) lists species, bans harming them and protects critical habitat. CITES is the international treaty controlling trade.
  • Recoveries: bald eagle (DDT ban plus protection), California condor and black-footed ferret (captive breeding and release).

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: An endangered species just needs to be bred in zoos to be saved.

What actually happens: Captive breeding helps when numbers are very low, but a species recovers in the wild only if the cause of decline is removed and enough habitat is protected.

Check yourself

1. Which species is most at risk of extinction?

  1. A fast-breeding generalist found on most continents
  2. A slow-breeding specialist with a small range
  3. A common bird that eats many kinds of seeds
  4. A weed that spreads easily into disturbed land
Show the answer

Extinction risk rises with specialization, slow reproduction, small or isolated populations and large territory needs.

  • A fast-breeding generalist found on most continents: Generalists with many offspring and wide ranges are least at risk.
  • Correct: A slow-breeding specialist with a small range: Right: specialist and K-selected traits plus a small range make recovery hard.
  • A common bird that eats many kinds of seeds: A broad diet and large numbers lower risk.
  • A weed that spreads easily into disturbed land: Weedy, r-selected species thrive in disturbed land.

Passage

How the bald eagle came back

In 1963 only 417 nesting pairs of bald eagles were left in the lower 48 states. The pesticide DDT, sprayed widely after World War II, washed into lakes and built up in the fish that eagles eat. In the eagles it caused thin eggshells that broke during nesting. The United States banned DDT for most uses in 1972, and the Endangered Species Act protected the birds and their nesting sites from shooting and disturbance. By 2007 there were 9,789 breeding pairs, and the bald eagle was removed from the endangered and threatened species list.

Summarized from U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, "Bald Eagle Numbers Soaring" (2007): 417 nesting pairs in 1963, 9,789 breeding pairs in 2007, lower 48 states.

5. What is the main claim of the passage?

  1. Bald eagles were not really at risk
  2. Captive breeding alone saved the bald eagle
  3. A DDT ban and protection let eagles recover

Show the answer

The author links the recovery to two actions: banning DDT and protecting the birds and their nests.

  • Bald eagles were not really at risk: The passage describes a fall to 417 nesting pairs.

  • Correct: A DDT ban and protection let eagles recover: Right: the passage credits the DDT ban and legal protection.

7. Which actions helped the bald eagle recover? Choose the best answer.

  1. A DDT ban plus legal protection
  2. Moving most eagles into zoos
  3. Introducing eagles from Europe
  4. Spraying more pesticides on lakes
Show the answer

DDT biomagnified up food chains and thinned eggshells; banning it removed the cause.

  • Correct: A DDT ban plus legal protection: Right: DDT thinned eggshells; the 1972 ban and ESA protection let numbers recover.
  • Moving most eagles into zoos: Most eagles stayed wild; captive breeding played a small part.
  • Introducing eagles from Europe: Bald eagles are North American; none were brought from Europe.
  • Spraying more pesticides on lakes: Pesticides such as DDT caused the decline.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Endangered species face extinction now; threatened species likely will soon. Specialists, K-selected species, small populations and species that need large territories are most at risk. The US Endangered Species Act protects listed species and their critical habitat, and CITES controls international trade. Recovery works when the cause of decline is removed, as with the bald eagle after DDT was banned.
