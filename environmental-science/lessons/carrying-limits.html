Carrying Capacity

Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support over time.

Practice 2: Visual RepresentationsPractice 5: Data Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1944, the US Coast Guard released 29 reindeer on St. Matthew Island in the Bering Sea, where there were no wolves and thick mats of lichen to eat. By 1963 there were about 6,000. Three years later, after a hard winter, fewer than 50 were left. The island never had food for 6,000 reindeer for long. The herd had grown past what the island could support, and it paid the price.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A population has plenty of food, water and space→births outnumber deaths and the population grows
  2. The population grows larger than its environment can support over time (overshoot)→individuals use resources faster than they are replaced, for example eating plants faster than they regrow
  3. Resources run short and are damaged→starvation, disease and fewer births make the population crash (dieback)
  4. The damaged resources recover slowly→the carrying capacity can stay lower than before for many years

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Carrying capacity (K) is the largest population of a species that an environment can support over time, given its food, water, space and other resources.
  • When a population grows past its carrying capacity, it is in overshoot: it uses resources faster than they are replaced.
  • Overshoot leads to dieback: a sharp drop in population as individuals starve, sicken or fail to reproduce.
  • Overshoot can damage the resources themselves (plants eaten down to bare ground, soil washed away), so carrying capacity can be lower after a dieback than before.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Carrying capacity is a fixed number that a population can never go above.

What actually happens: A population can go above carrying capacity for a while (overshoot), but it cannot stay there. Carrying capacity can also change when the environment changes, for example after a drought or after plants are grazed down.

3. After the dieback, the herd levels off near 750, below the original carrying capacity of 1,000. Which explanation fits best?

  1. The deer that survived were too old to have young
  2. Heavy grazing hurt the plants, lowering K
  3. Carrying capacity rises after a dieback
  4. Deer prefer to keep their herd smaller after a crash
Show the answer

Overshoot can lower carrying capacity by damaging the resources.

  • The deer that survived were too old to have young: The herd grows from 380 to about 750, so the deer are reproducing.
  • Correct: Heavy grazing hurt the plants, lowering K: Right: overshoot damaged the resources, so the island now supports fewer deer.
  • Carrying capacity rises after a dieback: The herd levels off lower than before, not higher.
  • Deer prefer to keep their herd smaller after a crash: Population size is set by resources, not by preference.

4. Which management action would most likely have prevented the overshoot shown in the figure?

  2. Removing the island’s food plants to slow growth
  3. Removing some deer once the herd neared 1,000

Show the answer

Keeping a population at or below carrying capacity prevents overshoot and dieback.

  • Adding more deer to the island in year 10: More deer would make the overshoot worse.
  • Removing the island’s food plants to slow growth: Removing food lowers carrying capacity and causes starvation.
  • Correct: Removing some deer once the herd neared 1,000: Right: keeping the herd near the carrying capacity avoids damage to the plants and the crash.
  • Waiting until year 20 to count the deer again: By year 20 the dieback had already happened.

Passage

The reindeer of St. Matthew Island

In 1944, 29 reindeer were released on St. Matthew Island, a remote island in the Bering Sea with no wolves or other large predators. The island’s lichens, the reindeer’s main winter food, had built up over centuries into thick mats. By 1963 the herd had grown to about 6,000 animals, and biologists found the lichen mats grazed down and trampled over much of the island. During the severe winter of 1963 to 1964, most of the reindeer starved. A survey in 1966 found 42 animals, and the herd later died out. Lichens grow only a few millimeters a year, so the island’s winter food would take decades to recover.

Invented passage for practice, summarizing published accounts of the St. Matthew Island reindeer, studied by the biologist David Klein (US Fish and Wildlife Service and University of Alaska).

5. Which assumption is the passage making when it calls the 6,000 reindeer an overshoot?

  1. The island could not supply that much lichen year after year
  2. The reindeer would have kept growing without the hard winter
  3. The first 29 reindeer were already too many for the island
  4. Reindeer herds tend to crash after about 20 years
Show the answer

Overshoot means resources are being used faster than they are replaced.

  • Correct: The island could not supply that much lichen year after year: Right: overshoot means using food faster than it grows back, which the grazed-down lichen shows.
  • The reindeer would have kept growing without the hard winter: The passage does not assume unlimited growth; it describes damaged food.
  • The first 29 reindeer were already too many for the island: The herd grew for years, so 29 was well below carrying capacity.
  • Reindeer herds tend to crash after about 20 years: The passage is about this island’s food supply, not a fixed time span.

6. Which statement best defines carrying capacity?

  1. The largest number of offspring one female can have
  2. The number of different species an area can hold
  3. The largest population an environment can support over time
  4. The total mass of food in an ecosystem at one time
Show the answer

Carrying capacity: the largest population supportable over time.

  • The largest number of offspring one female can have: That is about reproduction, not the environment.
  • The number of different species an area can hold: That is species richness.
  • Correct: The largest population an environment can support over time: Right: it is set by food, water, space and other resources.
  • The total mass of food in an ecosystem at one time: Carrying capacity is a number of individuals, not a mass of food.

7. A wildlife refuge feeds a deer herd extra hay every winter for 20 years, and the herd grows to twice its earlier size. The feeding then stops. Which outcome is most likely?

  1. A dieback, as the herd is far above the natural carrying capacity
  2. No change, because the deer are used to the area
  3. Faster growth, because the deer are well fed
  4. The herd stays at its new size for good
Show the answer

Remove an added resource and carrying capacity drops back.

  • Correct: A dieback, as the herd is far above the natural carrying capacity: Right: hay raised the carrying capacity; without it the herd is in overshoot.
  • No change, because the deer are used to the area: Being used to an area does not provide food.
  • Faster growth, because the deer are well fed: Once the extra food stops, there is less food per deer, not more.
  • The herd stays at its new size for good: The natural food supply cannot support twice the earlier herd.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support over time. A population that grows past it is in overshoot and uses resources faster than they are replaced; resource shortages then cause dieback, a sharp population crash. Because overshoot can damage the resources, carrying capacity may be lower afterward than before.
