Unit 2 · Topic 2.7

Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the series of changes in a community after a disruption.

Practice 1: Concept ExplanationPractice 5: Data Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

When a lava flow cools on Hawaii, it leaves black rock with no soil and no life. Within a few years, lichens and ferns appear in the cracks. A farm field abandoned in New England tells a faster story: weeds the first summer, shrubs within a decade, a young forest within a few decades. Both are ecological succession; what differs is whether soil was there to start with.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A disruption leaves bare rock (primary) or bare soil (secondary)→pioneer species, which grow fast and spread easily, arrive first
  2. Pioneers change the site: they help make soil, add nutrients and give shade→conditions become suitable for larger plants that could not grow there before
  3. Larger, longer-lived plants arrive and outcompete the pioneers→the community changes in stages, and species richness and biomass increase
  4. Many years pass without a major disruption→a mature, more stable community (climax community) develops

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Ecological succession is the gradual change in the species of a community after a disruption, in a series of stages.
  • Primary succession starts where there is no soil: new lava rock, rock left by a retreating glacier. Soil must form first, so it is slow (often centuries).
  • Secondary succession starts where soil remains after a disruption such as a fire, a flood, a hurricane or an abandoned farm field. With soil and seeds already present, it is faster (often decades).
  • Pioneer species (lichens and mosses on rock; grasses and weeds on soil) arrive first. Over time biodiversity and biomass increase, and a mature, more stable climax community develops.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Succession after a forest fire is primary succession, because almost everything was burned.

What actually happens: A fire leaves soil, and usually seeds and roots in it, so regrowth after a fire is secondary succession. Primary succession needs a start with no soil at all, such as new rock.

Passage

Life returns to a volcano

In May 1980, Mount St. Helens in Washington State erupted. The blast flattened 600 square kilometers of forest, and a fan of hot rock and ash on the volcano’s north side, later named the Pumice Plain, buried the ground under many meters of fresh rock. On the Pumice Plain, nothing survived; a few years later only scattered plants had arrived. Farther out, where the blast knocked trees down but left soil, roots and buried seeds, plants sprouted the first summer, and pocket gophers dug fresh soil up to the surface. Ecologists who have studied both areas since 1980 report that life returned much faster where soil remained than on the rock of the Pumice Plain.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Ecological succession is the series of changes in a community after a disruption. Primary succession begins on bare rock with no soil, and pioneer species such as lichens and mosses help form soil; it is slow. Secondary succession begins where soil remains, after a fire, flood or abandoned farm; it is faster. Over the stages, biodiversity and biomass increase until a mature, more stable climax community develops.
