Soil Formation and Erosion

Soil forms slowly from weathered parent material and humus from decomposed organisms, and it sorts into horizons: O, A (topsoil), B (subsoil), C and bedrock.

Practice 2: Visual RepresentationsPractice 1: Concept Explanation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A shovel of good farm soil holds more living things than there are people on Earth, and the dark top layer that feeds our crops took centuries to build. In the 1930s, after farmers plowed up the prairie grass of the southern Great Plains, a few dry, windy years carried that layer away in clouds of dust. Soil forms slowly and can disappear fast.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Weathering breaks parent material (bedrock or deposited sediment) into small mineral particles→the mineral part of soil forms
  2. Dead plants and animals are broken down by decomposers→humus forms and is mixed into the top of the soil
  3. Water soaking down carries minerals and fine particles from the top layers→the soil sorts itself into horizons: O, A, B, C
  4. Plants are removed and the soil is left bare→wind and water carry topsoil away, far faster than new soil forms

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Soil forms from parent material (bedrock, or sediment carried by rivers, glaciers or wind) broken down by weathering, plus humus from decomposed organisms.
  • Climate, organisms, the shape of the land, the parent material and time control how fast soil forms. Warm, wet places weather rock and decompose matter fastest.
  • A soil profile has layers called horizons: O (organic matter), A (topsoil: humus and minerals), B (subsoil: minerals leached from above), C (partly weathered parent material), then bedrock.
  • Erosion is the removal and transport of soil by wind and water. Plant roots hold soil and plant cover shields it, so bare soil erodes fastest.
  • Eroded soil fills streams with sediment, carries nutrients and pesticides with it, and leaves farms with poorer subsoil.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Soil keeps forming, so losing some to erosion is quickly made up.

What actually happens: New soil does form, but only over centuries: a few centimeters can take hundreds of years to form, but a bare field can lose that much in a few years.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. Which two processes form soil?

  1. Weathering of parent material and the decay of organic matter
  2. Erosion of topsoil and the deposit of salt by floodwater
  3. Photosynthesis in roots and evaporation from the soil surface
  4. Compaction by heavy machines and the melting of bedrock
Show the answer

Soil forms where rock is broken down into mineral particles by weathering and dead plants and animals decay into humus, mixing over long periods.

  • Correct: Weathering of parent material and the decay of organic matter: Right: weathered rock supplies minerals, and decayed organisms supply humus.
  • Erosion of topsoil and the deposit of salt by floodwater: Erosion removes soil; it does not form it where it starts.
  • Photosynthesis in roots and evaporation from the soil surface: Roots do not photosynthesize, and evaporation does not make soil.
  • Compaction by heavy machines and the melting of bedrock: Compaction damages soil, and bedrock is not melted at the surface.

2. Why does wind remove more soil from a field after it is plowed than from the same field covered in grass?

  1. Plowed soil is loose and bare, with no roots to hold it
  2. Plowing adds organic matter that is light enough for the wind to lift
  3. Grass makes the wind blow faster over a field than over bare soil
  4. Plowing moves the C horizon to the surface, which is lighter than topsoil
Show the answer

Roots bind soil particles together and leaves and stalks shield the surface. Plowing breaks up the soil and leaves it bare, so wind can lift it.

  • Correct: Plowed soil is loose and bare, with no roots to hold it: Right: bare, loose soil has nothing holding it down.
  • Plowing adds organic matter that is light enough for the wind to lift: Plowing turns plant matter under; it does not make the soil lighter.
  • Grass makes the wind blow faster over a field than over bare soil: Grass slows the wind at the ground; it does not speed it up.
  • Plowing moves the C horizon to the surface, which is lighter than topsoil: Ordinary plowing turns the topsoil, not the deep C horizon.

3. In the soil profile, which horizon is topsoil, with the most humus mixed into the minerals and most of the roots?

  1. The A horizon
  2. The B horizon
  3. The C horizon
  4. The O horizon
Show the answer

The A horizon is topsoil: humus mixed with mineral particles. It is dark and full of roots, worms and microbes.

  • Correct: The A horizon: Right: topsoil is the A horizon.
  • The B horizon: The B horizon is subsoil, where minerals leached from above collect.
  • The C horizon: The C horizon is partly weathered parent material with little humus.
  • The O horizon: The O horizon is mostly undecayed leaves and organic matter above the mineral soil.

4. The diagram says minerals leached from above collect in the B horizon. What carries them down?

  1. Water soaking down through the soil
  2. Roots pulling minerals down from the surface
  3. Wind blowing minerals into cracks in the soil
  4. Heat rising from the bedrock below the soil
Show the answer

Rain soaks into the soil and dissolves or carries fine particles and minerals downward. They build up in the subsoil: this is leaching.

  • Correct: Water soaking down through the soil: Right: water moving down leaches minerals into the B horizon.
  • Roots pulling minerals down from the surface: Roots take minerals up from the soil, the opposite direction.
  • Wind blowing minerals into cracks in the soil: Wind moves surface soil; it does not move minerals down through the profile.
  • Heat rising from the bedrock below the soil: Heat from bedrock does not move minerals in a soil profile.

6. Which conclusion do the data support?

  1. More plant cover goes with less soil loss on this slope
  2. Growing a crop doubles soil loss compared with leaving soil bare
  3. Plowing makes little difference to soil loss on this slope
  4. Native grass loses more soil than any field with a crop
Show the answer

The more cover the ground keeps, the less soil it loses: grass loses 0.4, stalks 2.5, a crop on bare soil 12.0 and bare soil 30.0 metric tons per hectare.

  • Correct: More plant cover goes with less soil loss on this slope: Right: every step toward more cover lowers soil loss.
  • Growing a crop doubles soil loss compared with leaving soil bare: Bare soil with no crop (30.0) loses more than a crop on plowed soil (12.0), not less.
  • Plowing makes little difference to soil loss on this slope: The plowed plots lose far more soil than the unplowed grass.
  • Native grass loses more soil than any field with a crop: Native grass loses the least, 0.4 metric tons per hectare.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Soil forms slowly from weathered parent material and humus from decomposed organisms, and it sorts into horizons: O, A (topsoil), B (subsoil), C and bedrock. Warm, wet climates form soil fastest. Wind and water erode soil, fastest where plants are removed and the ground is bare, and eroded soil harms both the land it leaves and the streams it reaches.
