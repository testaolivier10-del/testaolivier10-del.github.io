Unit 5 · Topic 5.5

Irrigation Methods

Irrigation methods differ in how much of the water reaches the roots: flood and furrow are cheap but lose much of it to evaporation and runoff, spray loses less, and drip loses the least but costs the most.

Practice 6: Mathematical RoutinesPractice 7: Environmental Solutions

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Two farms grow the same crop on the same kind of soil. One floods its fields from a ditch; the other runs thin tubes along each row and lets water drip onto the roots. At harvest the crops look alike, but the first farm used about twice as much water. Farms use about 70% of the fresh water people take from rivers, lakes and aquifers, so the method matters.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A field is flooded with more water than the crop can use→much of the water evaporates from the surface or soaks below the roots
  2. Irrigation water carries small amounts of dissolved salt and the water evaporates→salt is left behind in the topsoil
  3. Salt builds up over years (salinization)→roots struggle to take up water, so yields fall
  4. Wells pump an aquifer faster than it recharges→the water table drops and shallow wells go dry

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • About 70% of the fresh water people withdraw worldwide goes to agriculture, most of it for irrigation.
  • Drip is the most efficient (only about 5% of the water lost) and spray comes next (about a quarter or less lost). Flood and furrow are cheap surface methods that lose the most (furrow often about a third). Drip also costs the most to install.
  • Overirrigation causes waterlogging (roots without oxygen) and salinization (salt left behind when water evaporates).
  • Pumping groundwater faster than it recharges causes aquifer depletion: a falling water table, a cone of depression around wells, and dry shallow wells.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Salinization happens when farmers irrigate with seawater.

What actually happens: Fresh river and well water carries small amounts of dissolved salt. Each time irrigation water evaporates from a field it leaves that salt behind, and over years it builds up in the topsoil.

Check yourself

1. Which irrigation method delivers water slowly through tubes to the base of each plant?

  1. Drip irrigation
  2. Furrow irrigation
  3. Flood irrigation
  4. Spray irrigation
Show the answer

Drip irrigation runs tubes with small holes along the rows, so water goes straight to the roots.

  • Correct: Drip irrigation: Right: drip puts water at the roots.
  • Furrow irrigation: Furrow irrigation runs water down trenches between rows.
  • Flood irrigation: Flood irrigation covers the whole field.
  • Spray irrigation: Spray irrigation sprinkles water over the crop from above.

2. Why is drip irrigation more efficient than furrow irrigation?

  1. Water goes to the roots, so less evaporates from the surface or runs off the field
  2. Drip water contains more nutrients, so the plants need less of it to grow
  3. Drip tubes cool the soil around the roots, so plants take up water at a slower rate
  4. Drip systems pump water faster, so it reaches the roots before evaporating
Show the answer

Efficiency is the share of water applied that the crop uses. Drip loses little to evaporation and runoff because it wets only the soil around each plant.

  • Correct: Water goes to the roots, so less evaporates from the surface or runs off the field: Right: little water is lost before it reaches the roots.
  • Drip water contains more nutrients, so the plants need less of it to grow: The water is the same; the difference is where it goes.
  • Drip tubes cool the soil around the roots, so plants take up water at a slower rate: Drip does not work by cooling the soil.
  • Drip systems pump water faster, so it reaches the roots before evaporating: Drip delivers water slowly, not fast.

7. Using the cost column, which statement best explains why many farmers keep using flood or furrow irrigation?

  1. They cost far less to install, so farmers who pay little for water save money

Show the answer

Flood costs 150 dollars per ha to install and drip 3,200. Where water is cheap, the water savings may not pay back the equipment.

  • Correct: They cost far less to install, so farmers who pay little for water save money: Right: low cost to install is their main advantage.
  • They waste less water than the others, so farmers save on both water and equipment: They waste more water, not less.
  • They let crops take up more water, so the crops grow faster than with drip: The crop took up 4,800 m³ under flood and furrow, about the same as under the other methods.
  • They use the newest equipment, so they are easier to repair than drip: Flood and furrow use simple ditches, not new equipment.
