Unit 5 · Topic 5.16

Aquaculture

Aquaculture, raising fish and shellfish in ponds, tanks and net pens, supplies about half of the fish people eat and can ease pressure on wild stocks with high yields on little space.

Practice 7: Environmental Solutions

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Half of the fish and shrimp people eat today never swam in the wild. They grew in ponds in Asia, in tanks on land, or in net pens floating in Norwegian fjords and Chilean bays. Fish farming took pressure off wild fisheries just as many of them were collapsing. It also brought its own problems: waste under the pens, escaped fish, disease, and wild fish ground up to feed farmed ones.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Many fish are crowded in an open net pen→their waste and uneaten feed fall to the sea floor
  2. Nutrients from the waste build up under and around the pen→decomposers use oxygen, so the water near the bottom loses oxygen
  3. Crowding spreads parasites such as sea lice and disease→wild fish swimming past the pens can be infected
  4. Farmed fish escape through torn nets→they compete or breed with wild fish and can weaken wild populations

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Aquaculture raises fish, shellfish and seaweed in ponds, tanks or net pens. It now supplies about half of the fish and shellfish people eat.
  • Benefits: a lot of food from a small area, less pressure on wild fish stocks, little fuel compared with fishing fleets, and fish that turn feed into body mass efficiently.
  • Costs: concentrated waste, disease and parasites that spread to wild fish, antibiotics, escaped fish, and wild fish caught to make feed for carnivorous farmed species. Shrimp ponds have replaced mangrove forests.
  • Lower-impact options: closed tanks with filters, farming plant-eating fish (tilapia, carp) and filter feeders (mussels, oysters) that need no feed.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Farming fish always takes pressure off wild fish.

What actually happens: Carnivorous farmed fish such as salmon are fed fish meal and fish oil made from wild fish, so farming them still takes wild fish. Farming plant eaters like carp and tilapia, or filter feeders like mussels, takes much less.

1. Which is an example of aquaculture?

  1. Raising salmon in net pens in a bay
  2. Catching tuna with longlines at sea
  3. Trawling the sea floor for shrimp
  4. Fishing for trout with a rod in a river
Show the answer

Aquaculture is farming fish in controlled conditions; the others catch wild fish.

  • Correct: Raising salmon in net pens in a bay: Right: farmed fish in pens.
  • Catching tuna with longlines at sea: Longlining catches wild fish.
  • Trawling the sea floor for shrimp: Trawling catches wild shrimp.
  • Fishing for trout with a rod in a river: Sport fishing catches wild fish.

2. Which is a benefit of aquaculture compared with wild fishing?

  1. It grows a lot of food on little space
  2. It removes the need to give the fish any feed
  3. It is unable to spread disease to any wild fish
  4. It produces no waste in the water
Show the answer

Farms grow fish densely and can reduce demand on wild fisheries.

  • Correct: It grows a lot of food on little space: Right: high yield on little space and less wild catch.
  • It removes the need to give the fish any feed: Many farmed fish are fed.
  • It is unable to spread disease to any wild fish: Open pens can spread disease to wild fish.
  • It produces no waste in the water: Farmed fish produce waste.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Aquaculture, raising fish and shellfish in ponds, tanks and net pens, supplies about half of the fish people eat and can ease pressure on wild stocks with high yields on little space. Open pens release concentrated waste, spread disease and parasites to wild fish, use antibiotics and lose escaped fish, and carnivorous species are fed wild fish. Closed tanks and plant-eating or filter-feeding species reduce these costs.
