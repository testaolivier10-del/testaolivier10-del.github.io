Invasive Species

Invasive species are nonnative species that spread quickly and cause harm.

Practice 1: Concept ExplanationPractice 7: Environmental Solutions

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In the late 1980s, a cargo ship from Europe emptied its ballast tanks into Lake St. Clair, between Lake Huron and Lake Erie. In the water were the larvae of a small striped mussel. Within a decade zebra mussels had spread through the Great Lakes, clogging water intake pipes and smothering native mussels.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. People move a species outside its native range, by ship, trade or on purpose→it arrives where it has few predators, parasites or diseases
  2. Generalist, r-selected traits let it eat many foods and reproduce fast→its population grows explosively
  3. It outcompetes, eats or smothers native species→native populations decline and biodiversity falls
  4. Once established it is very hard to remove→prevention and early detection are the best control

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Invasive species are nonnative (exotic) species that spread quickly and cause harm. Not every nonnative species is invasive.
  • They are often generalists with r-selected traits. Lacking natural predators is a feature of the new ecosystem, not a trait.
  • Examples: zebra mussels (ballast water, Great Lakes), kudzu (planted for erosion control), cane toads (brought to Australia for pest control), Asian carp, emerald ash borer.
  • Control: prevention (ballast rules, boat cleaning, inspections), early detection, removal, and carefully tested biological control.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: An invasive species succeeds because its trait is "no predators."

What actually happens: Having few predators describes the new habitat. A trait belongs to the organism: a broad diet, fast reproduction, tolerance of many conditions.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. Which traits help a nonnative species become invasive?

  1. A narrow diet and slow reproduction
  2. A broad diet and fast reproduction
  3. A need for one kind of habitat and few offspring
  4. Large body size and a long time to maturity
Show the answer

Invasive species are usually generalists with r-selected traits: they tolerate many conditions and reproduce quickly.

  • A narrow diet and slow reproduction: Those are specialist, K-selected traits, which make spreading hard.
  • Correct: A broad diet and fast reproduction: Right: generalists find food almost anywhere, and many offspring let numbers explode.
  • A need for one kind of habitat and few offspring: A narrow habitat need and few young limit spread.
  • Large body size and a long time to maturity: Slow maturity limits how fast a population can grow.

2. Describe how the invasive mussel population changed over the 10 years.

  1. Steady, even growth each year
  2. Fast growth, then a crash to zero
  3. A steady decline from year 0 to year 10
  4. Slow, then explosive, then level
Show the answer

The invader’s curve is S-shaped: slow start, rapid growth, then leveling off as it nears its carrying capacity.

  • Steady, even growth each year: Growth was very uneven: small at first, then huge.
  • Fast growth, then a crash to zero: The population leveled off; it did not crash.
  • A steady decline from year 0 to year 10: The invader rose; the native mussel declined.
  • Correct: Slow, then explosive, then level: Right: under 30 per m² for 3 years, a jump to 1,200 by year 7, then near 1,400.

5. Which explanation best fits the decline of the native mussel?

  1. The native mussel died of old age
  2. The lake became too cold for mussels
  3. The invader outcompeted it for food and space
  4. The native mussel moved to a new lake
Show the answer

An invader crowding out a native for the same resources is interspecific competition; invasive mussels also attach directly to native mussels’ shells.

  • The native mussel died of old age: A whole population does not die of old age in a few years.
  • The lake became too cold for mussels: The invader thrived, so the lake still suited mussels.
  • Correct: The invader outcompeted it for food and space: Right: the native fell as the invader rose, the pattern of competition.
  • The native mussel moved to a new lake: Mussels attached to the bottom do not migrate between lakes.

6. Zebra mussels from Europe arrived in the Great Lakes in the late 1980s. How did they most likely get there?

  1. By swimming across the Atlantic Ocean
  2. On the feet of migrating birds from Europe
  3. In ballast water released by cargo ships
  4. Released as pets by aquarium owners
Show the answer

Ballast water is a major pathway; rules now require ships to exchange or treat it before entering the Great Lakes.

  • By swimming across the Atlantic Ocean: Zebra mussels cannot cross an ocean on their own.
  • On the feet of migrating birds from Europe: This is not how they reached the Great Lakes.
  • Correct: In ballast water released by cargo ships: Right: ships took on water in Europe and released it, with mussel larvae, in the Great Lakes.
  • Released as pets by aquarium owners: Ballast water, not the pet trade, is the accepted route.

7. Propose a solution, other than using chemicals, to stop zebra mussels from spreading to a new lake used by boaters.

Build the answer part by part, then write it in your own words.

Show the answer

Respect "other than": a non-chemical prevention step fitted to how the invader travels.

  • An answer that earns the point: Require boats to be cleaned, drained and dried at the boat ramp so that mussels and larvae are removed before boats enter the lake, which prevents the invader from reaching the new lake because boats are how it moves between lakes.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Invasive species are nonnative species that spread quickly and cause harm. They are often generalists with r-selected traits that face few predators in their new home. They outcompete natives and are very hard to remove once established. Zebra mussels, kudzu and cane toads are classic cases. Prevention, such as ballast water rules and boat cleaning, and early detection are the best tools.
