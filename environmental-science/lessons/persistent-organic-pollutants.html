Unit 8 · Topic 8.7

Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs)

Persistent organic pollutants are synthetic, carbon-based chemicals such as DDT, PCBs and dioxins that resist breakdown, dissolve in fat, travel long distances on wind and water and stay toxic for decades.

Practice 1: Concept ExplanationPractice 5: Data Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Inuit communities in the Canadian Arctic live thousands of kilometers from any farm or factory. Yet tests in the 1980s and 1990s found some of the highest levels of certain pesticides and industrial chemicals in the world in their blood and breast milk. The chemicals had traveled north on the wind and up the food chain into the seals and whales people ate.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A synthetic, carbon-based chemical is made and used (a pesticide, an insulator)→it enters soil, water and air
  2. Its chemical bonds resist breakdown by sunlight, water and microbes→it persists for years to decades
  3. It evaporates in warm places and condenses in cold ones→it travels long distances and collects in the Arctic
  4. It dissolves in fat rather than water→animals store it in fatty tissue instead of excreting it
  5. It stays toxic while stored→it harms reproduction, development and the immune system, as DDT did to birds of prey

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • A persistent organic pollutant (POP) is a synthetic, carbon-based chemical that resists breakdown, dissolves in fat, travels long distances and is toxic.
  • Examples: DDT and other old pesticides, PCBs from electrical equipment, and dioxins from burning plastics and some industrial processes.
  • POPs collect in cold regions far from where they were used, and they are stored in fat, so they build up in animals over time.
  • Harm: thin eggshells (DDT in birds of prey), reproductive and immune problems, some cancers. The Stockholm Convention (2001) restricts them worldwide.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Once a chemical is banned, it soon disappears from the environment.

What actually happens: A POP breaks down so slowly that it stays in soil, sediment and animal fat for decades after it is banned, falling only gradually.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What makes a pollutant a persistent organic pollutant (POP)?

  1. It lasts for years and is stored in fat
  2. It is a metal that dissolves easily in water
  3. It is a gas that breaks down in hours in sunlight
  4. It is a nutrient that feeds algae
Show the answer

POPs are carbon-based synthetic chemicals that resist breaking down, dissolve in fat rather than water, travel long distances, and are toxic.

  • Correct: It lasts for years and is stored in fat: Right: those features define a POP.
  • It is a metal that dissolves easily in water: POPs are organic (carbon-based), and they dissolve in fat, not water.
  • It is a gas that breaks down in hours in sunlight: POPs last for years or decades.
  • It is a nutrient that feeds algae: That describes nitrate or phosphate.

2. Which of these is a persistent organic pollutant?

  1. Nitrate from fertilizer
  2. Carbon monoxide from car exhaust
  3. Radon from rock under homes
  4. PCBs from old electrical gear
Show the answer

DDT, PCBs and dioxins are classic POPs, now restricted worldwide under the Stockholm Convention.

  • Nitrate from fertilizer: Nitrate is a nutrient, not a POP.
  • Carbon monoxide from car exhaust: CO is an air pollutant gas that does not persist this way.
  • Radon from rock under homes: Radon is a radioactive gas, not an organic chemical.
  • Correct: PCBs from old electrical gear: Right: PCBs are a classic POP.

4. The pesticide was never used within 2,000 km of the seals. How did it reach them?

  1. It moved north by air and water
  2. Arctic plants make the pesticide naturally
  3. Seals swim 2,000 km to farms to feed
  4. It formed in the seals from carbon dioxide in the air
Show the answer

POPs evaporate in warm places, travel in the air and ocean currents, and condense in cold regions, so they collect in the Arctic far from where they were used.

  • Correct: It moved north by air and water: Right: long-range transport carries POPs toward the poles.
  • Arctic plants make the pesticide naturally: The pesticide is synthetic; plants do not make it.
  • Seals swim 2,000 km to farms to feed: Seals do not feed on farmland; the chemical traveled to them.
  • It formed in the seals from carbon dioxide in the air: Animals do not make pesticides from CO2.

6. What does the slow decline after the ban show about this chemical?

  1. The ban had no effect on the pesticide
  2. It breaks down in a few weeks once it reaches the Arctic
  3. Seals produce more of it as they age
  4. It breaks down slowly, lasting decades
Show the answer

Even with no new use, the pesticide breaks down slowly and stays in food webs and fat for decades: it is persistent.

  • The ban had no effect on the pesticide: Levels fell after the ban, so it did have an effect.
  • It breaks down in a few weeks once it reaches the Arctic: If so, levels would drop to near zero quickly.
  • Seals produce more of it as they age: Seals do not make the pesticide.
  • Correct: It breaks down slowly, lasting decades: Right: that is what persistent means.

7. Why are birds of prey such as bald eagles and peregrine falcons linked to the story of DDT?

  1. DDT killed the fish the birds ate, so they starved
  2. DDT caused thin eggshells that broke
  3. DDT made the birds grow too large to fly
  4. DDT attracted birds to farms, where they were shot
Show the answer

DDT and its breakdown products interfered with calcium use in birds, so they laid thin-shelled eggs that broke during incubation. Populations recovered after the 1972 United States ban.

  • DDT killed the fish the birds ate, so they starved: The key effect was on eggshells, not food supply.
  • Correct: DDT caused thin eggshells that broke: Right: they recovered after DDT was banned.
  • DDT made the birds grow too large to fly: DDT did not change body size this way.
  • DDT attracted birds to farms, where they were shot: The decline came from failed eggs.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Persistent organic pollutants are synthetic, carbon-based chemicals such as DDT, PCBs and dioxins that resist breakdown, dissolve in fat, travel long distances on wind and water and stay toxic for decades. They collect in cold regions and in animal fat, harming reproduction and development; DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey. Bans such as the Stockholm Convention cut new inputs, but levels fall slowly.
