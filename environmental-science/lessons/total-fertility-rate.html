Unit 3 · Topic 3.7

Total Fertility Rate

The total fertility rate is the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime.

Practice 1: Concept ExplanationPractice 5: Data Analysis

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1960, women in many countries had six or seven children on average. Today, in most of those same countries, the average is under three, and in some it is under two. The single number that tracks this change is the total fertility rate, and whether it sits above or below about 2.1 tells you a lot about where a population is headed.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Girls and women get more years of schooling and more chances to work for pay→they tend to marry and have children later, and choose to have fewer
  2. Infant mortality falls, and family planning becomes easier to get→parents no longer need many births to be sure some children survive, and they can choose how many to have
  3. The total fertility rate falls toward or below replacement level, about 2.1→population growth slows, and in time the population can stop growing or start to shrink

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The total fertility rate (TFR) is the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at a population’s current birth patterns.
  • Replacement-level fertility is the TFR at which each generation just replaces itself: about 2.1 in countries where few children die young. It is a little above 2 because some children die before adulthood and slightly more boys than girls are born.
  • Infant mortality is the number of babies who die before their first birthday, per 1,000 live births. Where it is high, families tend to have more children; as it falls, TFR tends to fall too.
  • Factors that lower TFR: more education and paid work for women, access to family planning, lower infant mortality, people moving to cities, pensions for old age. Factors that raise it: early marriage, children working on family farms, religious and cultural values that favor large families.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A TFR of 2.0 means the population is growing slowly, because 2 is more than 0.

What actually happens: Two children replace two parents, but not quite: some children die before having their own, so a population needs about 2.1 to replace itself. Over the long run, a TFR of 2.0 means a slowly shrinking population.

3. Country A’s TFR in 2020 is 2.8. What does this predict for its population, if nothing else changes?

  1. It will shrink, because the TFR has been falling
  2. It keeps growing: 2.8 is above 2.1
  3. It holds steady, since 2.8 is close to 2
  4. It will double each year from now on
Show the answer

Compare TFR with about 2.1 to predict long-run growth or decline.

  • It will shrink, because the TFR has been falling: A falling TFR still above 2.1 means slower growth, not decline.
  • Correct: It keeps growing: 2.8 is above 2.1: Right: each generation more than replaces itself.
  • It holds steady, since 2.8 is close to 2: Replacement is about 2.1; 2.8 is well above it.
  • It will double each year from now on: A TFR of 2.8 means growth, but nowhere near doubling each year.

4. Country B has been below replacement since about 1990. Which long-run problem is it most likely to face?

  1. Too few schools for a growing number of children
  2. Rapid population growth that outruns its food
  3. Fewer workers and a larger share of older people
  4. A sharp rise in infant mortality
Show the answer

Low fertility over decades shrinks young cohorts and ages the population.

  • Too few schools for a growing number of children: Below-replacement fertility means fewer children, not more.
  • Rapid population growth that outruns its food: A TFR of 1.5 means the population will tend to shrink.
  • Correct: Fewer workers and a larger share of older people: Right: fewer births mean smaller young cohorts, so in time fewer workers support more retirees.
  • A sharp rise in infant mortality: A low TFR does not cause infant mortality to rise.

7. A country wants to lower its high total fertility rate without forcing anyone. Which policy is most likely to work, based on this topic?

  2. Paying families a bonus for each new child
  3. Lowering the legal age of marriage
  4. Closing clinics that care for infants
Show the answer

Expanding education and choice lowers fertility without force.

  • Correct: Free schooling for girls, and clinics: Right: both lower TFR by expanding choice, and both have worked in many countries.
  • Paying families a bonus for each new child: Payments per child tend to raise fertility.
  • Lowering the legal age of marriage: Earlier marriage tends to raise fertility.
  • Closing clinics that care for infants: Higher infant mortality tends to raise TFR, and it harms children.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The total fertility rate is the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime. Replacement level is about 2.1, a little above 2 because some children die young. Education and paid work for women, family planning, lower infant mortality and urban living lower TFR; where children work on farms and support parents in old age, TFR tends to stay high. A TFR below replacement means, in the long run, a shrinking population.
