Unit 3 · Topic 3.1

Generalist and Specialist Species

Specialist species have narrow niches: a few foods, one habitat or a narrow range of conditions.

Practice 1: Concept Explanation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A giant panda spends most of its day eating bamboo, which makes up nearly all of its diet. A raccoon a few continents away eats fruit, frogs, insects, eggs and whatever is in a city trash can. When a bamboo forest flowers and dies back, which happens every few decades, the panda is in trouble. When a forest is cleared for a suburb, the raccoon often does better than before.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A specialist depends on a narrow set of foods, habitats or conditions→it can be very efficient where those resources are plentiful
  2. The environment changes and its one key resource declines→the specialist has nothing to switch to, so its population falls
  3. A generalist uses many foods, habitats and conditions→when one resource declines it switches to others, so it copes better with change

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Specialist species have a narrow niche: they eat a few foods, live in one kind of habitat, or tolerate a narrow range of conditions. Examples: the giant panda (bamboo) and the koala (eucalyptus leaves).
  • Generalist species have a broad niche: they eat many foods, live in many habitats and tolerate a wide range of conditions. Examples: raccoons, coyotes, rats and cockroaches.
  • Specialists do well in stable environments where their resource is reliable. They are more likely to decline or die out when the environment changes.
  • Generalists cope better with change, and they often spread into new places, including towns and farms.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Generalists are always better off than specialists.

What actually happens: Specialists are often more efficient at using their one resource, so they can outcompete generalists where that resource is plentiful and stable. Generalists win when conditions change.

3. Which explanation best accounts for the change in woodpecker W?

  1. Bird X ate most of the woodpecker’s eggs after clearing
  2. Clearing removed the large dead trees it needs for both food and nests
  3. The woodpecker moved to the new houses and was not counted
  4. The woodpecker began eating seeds and scraps instead
Show the answer

A specialist declines when the one resource it depends on is removed.

  • Bird X ate most of the woodpecker’s eggs after clearing: Nothing in the data shows predation by bird X.
  • Correct: Clearing removed the large dead trees it needs for both food and nests: Right: as a specialist, W had no other food or nest site to switch to.
  • The woodpecker moved to the new houses and was not counted: W nests only in holes in large dead trees, not in buildings.
  • The woodpecker began eating seeds and scraps instead: If it had switched foods, it would have done better, not worse.

5. Where 6 mm seeds are plentiful year after year, the specialist is more common than the generalist. Which explanation fits best?

  1. It handles 6 mm seeds more efficiently than the generalist
  2. The generalist is unable to eat 6 mm seeds
  3. Specialists have more offspring in each generation
  4. Generalists avoid areas where seeds are common
Show the answer

Specialists can win where conditions are stable and their resource is abundant.

  • Correct: It handles 6 mm seeds more efficiently than the generalist: Right: specializing often means being better at one task, which pays off when that resource is reliable.
  • The generalist is unable to eat 6 mm seeds: The generalist’s curve includes 6 mm food.
  • Specialists have more offspring in each generation: The figure shows food use, not number of offspring.
  • Generalists avoid areas where seeds are common: Nothing in the figure suggests that.

6. Why are specialist species more likely than generalists to decline when a habitat changes?

  1. They reproduce faster and use up their food
  2. They are smaller and weaker than most generalists
  3. They rely on few resources, with little to switch to
  4. They are eaten by generalists in disturbed areas
Show the answer

A narrow niche means fewer options when conditions change.

  • They reproduce faster and use up their food: How fast they reproduce is not what makes a species a specialist.
  • They are smaller and weaker than most generalists: Body size does not define a specialist; pandas are large.
  • Correct: They rely on few resources, with little to switch to: Right: if their resource declines, they have no fallback.
  • They are eaten by generalists in disturbed areas: Predation can happen, but the main reason is their narrow niche.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Specialist species have narrow niches: a few foods, one habitat or a narrow range of conditions. They thrive where their resource is stable but decline when the environment changes. Generalist species have broad niches and can switch foods and habitats, so they cope better with change and often spread into new and disturbed places.
