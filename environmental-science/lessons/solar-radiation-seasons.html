Unit 4 · Topic 4.7

Solar Radiation and Earth's Seasons

Insolation, the solar energy per square meter, is highest near the equator because sunlight strikes it most directly.

Practice 2: Visual RepresentationsPractice 1: Concept Explanation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Earth is about 5 million kilometers closer to the Sun in early January than in early July, yet January is the middle of winter in New York. Meanwhile, in Sydney, Australia, people spend January at the beach. If distance does not make the seasons, something else must, and it also explains why the tropics are warm and the poles frozen.

Part 4 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Earth is a sphere, so sunlight strikes the equator head-on and high latitudes at a slant→insolation, the solar energy per square meter, is highest near the equator
  2. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5° and keeps pointing the same way as Earth orbits→each hemisphere leans toward the Sun for half the year and away for the other half
  3. The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets more direct sunlight and longer days→it has summer, while the other hemisphere has winter
  4. Dark surfaces absorb more sunlight than bright ones (lower albedo)→dark ocean and forest warm more than snow and ice under the same sun

Part 5 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Insolation is the solar radiation reaching an area, in watts per square meter (W/m²). It is greatest near the equator, where sunlight is most direct.
  • Seasons come from axial tilt (about 23.5°): the hemisphere tilted toward the Sun has higher sun angles and longer days. Distance from the Sun is not the cause; Earth is closest in early January.
  • Northern summer is southern winter, and the reverse. Near the equator, insolation stays high all year, so seasons are mostly wet and dry, not warm and cold.
  • Albedo is the share of sunlight a surface reflects: high for snow and ice, low for ocean and forest. Low-albedo surfaces absorb more energy and warm more.
  • Insolation sets temperature by latitude, which helps place the biomes and drives the global winds.

Part 6 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: It is summer when Earth is closest to the Sun.

What actually happens: Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter. The seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis, which is also why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.

Part 7 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What causes Earth's seasons?

  1. The tilt of Earth's axis
  2. Earth's changing distance from the Sun
  3. The Sun giving off more energy in summer
  4. Changes in how fast Earth spins
Show the answer

Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5°. As Earth orbits, each hemisphere takes turns leaning toward the Sun, getting more direct sunlight and longer days.

  • Correct: The tilt of Earth's axis: Right: the tilt makes each hemisphere lean toward or away from the Sun.
  • Earth's changing distance from the Sun: Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter, so distance is not the cause.
  • The Sun giving off more energy in summer: The Sun's output barely changes over a year.
  • Changes in how fast Earth spins: Earth spins at a steady rate; that makes day and night, not seasons.

2. Why does the equator receive more solar energy per square meter than the poles over a year?

  1. Sunlight strikes it more directly, so it is more concentrated
  2. The equator is much closer to the Sun than the poles are
  3. The poles have more clouds, which block most of the sunlight
  4. The equator has more hours of daylight on each day of the year
Show the answer

A beam of light that hits the surface head-on lights a small area; the same beam at a low angle spreads over a larger area, so each square meter gets less.

  • Correct: Sunlight strikes it more directly, so it is more concentrated: Right: the angle of the sunlight decides how concentrated it is.
  • The equator is much closer to the Sun than the poles are: The difference in distance is tiny compared with 150 million km.
  • The poles have more clouds, which block most of the sunlight: Clouds matter locally, but the main cause is the angle of sunlight.
  • The equator has more hours of daylight on each day of the year: The equator has about 12 hours of daylight all year; the poles have long summer days, so day length is not the main cause.

3. What is albedo?

  1. The share of incoming sunlight a surface reflects
  2. The amount of heat a surface gives off at night
  3. The angle at which sunlight strikes the ground
  4. The amount of sunlight a plant uses for photosynthesis
Show the answer

Albedo is reflectivity: fresh snow reflects most sunlight (high albedo); dark ocean water absorbs most of it (low albedo).

  • Correct: The share of incoming sunlight a surface reflects: Right: albedo is the fraction or percent of sunlight reflected.
  • The amount of heat a surface gives off at night: Heat given off is a separate process from reflecting sunlight.
  • The angle at which sunlight strikes the ground: The angle of sunlight is a different idea; it sets insolation.
  • The amount of sunlight a plant uses for photosynthesis: Photosynthesis uses a small share of light; albedo is about reflection.

Part 8 · Summary

Summary

Insolation, the solar energy per square meter, is highest near the equator because sunlight strikes it most directly. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5°, so as Earth orbits, each hemisphere takes turns leaning toward the Sun: that tilt, not distance, makes the seasons. Albedo, the share of sunlight a surface reflects, decides how much of that energy the surface absorbs.
