Plate Tectonics

Convection in the mantle moves Earth's plates.

Practice 2: Visual Representations

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 2011 the seafloor off Japan jumped up several meters in a few minutes. The water above it rose too, and a tsunami crossed the coast. In Iceland, at the same time, the ground was slowly pulling apart and filling with new rock. Both places sit on the edges of Earth's moving plates, and the kind of edge decides what happens there.

Part 4 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Heat from deep inside Earth warms mantle rock, which rises, cools and sinks→slow convection currents form in the mantle
  2. Convection currents drag the rigid plates above them→plates move a few centimeters a year, apart, together or past each other
  3. Plates pull apart→magma rises and forms new crust: mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys
  4. A dense ocean plate meets a lighter plate and sinks→a trench, strong earthquakes and a chain of volcanoes form
  5. Plates grind past each other and then suddenly slip→earthquakes shake the land, and a slip under the sea can launch a tsunami

Part 5 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Earth's outer layer is broken into rigid tectonic plates that ride on the hotter, slowly flowing mantle, moved by convection currents.
  • Divergent boundary: plates move apart; magma forms new crust (mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, seafloor spreading).
  • Convergent boundary: plates move together. Ocean meets plate: the denser ocean plate sinks (subduction), making a trench, earthquakes and volcanoes. Continent meets continent: crust crumples into high mountains.
  • Transform boundary: plates slide past each other; earthquakes, little volcanic activity (the San Andreas Fault).
  • A hot spot is a fixed plume of magma under a plate; the plate moving over it leaves a chain of volcanoes that get older in one direction (Hawaii).

Part 6 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Volcanoes and earthquakes happen at random places on Earth.

What actually happens: Most cluster along plate boundaries, especially subduction zones such as the ring around the Pacific. Hot spots are the main exception, and even they make a predictable chain.

Part 7 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. The Himalayas are still rising where the plate carrying India pushes into the plate carrying most of Asia. Which kind of boundary is this?

  1. A convergent boundary between two continental plates
  2. A divergent boundary where two plates spread apart
  3. A transform boundary where plates slide side by side
  4. A hot spot under the middle of one plate
Show the answer

Two continents collide. Continental crust is too light to sink far, so the crust crumples and stacks up into very high mountains.

  • Correct: A convergent boundary between two continental plates: Right: the plates move toward each other, and the crust folds into mountains.
  • A divergent boundary where two plates spread apart: Spreading plates form ridges and rift valleys, not the highest mountains on Earth.
  • A transform boundary where plates slide side by side: At a transform boundary the plates slide past each other; they do not push crust upward this much.
  • A hot spot under the middle of one plate: A hot spot forms volcanoes in the middle of a plate, not a long mountain range at a plate edge.

2. The San Andreas Fault in California causes frequent earthquakes but has few volcanoes. Which kind of plate boundary is it?

  1. Transform
  2. Divergent
  3. Convergent, with subduction
  4. Convergent, between two continents
Show the answer

At a transform boundary the plates grind past each other. Stress builds and is released as earthquakes, but no plate sinks and little magma rises.

  • Correct: Transform: Right: the Pacific Plate slides past the North American Plate.
  • Divergent: A divergent boundary would let magma rise and form new crust, with volcanic activity.
  • Convergent, with subduction: Subduction makes trenches and chains of volcanoes, which this fault does not have.
  • Convergent, between two continents: A collision of continents builds high mountain ranges, not a long sliding fault.

3. An ocean plate sinks under a continental plate. Which features form at this boundary? Select all that apply.

  1. A deep ocean trench
  2. A chain of volcanoes on the continent
  3. A rift valley that splits the continent
  4. A mid-ocean ridge with new seafloor
Show the answer

Where the denser ocean plate sinks, the seafloor bends down into a trench, and the melting rock above the sinking plate feeds volcanoes, as in the Andes.

  • Correct: A deep ocean trench: Right: the sinking plate forms a trench.
  • Correct: A chain of volcanoes on the continent: Right: rock above the sinking plate melts, and the magma rises to form volcanoes.
  • A rift valley that splits the continent: A rift valley forms where plates pull apart, at a divergent boundary.
  • A mid-ocean ridge with new seafloor: A mid-ocean ridge forms where ocean plates move apart, not where they collide.

5. According to the diagram, why does the ocean plate, and not the continent, sink at the convergent boundary?

  1. Ocean crust is denser than continental crust
  2. Ocean crust is thicker than continental crust
  3. The ocean pushes the plate down with its weight
  4. The continent is moving faster than the ocean plate
Show the answer

Ocean crust is made of dense basalt; continental crust is lighter. When they meet, the denser plate sinks into the mantle.

  • Correct: Ocean crust is denser than continental crust: Right: the denser plate sinks below the lighter one.
  • Ocean crust is thicker than continental crust: Ocean crust is thinner than continental crust, not thicker.
  • The ocean pushes the plate down with its weight: The water above is not what makes the plate sink; the rock is denser.
  • The continent is moving faster than the ocean plate: Speed does not decide which plate sinks; density does.

6. A city is built on the continent shown in the diagram, near the trench. Which natural hazards is it most likely to face?

  1. Strong earthquakes and volcanic eruptions
  2. Rift valleys opening under the city each year
  3. Hurricanes caused by the sinking plate
  4. Volcanic eruptions but no earthquakes
Show the answer

A subduction zone produces some of the strongest earthquakes on Earth, and the melting plate feeds explosive volcanoes, as along the Andes and in Japan.

  • Correct: Strong earthquakes and volcanic eruptions: Right: subduction brings both strong earthquakes and volcanoes.
  • Rift valleys opening under the city each year: Rift valleys form where plates pull apart, not where they collide.
  • Hurricanes caused by the sinking plate: Hurricanes form over warm ocean water; plate motion does not cause them.
  • Volcanic eruptions but no earthquakes: The grinding and sudden slipping of the sinking plate causes frequent earthquakes too.

Part 8 · Summary

Summary

Convection in the mantle moves Earth's plates. Where plates spread apart (divergent), new crust forms; where they collide (convergent), a dense ocean plate sinks, making trenches, earthquakes and volcanoes, or continents crumple into mountains; where they slide past each other (transform), earthquakes strike. Hot spots build volcanic chains in the middle of plates. Knowing the boundary tells you the hazards a place faces.
