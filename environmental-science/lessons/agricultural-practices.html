Unit 5 · Topic 5.4 Beta

Impacts of Agricultural Practices

Tilling, slash-and-burn and heavy fertilizer use each raise harvests in the short run and harm the land or water in the long run.

Practice 7: Environmental Solutions

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In the 1930s, farmers on the southern Great Plains had plowed up millions of hectares of grassland. Then came years of drought. With no grass roots to hold it, the loose, dry topsoil blew away in black clouds that buried fences and reached cities on the Atlantic coast. The Dust Bowl showed how a farming practice can turn a dry spell into a disaster.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A field is plowed (tilled) every year→the soil surface is bare and loose
  2. Wind and rain hit bare, loose soil→topsoil blows or washes away (erosion)
  3. Mixing air into the soil speeds decomposition→soil organic matter falls and its carbon enters the air as carbon dioxide
  4. Topsoil and organic matter are lost→the soil holds less water and fewer nutrients, so yields fall

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Tilling buries weeds and loosens the seedbed, but leaves soil bare, so wind and water erode it; it also speeds the loss of organic matter.
  • Slash-and-burn clears tropical forest by cutting and burning. Ash fertilizes the poor soil for a few seasons, then the plot is left and more forest is cleared.
  • Burning releases carbon dioxide and smoke; clearing removes habitat and carbon storage. With long fallow periods and few people, plots can regrow forest.
  • Fertilizer the crop does not take up runs off or leaches into rivers, lakes and groundwater, where nitrate and phosphate become pollutants.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Plowing improves the soil, because loose soil is healthy soil.

What actually happens: Plowing loosens the soil for planting, but year after year it leaves the surface bare, speeds erosion, burns off organic matter and can pack a hard layer just below the plow depth.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What is tilling?

  1. Turning over and breaking up the soil before planting
  2. Adding crushed limestone to a field to raise the soil pH
  3. Planting rows of trees along the edges of a field
  4. Spreading animal manure on a field after harvest
Show the answer

Tilling (plowing or disking) turns over and loosens the soil to bury weeds and prepare a seedbed.

  • Correct: Turning over and breaking up the soil before planting: Right: tilling is mechanical turning of the soil.
  • Adding crushed limestone to a field to raise the soil pH: Liming changes soil chemistry; it is not tilling.
  • Planting rows of trees along the edges of a field: Tree rows protect a field from wind; they are not tilling.
  • Spreading animal manure on a field after harvest: Spreading manure adds nutrients; it does not turn the soil.

2. In slash-and-burn farming, what provides the nutrients for the first crops?

  1. The ash left after the cut plants are burned
  2. Synthetic fertilizer bought before clearing the plot
  3. Deep, rich soil built up under the forest
  4. Manure from cattle grazing in the forest
Show the answer

Burning releases the nutrients stored in the plants as ash, which fertilizes the poor soil for a short time.

  • Correct: The ash left after the cut plants are burned: Right: ash carries the nutrients from the burned plants.
  • Synthetic fertilizer bought before clearing the plot: Slash-and-burn farmers usually do not buy fertilizer.
  • Deep, rich soil built up under the forest: Tropical rainforest soils are thin and poor; most nutrients are in the plants.
  • Manure from cattle grazing in the forest: The method does not rely on grazing cattle.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Tilling, slash-and-burn and heavy fertilizer use each raise harvests in the short run and harm the land or water in the long run. Tilling bares the soil to erosion and loss of organic matter; slash-and-burn removes forest and releases carbon dioxide; fertilizer the crop does not use pollutes rivers, lakes and groundwater.
