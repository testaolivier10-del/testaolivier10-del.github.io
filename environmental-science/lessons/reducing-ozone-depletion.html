Reducing Ozone Depletion

The Montreal Protocol of 1987 phased out CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances worldwide.

Practice 7: Environmental Solutions

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

In 1987, countries that disagreed on almost everything signed a treaty to stop making the chemicals that were destroying the ozone layer. Today every member of the United Nations has signed it, production of CFCs has stopped and the ozone hole is starting to heal. It is the clearest example of the world fixing a global environmental problem.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Scientists linked CFCs to ozone loss and found the Antarctic ozone hole→nations signed the Montreal Protocol in 1987
  2. The treaty phased out CFCs and halons, with more time and help for developing countries→production of ozone-depleting substances fell by more than 99%
  3. Replacements such as HCFCs, then HFCs and hydrocarbons, took their place→far less chlorine reaches the stratosphere
  4. Long-lived CFCs already in the air slowly break down→ozone is recovering and should return to 1980 levels around 2066 over Antarctica

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The Montreal Protocol (1987) is the treaty that phased out CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances. Every UN member has ratified it.
  • HCFCs were a temporary replacement: less ozone-depleting, but they still contain chlorine, so they are being phased out too.
  • HFCs contain no chlorine and do not harm ozone, but many trap heat thousands of times more strongly than CO₂. The Kigali Amendment (2016) phases them down.
  • Other fixes: hydrocarbons such as isobutane, ammonia and CO₂ as refrigerants, and capturing refrigerant from old equipment.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Since CFC production ended in 2010, the ozone hole should already be gone.

What actually happens: CFCs stay in the atmosphere for 50 to 100 years or more, so chlorine already up there keeps destroying ozone. Recovery is under way but should take until about the 2060s over Antarctica.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What did the Montreal Protocol, signed in 1987, do?

  1. It phased out CFCs and other ozone-depleting chemicals
  2. It set limits on carbon dioxide emissions from power plants
  3. It banned the trade of endangered animals and their parts
  4. It required cars to use catalytic converters
Show the answer

The Montreal Protocol is an international treaty that phased out the production of CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances.

  • Correct: It phased out CFCs and other ozone-depleting chemicals: Right: it is the treaty that ended most production of ozone-depleting chemicals.
  • It set limits on carbon dioxide emissions from power plants: Carbon dioxide limits are the subject of climate agreements, not the Montreal Protocol.
  • It banned the trade of endangered animals and their parts: That describes a wildlife trade treaty, not the Montreal Protocol.
  • It required cars to use catalytic converters: Catalytic converters were required by national air pollution laws, not this treaty.

2. HFCs replaced many CFCs and HCFCs in air conditioners. Which statement describes a drawback of HFCs?

  1. They destroy ozone faster than the CFCs they replaced
  2. They do not work as refrigerants in air conditioners
  3. They spare ozone but trap far more heat than CO₂
  4. They are banned by the original 1987 Montreal Protocol
Show the answer

Solving one problem created another: HFCs protect ozone but warm the climate, so the 2016 Kigali Amendment phases them down.

  • They destroy ozone faster than the CFCs they replaced: HFCs contain no chlorine or bromine, so they do not destroy ozone.
  • They do not work as refrigerants in air conditioners: HFCs work well as refrigerants; that is why they spread.
  • Correct: They spare ozone but trap far more heat than CO₂: Right: HFCs contain no chlorine, but many are powerful heat-trapping gases.
  • They are banned by the original 1987 Montreal Protocol: HFCs were added later, by the Kigali Amendment of 2016.

Developed countries stopped by 1996 and developing countries had until 2010: 2010−1987=232010 - 1987 = 23 years.

4. Why does the timeline show recovery of Antarctic ozone around 2066, decades after CFC production ended?

  1. CFCs already released last for decades
  2. Countries are still allowed to make CFCs until 2066
  3. The ozone layer needs new CFCs to rebuild itself
  4. UV light will stop reaching the stratosphere in 2066
Show the answer

Recovery lags production cuts because CFCs are long-lived.

  • Correct: CFCs already released last for decades: Right: CFCs last 50 to 100 years or more, so their chlorine keeps destroying ozone.
  • Countries are still allowed to make CFCs until 2066: Production ended worldwide by 2010.
  • The ozone layer needs new CFCs to rebuild itself: CFCs destroy ozone; they do not rebuild it.
  • UV light will stop reaching the stratosphere in 2066: UV keeps reaching the stratosphere; that is how ozone forms.

6. What does the timeline suggest about why the Montreal Protocol is seen as a success?

  1. Ozone recovered fully within a year of the treaty
  2. CFCs were found to be harmless after the treaty
  3. Quick action, and ozone is on track to recover
  4. The treaty was signed before anyone had measured ozone loss
Show the answer

From the 1974 warning to the 1987 treaty took 13 years; with every UN member taking part, ozone-depleting substances fell sharply and recovery is under way.

  • Ozone recovered fully within a year of the treaty: Recovery is expected to take until about 2066.
  • CFCs were found to be harmless after the treaty: CFCs do destroy ozone; that is why they were phased out.
  • Correct: Quick action, and ozone is on track to recover: Right: quick, worldwide action, then measured recovery.
  • The treaty was signed before anyone had measured ozone loss: The ozone hole was reported in 1985, two years before the treaty.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The Montreal Protocol of 1987 phased out CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances worldwide. HCFCs were a stopgap that still contains chlorine; HFCs spare ozone but trap heat, so the Kigali Amendment phases them down. Hydrocarbon and other refrigerants and capture of old refrigerant help too. Ozone is recovering slowly because CFCs last for decades.
