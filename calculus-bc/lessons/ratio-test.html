Unit 10 · Topic 10.8

The Ratio Test

The ratio test compares a series with a geometric one.

Practice 1: Implementing Mathematical ProcessesPractice 3: JustificationPractice 4: Communication and Notation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A geometric series converges when each term is a fixed fraction of the one before. Many series are not geometric, but far out their terms behave as if they were: each term is close to a fixed fraction LL of the one before. The ratio test finds that LL and compares it with 1.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Compute |an+1an|\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| and its limit LL→far out, each term is about LL times the one before, as in a geometric series with ratio LL
  2. L<1L < 1→the terms shrink at least as fast as a convergent geometric series, so the series converges
  3. L>1L > 1 (or LL is infinite)→the terms grow, do not approach 0, and the series diverges
  4. L=1L = 1→the test decides nothing: use another test

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Ratio test: LL=limn→∞|an+1an|= \lim_{n \to \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|. L<1L < 1: converges. L>1L > 1 or infinite: diverges. L=1L = 1: no conclusion.
  • Put an+1a_{n+1} on top and simplify before taking the limit: (n+1)!n!=n+1\frac{(n+1)!}{n!} = n + 1, 3n+13n=3\frac{3^{n+1}}{3^n} = 3.
  • Use it for factorials and exponentials. For pp-series and ratios of polynomials the limit is 1, so compare instead.
  • Only the limit matters; early ratios above or below 1 change nothing.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: limn→∞|an+1an|\lim_{n \to \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|=1= 1 for ∑1n2\sum \frac{1}{n^2}, so the series diverges.

What actually happens: A limit of 1 decides nothing. ∑1n2\sum \frac{1}{n^2} converges as a pp-series with p=2p = 2, while ∑1n\sum \frac{1}{n}, with the same limit, diverges.

2. What does the ratio test conclude about ∑n=1∞n23n\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n^2}{3^n}?

  1. It diverges, since the first ratio, 43\frac{4}{3}, is greater than 1
  2. Nothing, since some of the ratios are above 1
  3. It converges to 13\frac{1}{3}, the limit of the ratios
  4. It converges, since the limit 13\frac{1}{3} is less than 1
Show the answer

The ratio test limit is L=13<1L = \frac{1}{3} < 1, so the series converges. The limit says whether it converges, not what it converges to.

  • It diverges, since the first ratio, 43\frac{4}{3}, is greater than 1: Only the limit decides; one early ratio above 1 changes nothing.
  • Nothing, since some of the ratios are above 1: The test uses the limit, not every ratio; the limit 13\frac{1}{3} is below 1.
  • It converges to 13\frac{1}{3}, the limit of the ratios: The limit of the ratios decides convergence; it is not the sum.
  • Correct: It converges, since the limit 13\frac{1}{3} is less than 1: Right: a ratio test limit below 1 gives convergence.

3. What does the ratio test conclude about ∑n=1∞n!10n\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n!}{10^n}?

  1. It converges: the first ratios are below 1
  2. It diverges: n+110\frac{n+1}{10} grows without bound
  3. Nothing: the ratio test limit is not a number
  4. It converges: n+110\frac{n+1}{10} is 1 at n=9n = 9
Show the answer

limn→∞n+110\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n + 1}{10}=∞= \infty. A ratio test limit greater than 1, including an infinite one, means the series diverges.

  • It converges: the first ratios are below 1: Only the limit decides, and the ratios grow past every bound.
  • Correct: It diverges: n+110\frac{n+1}{10} grows without bound: Right: a limit of infinity counts as greater than 1.
  • Nothing: the ratio test limit is not a number: An infinite limit is a conclusion: the series diverges.
  • It converges: n+110\frac{n+1}{10} is 1 at n=9n = 9: A ratio of exactly 1 at one nn means nothing; the limit is infinite.

4. A student notes that the ratios for n=1n = 1 to 88 are all below 1 and concludes that the series converges. What is wrong?

  1. The ratio test uses the limit as n→∞n \to \infty, which is infinite
  2. Nothing: ratios below 1 mean the terms shrink, so the series converges
  3. The ratios should be compared with 0, not with 1
  4. The ratio test needs each ratio to be below 12\frac{1}{2}
Show the answer

The ratio test looks at limn→∞|an+1an|\lim_{n \to \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|. Here the limit is infinite, so the series diverges. Early ratios below 1 only say the first terms shrink.

  • Correct: The ratio test uses the limit as n→∞n \to \infty, which is infinite: Right: finitely many ratios never decide the test.
  • Nothing: ratios below 1 mean the terms shrink, so the series converges: The terms shrink only until n=9n = 9; after that they grow without bound.
  • The ratios should be compared with 0, not with 1: The threshold is 1: a limit below 1 converges, above 1 diverges.
  • The ratio test needs each ratio to be below 12\frac{1}{2}: No ratio needs to be below 12\frac{1}{2}; the limit decides.

5. For both ∑1n\sum \frac{1}{n} and ∑1n2\sum \frac{1}{n^2} the ratio test limit is 1. What does that show?

  1. Both series converge, since their ratio test limits are equal
  2. A limit of 1 decides nothing: the two series differ
  3. Both series diverge, since their ratio test limits equal 1
  4. The ratio test was done wrong, since its limit is less than 1
Show the answer

nn+1→1\frac{n}{n + 1} \to 1 and n2(n+1)2→1\frac{n^2}{(n + 1)^2} \to 1, yet ∑1n\sum \frac{1}{n} diverges and ∑1n2\sum \frac{1}{n^2} converges. A ratio test limit of 1 decides nothing; use another test (here the pp-series test).

  • Both series converge, since their ratio test limits are equal: ∑1n\sum \frac{1}{n} diverges, so equal limits do not mean equal behavior.
  • Correct: A limit of 1 decides nothing: the two series differ: Right: the two series behave differently with the same limit.
  • Both series diverge, since their ratio test limits equal 1: ∑1n2\sum \frac{1}{n^2} converges (p=2p = 2), so a limit of 1 does not mean divergence.
  • The ratio test was done wrong, since its limit is less than 1: nn+1→1\frac{n}{n+1} \to 1 and n2(n+1)2→1\frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2} \to 1: both limits are 1.

6. What does the ratio test conclude about ∑n=1∞2nn5\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2^n}{n^5}?

  1. It converges: the limit is 12\frac{1}{2}
  2. It diverges: the limit is 2
  3. Nothing: the limit is 1
  4. It converges: n5n^5 is large
Show the answer

an+1an\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=2n+1(n+1)5⋅n52n= \frac{2^{n+1}}{(n+1)^5} \cdot \frac{n^5}{2^n}=2(nn+1)5→2>1= 2\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^5 \to 2 > 1, so the series diverges.

  • It converges: the limit is 12\frac{1}{2}: That inverts the ratio; an+1an\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=2⋅n5(n+1)5→2= 2 \cdot \frac{n^5}{(n+1)^5} \to 2.
  • Correct: It diverges: the limit is 2: Right: 2>12 > 1.
  • Nothing: the limit is 1: n5(n+1)5→1\frac{n^5}{(n+1)^5} \to 1, but the factor 2 stays: the limit is 2.
  • It converges: n5n^5 is large: 2n2^n eventually outgrows n5n^5; the limit 2 shows it.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The ratio test compares a series with a geometric one. Find LL=limn→∞|an+1an|= \lim_{n \to \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|: if L<1L < 1 the series converges, if L>1L > 1 or LL is infinite it diverges, and if L=1L = 1 the test gives no conclusion. It works best on factorials and powers like 3n3^n.
