Unit 9 · Topic 9.5

Integrating Vector-Valued Functions

To integrate a vector-valued function, integrate each component; an indefinite integral carries a constant vector C .

Practice 1: Implementing Mathematical ProcessesPractice 3: Justification

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A ship's navigator without GPS knows where the ship started and logs its velocity east and north every hour. From those two facts alone she can say where the ship is now: start plus the accumulated change in each direction. That is dead reckoning, and it is exactly how you integrate a velocity vector.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The velocity vector is ⟨x′(t),y′(t)⟩\left\langle x'(t), y'(t) \right\rangle→antidifferentiate each component, adding a constant vector C=⟨C1,C2⟩\mathbf{C} = \left\langle C_1, C_2 \right\rangle
  2. An initial position r(0)\mathbf{r}(0) is known→it fixes C\mathbf{C} and picks out the one path the particle follows
  3. The integral of a rate over an interval is the net change→r(b)\mathbf{r}(b)=r(a)= \mathbf{r}(a)+∫abv(t)dt+ \int_a^b \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt, one component at a time
  4. The velocity is known only from a table→approximate each component's integral with a Riemann or trapezoidal sum, then add the start

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Integrate a vector one component at a time: ∫⟨f(t),g(t)⟩dt\int \left\langle f(t), g(t) \right\rangle\,dt=⟨F(t),G(t)⟩= \left\langle F(t), G(t) \right\rangle+C+ \mathbf{C}.
  • Position from velocity always adds the start: x(b)x(b)=x(a)= x(a)+∫abx′(t)dt+ \int_a^b x'(t)\,dt and y(b)y(b)=y(a)= y(a)+∫aby′(t)dt+ \int_a^b y'(t)\,dt.
  • From acceleration, integrate twice: v(0)\mathbf{v}(0) fixes the first constant and r(0)\mathbf{r}(0) the second.
  • ∫aby′(t)dt\int_a^b y'(t)\,dt is the change in yy from t=at = a to t=bt = b, not y(b)y(b) and not a distance.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: r(0)=⟨2,5⟩\mathbf{r}(0) = \left\langle 2, 5 \right\rangle and ∫03v(t)dt\int_0^3 \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt=⟨4,−1⟩= \left\langle 4, -1 \right\rangle, so r(3)\mathbf{r}(3)=⟨4,−1⟩−⟨2,5⟩= \left\langle 4, -1 \right\rangle - \left\langle 2, 5 \right\rangle=⟨2,−6⟩= \left\langle 2, -6 \right\rangle.

What actually happens: The integral is the change in position, and a change is added to where you started: r(3)\mathbf{r}(3)=⟨2,5⟩+⟨4,−1⟩= \left\langle 2, 5 \right\rangle + \left\langle 4, -1 \right\rangle=⟨6,4⟩= \left\langle 6, 4 \right\rangle. Subtracting the start, or leaving it out, is the most common lost point on this topic.

3. What does ∫25y′(t)dt\int_2^5 y'(t)\,dt represent?

  1. The boat's yy-coordinate at t=5t = 5, in meters
  2. The change in the boat's yy-coordinate, in meters, from t=2t = 2 to t=5t = 5
  3. The boat's average vertical velocity, in meters per minute, from t=2t = 2 to t=5t = 5
  4. The distance, in meters, that the boat travels from t=2t = 2 to t=5t = 5
Show the answer

The integral of a rate over an interval is the net change of the quantity: ∫25y′(t)dt\int_2^5 y'(t)\,dt=y(5)−y(2)= y(5) - y(2), in meters.

  • The boat's yy-coordinate at t=5t = 5, in meters: To get y(5)y(5) you would add a known value, such as y(2)y(2), to the change.
  • Correct: The change in the boat's yy-coordinate, in meters, from t=2t = 2 to t=5t = 5: Right: y(5)−y(2)y(5) - y(2).
  • The boat's average vertical velocity, in meters per minute, from t=2t = 2 to t=5t = 5: An average would divide by 5−2=35 - 2 = 3 minutes.
  • The distance, in meters, that the boat travels from t=2t = 2 to t=5t = 5: Distance along the path uses the speed (x′)2+(y′)2\sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}, not y′y' alone.

6. Find ∫⟨e2t,1t⟩dt\int \left\langle e^{2t}, \frac{1}{t} \right\rangle\,dt.

  1. ⟨2e2t,−1t2⟩+C\left\langle 2e^{2t}, -\frac{1}{t^2} \right\rangle + \mathbf{C}
  2. ⟨12e2t,ln⁡|t|⟩+C\left\langle \frac{1}{2}e^{2t}, \ln|t| \right\rangle + \mathbf{C}
  3. ⟨e2t,ln⁡|t|⟩+C\left\langle e^{2t}, \ln|t| \right\rangle + \mathbf{C}
  4. ⟨12e2t,−1t2⟩+C\left\langle \frac{1}{2}e^{2t}, -\frac{1}{t^2} \right\rangle + \mathbf{C}
Show the answer

Antidifferentiate each component: ∫e2tdt=12e2t\int e^{2t}\,dt = \frac{1}{2}e^{2t} and ∫1tdt=ln⁡|t|\int \frac{1}{t}\,dt = \ln|t|. The constant is a vector C=⟨C1,C2⟩\mathbf{C} = \left\langle C_1, C_2 \right\rangle, one constant per component.

  • ⟨2e2t,−1t2⟩+C\left\langle 2e^{2t}, -\frac{1}{t^2} \right\rangle + \mathbf{C}: These are derivatives of the components; the integral reverses differentiation.
  • Correct: ⟨12e2t,ln⁡|t|⟩+C\left\langle \frac{1}{2}e^{2t}, \ln|t| \right\rangle + \mathbf{C}: Right: one antiderivative per component, plus a constant vector.

  1. ⟨5,−2⟩\left\langle 5, -2 \right\rangle
  2. ⟨6,−1⟩\left\langle 6, -1 \right\rangle
  3. ⟨4,−3⟩\left\langle 4, -3 \right\rangle
  4. ⟨1,1⟩\left\langle 1, 1 \right\rangle
Show the answer

The integral of velocity is the change in position: r(4)\mathbf{r}(4)=r(0)= \mathbf{r}(0)+∫04v(t)dt+ \int_0^4 \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt=⟨1+5,1−2⟩= \left\langle 1 + 5, 1 - 2 \right\rangle=⟨6,−1⟩= \left\langle 6, -1 \right\rangle.

  • ⟨5,−2⟩\left\langle 5, -2 \right\rangle: That is the change in position from t=0t = 0 to t=4t = 4; add it to the start.
  • Correct: ⟨6,−1⟩\left\langle 6, -1 \right\rangle: Right: start plus change.
  • ⟨4,−3⟩\left\langle 4, -3 \right\rangle: The start is added to the change, not subtracted from it.
  • ⟨1,1⟩\left\langle 1, 1 \right\rangle: That is where the particle started; the integral says it moved.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

To integrate a vector-valued function, integrate each component; an indefinite integral carries a constant vector C\mathbf{C}. An initial condition fixes it: the position at time bb is the starting position plus the integral of the velocity, r(b)\mathbf{r}(b)=r(a)= \mathbf{r}(a)+∫abv(t)dt+ \int_a^b \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt. From a table, approximate each component's integral with a sum, then add the start.
