Unit 10 · Topic 10.1

Convergent and Divergent Series

A series ∑ a_n converges to S when its partial sums S_n = a_1 + ⋯ + a_n approach S ; otherwise it diverges.

Practice 2: Connecting RepresentationsPractice 3: Justification

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Walk halfway to a wall, then half the remaining distance, then half again. Infinitely many steps, yet you never pass the wall: the distances add to a finite total. Other endless sums grow without bound. Deciding which, and naming the total when there is one, is what Unit 10 is about, and it all rests on one definition.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Add the first nn terms of ∑an\sum a_n→the partial sum SnS_n=a1+a2+⋯+an= a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n
  2. Do this for every nn→a new sequence, S1,S2,S3,…S_1, S_2, S_3, \ldots
  3. If limn→∞Sn=S\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n = S, a finite number→the series converges and its sum is SS
  4. If the partial sums grow without bound or never settle→the series diverges and has no sum

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • An infinite series ∑n=1∞an\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n converges to SS exactly when its partial sums SnS_n approach SS. Two sequences are in play: the terms ana_n and the partial sums SnS_n. Keep them apart.
  • From partial sums back to terms: a1=S1a_1 = S_1 and an=Sn−Sn−1a_n = S_n - S_{n-1} for n≥2n \ge 2.
  • Say \"the series converges\" or \"the series diverges\". The terms of a convergent series approach 0, not the sum.
  • Constants factor out of a convergent series; adding or removing finitely many terms changes the sum but never whether the series converges.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The terms of ∑ln⁡(n+1n)\sum \ln\left(\frac{n + 1}{n}\right) approach 0, so the series converges.

What actually happens: Its partial sums are ln⁡(n+1)\ln(n + 1), which grow without bound: the series diverges. Terms that shrink to 0 are not enough.

1. What is the sum of the series ∑n=1∞an\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n?

No calculator. Type the exact value (a fraction, π, e or a root is fine).

Show the answer

The sum of a series is the limit of its partial sums: limn→∞3nn+2\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n}{n + 2}=3= 3.

  • Answer: 33

2. Find a3a_3, the third term of the series.

No calculator. Type the exact value (a fraction, π, e or a root is fine).

Show the answer

a3a_3=S3−S2= S_3 - S_2=95−32= \frac{9}{5} - \frac{3}{2}=310= \frac{3}{10}: the third partial sum minus the second.

  • Answer: 310\frac{3}{10} ≈ 0.300

3. Which statement about this series is correct?

  1. The terms converge to 3, so the series converges
  2. The series diverges, since SnS_n keeps growing as nn grows
  3. The series converges to 3, and its terms approach 0
  4. The series converges to 3, and its terms approach 3
Show the answer

A series converges when its partial sums have a limit; here Sn→3S_n \to 3, so the series converges, to 3. Say it about the series: \"the series converges\", not \"the terms converge to 3\".

  • The terms converge to 3, so the series converges: The terms approach 0, not 3. It is the partial sums that approach 3.
  • The series diverges, since SnS_n keeps growing as nn grows: Partial sums can rise forever and still approach a limit; here they level off at 3.
  • Correct: The series converges to 3, and its terms approach 0: Right: the partial sums approach 3, and ana_n=6(n+1)(n+2)→0= \frac{6}{(n + 1)(n + 2)} \to 0.
  • The series converges to 3, and its terms approach 3: If every term were near 3, the partial sums would grow by about 3 each step and never level off.

4. Does the series ∑n=1∞(−1)n+1\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n+1} converge?

  1. Yes, to 0, since the even partial sums are 0
  2. Yes, to 1, since the first term is 1
  3. Yes, since each term is 1 or −1-1 and so stays bounded
  4. No: the partial sums jump between 1 and 0
Show the answer

SnS_n is 1 for odd nn and 0 for even nn. The sequence of partial sums has no limit, so the series diverges.

  • Yes, to 0, since the even partial sums are 0: The odd partial sums are 1; the whole sequence of partial sums has no limit.
  • Yes, to 1, since the first term is 1: The sum of a series is the limit of all its partial sums, not its first term.
  • Yes, since each term is 1 or −1-1 and so stays bounded: Bounded partial sums are not enough; they must approach a single number.
  • Correct: No: the partial sums jump between 1 and 0: Right: a series converges when its partial sums approach one number, and these do not.

5. The partial sums of ∑n=1∞ln⁡(n+1n)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ln\left(\frac{n + 1}{n}\right) are Sn=ln⁡(n+1)S_n = \ln(n + 1). What does this show?

  1. The series converges, since its terms approach 0
  2. The series converges to ln⁡2\ln 2, its first term
  3. The series diverges, though its terms approach 0
  4. The series converges to 0
Show the answer

SnS_n=ln⁡2−ln⁡1= \ln 2 - \ln 1+ln⁡3−ln⁡2+ \ln 3 - \ln 2+⋯+ \cdots=ln⁡(n+1)→∞= \ln(n + 1) \to \infty, so the series diverges, even though each term ln⁡(1+1n)\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right) approaches 0.

  • The series converges, since its terms approach 0: Terms approaching 0 do not make a series converge: these partial sums grow forever.
  • The series converges to ln⁡2\ln 2, its first term: ln⁡2\ln 2 is a1=S1a_1 = S_1. The sum would be limSn\lim S_n, which does not exist.
  • Correct: The series diverges, though its terms approach 0: Right: ln⁡(n+1)\ln(n + 1) grows without bound, while ln⁡(1+1n)→0\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right) \to 0.
  • The series converges to 0: The terms approach 0; the partial sums grow without bound.

6. For a series ∑n=1∞an\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n, what is the partial sum SnS_n?

  1. The nnth term, ana_n
  2. The sum of the first nn terms, a1+a2+⋯+ana_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n
  3. The sum of the terms after the nnth one
  4. The limit of ana_n as n→∞n \to \infty
Show the answer

SnS_n=∑k=1nak= \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k. The series converges to SS exactly when limn→∞Sn=S\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n = S.

  • The nnth term, ana_n: ana_n is one term; SnS_n adds the first nn of them.
  • Correct: The sum of the first nn terms, a1+a2+⋯+ana_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n: Right: the series converges when these partial sums have a limit.
  • The sum of the terms after the nnth one: That is what is left over, S−SnS - S_n, when the series converges.
  • The limit of ana_n as n→∞n \to \infty: That limit describes the terms, not the sum of the first nn.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

A series ∑an\sum a_n converges to SS when its partial sums Sn=a1+⋯+anS_n = a_1 + \cdots + a_n approach SS; otherwise it diverges. Find terms from partial sums by subtracting, keep the terms and the partial sums apart, and say what converges: the series.
