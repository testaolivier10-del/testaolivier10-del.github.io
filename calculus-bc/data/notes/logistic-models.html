An exponential model, $\frac{dP}{dt} = kP$, says the growth rate is proportional to the population, so the population grows without bound. That fits a small population with plenty of food and space, but not a crowded one. A logistic model adds a brake: as the population nears the most the environment can support, its growth slows to zero.

The logistic differential equation

In a logistic model the rate of change is jointly proportional to the population $P$ and to the room left, $K - P$:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = kP(K - P), \qquad k > 0.$$

The same model is often written $\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right)$, with $r = kK$. In both forms, $K$ is the carrying capacity: the positive value of $P$ at which the rate is 0. To read $K$, set the rate equal to 0 and take the positive solution.

Reading the model without solving it

The equilibrium solutions are $P = 0$ and $P = K$. The sign of $kP(K - P)$ tells what happens from any other starting value:

  • $0 < P(0) < K$: the rate is positive, so $P$ increases toward $K$, never reaching it.
  • $P(0) > K$: the factor $K - P$ is negative, so $P$ decreases toward $K$.
  • In both cases $\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = K$.

The questions on this topic ask you to interpret the equation: its carrying capacity, its limit, where growth is fastest, what a rate means in context. Solving the equation for a formula is not needed.

Worked example 1. Fish in a lake satisfy $\frac{dP}{dt} = 0.0006P(1200 - P)$, with $t$ in years and $P(0) = 200$. Find (a) $\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t)$, (b) the rate at $t = 0$, (c) the population when it grows fastest.

(a) The rate is 0 at $P = 1200$, and $0 < 200 < 1200$, so $\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = 1200$ fish.

(b) $\frac{dP}{dt} = 0.0006(200)(1000) = 120$ fish per year.

(c) Half the carrying capacity: 600 fish.

Why growth is fastest at half the carrying capacity

As a function of $P$, the rate $R(P) = kP(K - P) = kKP - kP^2$ is a downward parabola with zeros at 0 and $K$. Its derivative $R'(P) = kK - 2kP$ is 0 at $P = \frac{K}{2}$, where $R$ has its maximum. The figure shows both views: the population against time on the left, the rate against the population on the right.

The same fact gives the concavity of $P(t)$. By the chain rule,

$$\frac{d^2P}{dt^2} = k(K - 2P)\frac{dP}{dt}.$$

For a population rising from below $\frac{K}{2}$, $\frac{dP}{dt} > 0$, so the graph of $P$ is concave up while $P < \frac{K}{2}$ and concave down while $\frac{K}{2} < P < K$. The population at $\frac{K}{2}$ is a point of inflection: growth is fastest there.

Worked example 2. In a school of 5000 students, the number $R$ who have heard a rumor satisfies $\frac{dR}{dt} = 0.0002R(5000 - R)$, with $t$ in hours and $R(0) = 100$. When is the rumor spreading fastest, and how fast?

Fastest at $R = 2500$ students, half the carrying capacity. The rate then is $0.0002(2500)(2500) = 1250$ students per hour.

In context: when half the school has heard the rumor, it is spreading at 1250 students per hour, faster than at any other time.
