In 9.1 you found the slope of a parametric curve, $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$. The next question is how that slope changes as you move along the curve: the second derivative $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$, which tells you whether the curve bends up or down. It takes one idea, used twice.

The slope rule, applied to the slope

The rule from 9.1 works for any quantity that depends on $t$: its rate of change with respect to $x$ is its rate with respect to $t$, divided by $\frac{dx}{dt}$. The slope $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is such a quantity. So

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}, \qquad \frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0.$$

That gives a three-step method:

  1. Find $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$ and simplify it as a function of $t$.
  2. Differentiate that function with respect to $t$.
  3. Divide the result by $\frac{dx}{dt}$.

Worked example 1. A curve is given by $x = t^2$, $y = t^3 - 3t$ for $t > 0$. Find $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ and the concavity of the curve.

Step 1: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t} = \frac{3t}{2} - \frac{3}{2t}$. Splitting the fraction makes step 2 a power rule instead of a quotient rule.

Step 2: $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{3t}{2} - \frac{3}{2}t^{-1}\right) = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{3}{2t^2}$.

Step 3: divide by $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{3/2 + 3/(2t^2)}{2t} = \frac{3}{4t} + \frac{3}{4t^3} = \frac{3(t^2 + 1)}{4t^3}$.

For $t > 0$ every factor is positive, so the curve is concave up there. At $t = 1$ the value is $\frac{3 \cdot 2}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$.

Why the extra division matters

$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)$ measures how fast the slope changes per unit of time. Concavity is about how fast the slope changes per unit of $x$. Dividing by $\frac{dx}{dt}$ converts one into the other, and it can change the sign.

The figure shows the circle $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$. Its slope is $-\cot t$, and $\frac{d}{dt}(-\cot t) = \csc^2 t$, positive for every $t$. If you stopped there, you would call the whole circle concave up. Dividing by $\frac{dx}{dt} = -\sin t$ gives $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -\frac{1}{\sin^3 t}$: negative on the upper half, where $\sin t > 0$, and positive on the lower half. That matches the picture.

The other tempting shortcut, $\frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}$, is simply not the second derivative. For $x = e^t$, $y = e^{3t}$ it gives $\frac{9e^{3t}}{e^t} = 9e^{2t}$, but the curve is $y = x^3$, whose second derivative is $6x = 6e^t$. The three steps give the same $6e^t$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3e^{3t}}{e^t} = 3e^{2t}$, its $t$-derivative is $6e^{2t}$, and $\frac{6e^{2t}}{e^t} = 6e^t$.

Concavity, inflection points and the second derivative test

Once you have $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ as a function of $t$, Unit 5 applies:

FindingConclusion
$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} > 0$ at $t$The curve is concave up there
$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} < 0$ at $t$The curve is concave down there
$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ changes sign at $t_0$A point of inflection at $(x(t_0), y(t_0))$
$\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} > 0$ at $t_0$A relative minimum of $y$ there (second derivative test)
$\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} < 0$ at $t_0$A relative maximum of $y$ there

Worked example 2. At $t = 3$, a curve has $\frac{dx}{dt} = 6$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = -12$. What happens at that point?

$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{-12}{6} = -2 < 0$. The tangent is horizontal and the curve is concave down, so $y$ has a relative maximum at the point where $t = 3$.

Always report a point as $(x(t_0), y(t_0))$, not as $t_0$, and name what changes sign when you claim an inflection point: "$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ changes from negative to positive at $t = 2$."
