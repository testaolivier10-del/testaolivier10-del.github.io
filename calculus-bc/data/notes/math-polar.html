Rectangular coordinates say "go $x$ units right and $y$ units up". Polar coordinates say "turn to an angle, then go a distance". The 2025 exam's polar question had the lowest average of any question that year, and the readers' advice was simple: review the precalculus first. This page is that review.

Naming a point with $(r, \theta)$

Start at the pole (the origin). The angle $\theta$ is measured from the positive $x$-axis, counterclockwise, in radians. Turn to the ray at angle $\theta$, then go $r$ units along it. That point is $(r, \theta)$.

Two facts make polar different from rectangular:

  • $r$ can be negative. Then you go $|r|$ units backward, through the pole, onto the opposite ray. The figure shows $Q = (-2, \frac{\pi}{4})$, which sits in the third quadrant.
  • A point has many names. $(2, \frac{\pi}{4})$, $(2, \frac{9\pi}{4})$ and $(-2, \frac{5\pi}{4})$ are the same point. Adding $2\pi$ to $\theta$ changes nothing; adding $\pi$ and flipping the sign of $r$ changes nothing.

Converting between polar and rectangular

The segment from the pole to $(r, \theta)$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $x$ and $y$:

$$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}.$$

Worked example 1. Convert $(4, \frac{5\pi}{6})$ to rectangular, then convert $(0, -5)$ to polar.

$x = 4\cos\frac{5\pi}{6} = 4 \cdot \left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = -2\sqrt{3}$ and $y = 4\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 2$. The point is $(-2\sqrt{3}, 2)$.

For $(0, -5)$: $r = \sqrt{0 + 25} = 5$. The point is straight down, on the ray $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}$. So $(5, \frac{3\pi}{2})$, or equally $(-5, \frac{\pi}{2})$.

Do not trust $\tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}$ alone: $(1, -1)$ and $(-1, 1)$ have the same tangent. Always check the quadrant.

The same formulas convert equations. To rewrite $r = 6\cos\theta$, multiply by $r$: $r^2 = 6r\cos\theta$, so $x^2 + y^2 = 6x$, which is $(x - 3)^2 + y^2 = 9$: a circle of radius 3 through the pole.

Plotting a polar curve from a table

A polar curve $r = f(\theta)$ gives one distance for each angle. To sketch one, make a table, then sweep around like a lighthouse beam, marking each distance on its ray.

Worked example 2. Sketch $r = 1 + 2\cos\theta$ for $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$.

$\theta$$0$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$\pi$$\frac{4\pi}{3}$$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$2\pi$
$r$$3$$2$$1$$0$$-1$$0$$1$$3$

From $\theta = 0$ to $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ the distance shrinks from 3 to 0: a big arc into the pole. From $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ to $\frac{4\pi}{3}$, $r$ is negative, so the rays point left but the points land on the right: a small inner loop. From $\frac{4\pi}{3}$ to $2\pi$, $r$ grows back to 3 below the axis.

Two readings matter most for calculus: where $r = 0$ (the curve passes through the pole) and where $r$ changes sign (the curve switches to the opposite ray).

A rose $r = 3\sin(2\theta)$ has four petals (the figure): one for each stretch of $\theta$ of length $\frac{\pi}{2}$ between zeros of $r$. On a graphing calculator, use polar mode and radians, and set the $\theta$-window to $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$ to see the whole curve.

Why calculus cares

Writing $x = f(\theta)\cos\theta$ and $y = f(\theta)\sin\theta$ turns a polar curve into a parametric curve with parameter $\theta$. Everything from Topics 9.1 to 9.3 then applies: slopes in 9.7, and areas built from thin sectors in 9.8 and 9.9.
