Parametric equations give a moving point's $x$ and $y$ separately. A vector-valued function packages them as one object, the position vector

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle x(t), y(t) \right\rangle,$$

the arrow from the origin to the particle at time $t$. Nothing new is needed to differentiate it: every rule you know works on each component. What is new is the meaning of the result, a vector that tells you both how fast and which way.

Velocity and acceleration vectors

Differentiate each component:

$$\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle x'(t), y'(t) \right\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{v}'(t) = \mathbf{r}''(t) = \left\langle x''(t), y''(t) \right\rangle.$$

$\mathbf{v}(t)$ is the velocity vector and $\mathbf{a}(t)$ the acceleration vector. The rules from straight-line motion still hold, one direction at a time: $x'(t)$ is the horizontal velocity, $y'(t)$ the vertical velocity, and so on.

Worked example 1. A particle has position $\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t^2 - 4t, \sin(\pi t) \right\rangle$. Find its velocity and acceleration vectors at $t = 1$.

Velocity: $\mathbf{v}(t) = \left\langle 2t - 4, \pi\cos(\pi t) \right\rangle$ (chain rule on $\sin(\pi t)$), so $\mathbf{v}(1) = \left\langle -2, \pi\cos\pi \right\rangle = \left\langle -2, -\pi \right\rangle$.

Acceleration: $\mathbf{a}(t) = \left\langle 2, -\pi^2\sin(\pi t) \right\rangle$, so $\mathbf{a}(1) = \left\langle 2, 0 \right\rangle$.

Reading: at $t = 1$ the particle moves left (first component negative) and down (second component negative), while its acceleration points right.

What the velocity vector shows

Draw $\mathbf{v}(t)$ starting at the particle's position. Two facts make it useful:

  • Direction. Its slope is $\frac{y'(t)}{x'(t)} = \frac{dy}{dx}$, the slope of the tangent line from 9.1, and its signs say which way the particle moves. So the velocity vector is tangent to the path and points in the direction of motion.
  • Length. Its magnitude is the speed: $\left\| \mathbf{v}(t) \right\| = \sqrt{(x'(t))^2 + (y'(t))^2}$. In worked example 1 the speed at $t = 1$ is $\sqrt{4 + \pi^2} \approx 3.724$.

The figure shows $\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle 3\cos t, 2\sin t \right\rangle$. At $t = 0$ the particle is at $(3, 0)$ and $\mathbf{v}(0) = \left\langle 0, 2 \right\rangle$ points straight up, along the vertical tangent. At $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$ it is at $(0, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v} = \left\langle -3, 0 \right\rangle$ points straight left. Here $\mathbf{a}(t) = -\mathbf{r}(t)$, so the acceleration always points from the particle back toward the center: it bends the path.

Special cases follow from the components:

Velocity at $t_0$Meaning
$\left\langle 0, b \right\rangle$, $b \ne 0$Moving straight up or down: vertical tangent
$\left\langle a, 0 \right\rangle$, $a \ne 0$Moving straight across: horizontal tangent
$\left\langle 0, 0 \right\rangle$At rest for an instant; the path may have a sharp point there

Velocity from a table

When positions are known only at some times, estimate each component of the velocity with a difference quotient, as in 2.3.

Worked example 2. A boat's position, in kilometers, is $\left\langle 1, 2 \right\rangle$ at $t = 1$ hour and $\left\langle 9, -4 \right\rangle$ at $t = 3$ hours. Estimate its velocity vector and speed at $t = 2$.

Each component over $[1, 3]$: $\frac{9 - 1}{2} = 4$ and $\frac{-4 - 2}{2} = -3$. So $\mathbf{v}(2) \approx \left\langle 4, -3 \right\rangle$ kilometers per hour.

Speed $\approx \sqrt{4^2 + (-3)^2} = 5$ kilometers per hour.
