Every definite integral so far had finite limits and a continuous integrand on a closed interval. An improper integral breaks one of those rules: a limit of integration is infinite, as in $\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$, or the integrand is unbounded on the interval, as in $\int_0^1 \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx$. Calculus handles both the same way: integrate on a safe interval, then take a limit.

Infinite limits of integration

$$\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$

If the limit is a finite number, the improper integral converges to that number. If the limit is infinite or does not exist, the integral diverges. The same idea works at the other end: $\int_{-\infty}^{b} f(x)\,dx = \lim_{a \to -\infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

Worked example 1. Evaluate $\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ or show that it diverges.

$\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty}\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)\Big|_1^b = \lim_{b \to \infty}\left(1 - \frac{1}{b}\right) = 1.$

The limit is a finite number, so the integral converges to 1.

Worked example 2. Evaluate $\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x}\,dx$ or show that it diverges.

$\lim_{b \to \infty} \int_1^b \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \ln b = \infty$, so the integral diverges.

The figure puts the two side by side. Both graphs approach 0, yet one area levels off and the other grows without bound. The height of the curve going to 0 is necessary for convergence on $[a, \infty)$, but it is not enough.

Powers of x: the p-integrals

For $p \ne 1$, $\int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = \frac{b^{1 - p} - 1}{1 - p}$. When $p > 1$, $b^{1 - p} \to 0$ and the integral converges to $\frac{1}{p - 1}$. When $p < 1$, $b^{1 - p} \to \infty$ and it diverges; $p = 1$ gives $\ln b$, which also diverges.

Integrands that blow up

If $f$ is unbounded at an endpoint, put the limit at that endpoint. For an upper endpoint $c$: $\int_a^c f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b \to c^-} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

Worked example 3. Evaluate $\int_0^3 \frac{1}{\sqrt{3 - x}}\,dx$.

The integrand is unbounded as $x \to 3^-$. With $\int (3 - x)^{-1/2}\,dx = -2\sqrt{3 - x}$:

$\lim_{b \to 3^-}\left(-2\sqrt{3 - x}\right)\Big|_0^b = \lim_{b \to 3^-}\left(-2\sqrt{3 - b} + 2\sqrt{3}\right) = 2\sqrt{3}.$ The integral converges.

The dangerous case is a blow-up inside the interval. $\int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ looks like $-\frac{1}{x}\Big|_{-1}^1 = -2$, a negative answer for a positive integrand. The Fundamental Theorem does not apply, because $\frac{1}{x^2}$ is not continuous at 0. Look at the piece from 0 to 1: $\int_0^1 \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \lim_{a \to 0^+}\left(\frac{1}{a} - 1\right) = \infty$, so the integral diverges. Before integrating, always check the integrand for vertical asymptotes on the interval.

Using parts and L'Hospital's rule

Worked example 4. Evaluate $\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx$.

Parts with $u = x$, $dv = e^{-x}\,dx$: $\int xe^{-x}\,dx = -xe^{-x} - e^{-x} + C$.

$\lim_{b \to \infty}\left(-xe^{-x} - e^{-x}\right)\Big|_0^b = \lim_{b \to \infty}\left(-\frac{b}{e^b} - \frac{1}{e^b}\right) - (0 - 1).$

$\frac{b}{e^b}$ has the form $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, so by L'Hospital's rule $\lim_{b \to \infty}\frac{b}{e^b} = \lim_{b \to \infty}\frac{1}{e^b} = 0$. The integral converges to $0 - 0 + 1 = 1$.

Writing it so it earns the point

  • Write $\lim_{b \to \infty}$ in front of every expression until the step where you take the limit. Dropping it in the middle makes a false statement such as $\int_1^b \frac{dx}{x^2} = 1$.
  • Never substitute $\infty$: $-\frac{1}{\infty}$ and $e^{-\infty}$ are not numbers. Recent scoring guidelines give the point only when the notation is right all the way.
  • State the conclusion in words: "the integral converges to 1" or "the integral diverges".
