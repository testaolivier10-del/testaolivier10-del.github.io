A Taylor polynomial has a last term. If you never stop adding terms, you get a power series, the Taylor series of the function. For the functions in this course, that series does more than approximate: on its interval of convergence it equals the function.

From polynomials to a series

The Taylor series of $f$ about $x = a$ is

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x - a)^2 + \cdots$$

About $a = 0$ it is called the Maclaurin series. The Taylor polynomial $P_n$ is its partial sum through the $(x - a)^n$ term. So the questions of the last three topics fit together: $P_n(x)$ is a partial sum, the error bounds measure how far a partial sum is from the sum, and the interval of convergence says where the partial sums settle at all.

For $e^x$, every derivative at 0 is 1, so the Maclaurin series is $\sum \frac{x^n}{n!}$. The figure shows its first partial sums closing in on $e^x$. The ratio test gives $\frac{|x|}{n + 1} \to 0$ for every $x$, so the series converges everywhere, and it converges to $e^x$.

Patterns help you remember them. $\sin x$ is odd, so only odd powers appear; $\cos x$ is even, so only even powers. Both alternate in sign because their derivatives cycle through $\sin, \cos, -\sin, -\cos$. The series for $\frac{1}{1 - x}$ is just the geometric series, which is why it works only for $|x| < 1$.

Building a Taylor series from derivatives

Worked example 1. Find the Taylor series for $f(x) = e^x$ about $x = 2$.

Every derivative of $e^x$ is $e^x$, so $f^{(n)}(2) = e^2$ for every $n$.

The coefficients are $\frac{e^2}{n!}$, and the series is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^2 (x - 2)^n}{n!} = e^2 + e^2(x - 2) + \frac{e^2}{2!}(x - 2)^2 + \cdots$

When a question gives a formula for $f^{(n)}(a)$ instead of the function, the same recipe applies: divide by $n!$ and simplify. If $f^{(n)}(0) = \frac{n!}{3^n}$, the coefficients are $\frac{1}{3^n}$ and the series is the geometric series $\sum \left(\frac{x}{3}\right)^n$.

Reading coefficients backward

If a function is given by a power series $\sum c_n (x - a)^n$ with a positive radius, then that series is its Taylor series, so $c_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}$ and

$$f^{(n)}(a) = n! \cdot c_n.$$

Worked example 2. $g(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^n}{n + 2}$. Find $g''(0)$.

The coefficient of $x^2$ is $c_2 = \frac{(-1)^2}{2 + 2} = \frac{1}{4}$, so $g''(0) = 2! \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$.

Using known series to find sums and limits

Worked example 3. Find $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n + 1)!}\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)^{2n+1}$.

This is the series for $\sin x$ with $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$, so the sum is $\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$.

Worked example 4. Find $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2}$.

$1 - \cos x = \frac{x^2}{2!} - \frac{x^4}{4!} + \cdots$, so $\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{x^2}{24} + \cdots \to \frac{1}{2}$.

Series or polynomial?

When a question asks for a Taylor polynomial, write a finite sum that ends at the stated degree. When it asks for a series, write the first few terms followed by "$+ \cdots$", or use sigma notation with the general term. Mixing the two, a polynomial with "$+ \cdots$" or a series with no general term when one is asked for, costs points on the exam.
