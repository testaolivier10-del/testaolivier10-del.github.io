A polar curve $r = f(\theta)$ is traced as $\theta$ turns. Write it in $x$ and $y$ and it becomes a parametric curve with parameter $\theta$:

$$x = r\cos\theta = f(\theta)\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta = f(\theta)\sin\theta.$$

So every idea from Topics 9.1 and 9.2 carries over. This page adds two things polar questions ask about: the slope in polar form, and the meaning of $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$.

Derivatives of $x$ and $y$ with respect to $\theta$

Both $x$ and $y$ are products, so each derivative has two terms:

$$\frac{dx}{d\theta} = \frac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta, \qquad \frac{dy}{d\theta} = \frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta.$$

Do not memorize these as new formulas. Write $x = r\cos\theta$, apply the product rule, and they appear. The signs tell you how the point moves: $\frac{dy}{d\theta} > 0$ means the point is moving up as $\theta$ increases, $\frac{dx}{d\theta} < 0$ means it is moving left.

The slope of a polar curve

As for any parametric curve, where $\frac{dx}{d\theta} \ne 0$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta} = \frac{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta}.$$

Worked example 1. Find the tangent line to $r = 2 + 2\cos\theta$ at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

Values first: $r = 2 + 2\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 2$ and $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = -2\sin\theta = -2$.

Derivatives: $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = (-2)(0) - (2)(1) = -2$ and $\frac{dy}{d\theta} = (-2)(1) + (2)(0) = -2$.

Slope: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-2}{-2} = 1$. The point: $x = 2\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$, $y = 2\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 2$.

Tangent line: $y - 2 = 1(x - 0)$, so $y = x + 2$ (the blue line in the figure).

Horizontal and vertical tangents. A horizontal tangent needs $\frac{dy}{d\theta} = 0$ with $\frac{dx}{d\theta} \ne 0$; a vertical tangent needs $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = 0$ with $\frac{dy}{d\theta} \ne 0$. On the cardioid at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$: $r = 3$, $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = -\sqrt{3}$, so $\frac{dy}{d\theta} = -\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 0$ while $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} = -2\sqrt{3}$. That is the top of the curve.

At the pole. If $r = 0$ at $\theta = \alpha$ and $\frac{dr}{d\theta} \ne 0$ there, the formula simplifies to $\frac{dy}{dx} = \tan\alpha$: the curve leaves the pole along the ray $\theta = \alpha$.

What $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$ tells you

$\frac{dr}{d\theta}$ is the rate of change of $r$, the signed distance from the pole. It is not the slope. Its sign, read together with the sign of $r$, says whether the point is moving toward or away from the pole:

$> 0$$> 0$increasingaway from the pole
$> 0$$< 0$decreasingtoward the pole
$< 0$$< 0$increasingaway from the pole

The short rule: the same sign means away, opposite signs mean toward. On the exam, give both signs as your reason: "$r(2) > 0$ and $r'(2) < 0$, so the point is moving toward the origin."

Worked example 2. For $r = 1 + 2\cos\theta$ at $\theta = \pi$, is the point moving toward or away from the pole?

$r(\pi) = 1 - 2 = -1$ and $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = -2\sin\pi = 0$. Neither, for an instant: the distance has a turning point. A little later, at $\theta = \frac{7\pi}{6}$, $r = 1 - \sqrt{3} < 0$ and $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = -2\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = 1 > 0$: opposite signs, so the point is moving toward the pole, finishing the inner loop.

Rates in time and distances from the axes

If $\theta$ depends on time, the chain rule converts: $\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{dr}{d\theta} \cdot \frac{d\theta}{dt}$, and likewise for $x$ and $y$. Distance from the $y$-axis is $|x| = |r\cos\theta|$; distance from the $x$-axis is $|y| = |r\sin\theta|$. On calculator questions, write the setup ("$x = r(\theta)\cos\theta$") before the number, use radian mode, and round to three decimals only at the end.
