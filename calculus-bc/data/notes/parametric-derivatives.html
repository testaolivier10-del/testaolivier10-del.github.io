A curve given by parametric equations $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$ is the path of a moving point. Calculus asks three questions about it at each instant: how fast is $x$ changing, how fast is $y$ changing, and how steep is the path? The first two are derivatives you already know how to take. The third needs one new idea.

Two rates and a direction

Differentiate each coordinate with respect to $t$. $\frac{dx}{dt}$ is the horizontal rate and $\frac{dy}{dt}$ the vertical rate of the point. Their signs tell you which way it moves:

SignMeaning
$\frac{dx}{dt} > 0$$x$ is increasing: the point moves right
$\frac{dx}{dt} < 0$$x$ is decreasing: the point moves left
$\frac{dy}{dt} > 0$$y$ is increasing: the point moves up
$\frac{dy}{dt} < 0$$y$ is decreasing: the point moves down

Every rule from Units 2 and 3 applies, one coordinate at a time: the power, product, quotient and chain rules all work exactly as before, with $t$ as the variable.

The slope of the curve

If $y$ can be thought of as a function of $x$ near a point, the chain rule says

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}.$$

Solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}, \qquad \text{provided } \frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0.$$

In words: the slope is the vertical rate over the horizontal rate. If the point rises 6 units per second while it moves right 2 units per second, the path rises 3 units per unit of $x$. Notice that $\frac{dy}{dx}$ comes out as a function of $t$. You evaluate it at a time, not at a point.

Worked example 1. A curve is given by $x = 2t + 1$, $y = t^2 - 4t$. Find the line tangent to the curve at $t = 3$.

Rates: $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} = 2t - 4$.

Slope: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2t - 4}{2} = t - 2$, which is $1$ at $t = 3$.

Point: $(x(3), y(3)) = (7, 9 - 12) = (7, -3)$. Use the same $t$ for the point and the slope.

Tangent line: $y + 3 = 1(x - 7)$, that is, $y = x - 10$.

Check by eliminating $t$: $t = \frac{x - 1}{2}$, so $y = \frac{(x - 1)^2}{4} - 2(x - 1)$ and $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x - 1}{2} - 2$. At $x = 7$ this is $3 - 2 = 1$. The parametric route was faster and needed no elimination.

Horizontal and vertical tangents

A fraction is 0 when its numerator is 0, and it is undefined when its denominator is 0. So:

  • Horizontal tangent: $\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0$. For an instant the point moves straight across.
  • Vertical tangent: $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} \ne 0$. For an instant the point moves straight up or down.

In worked example 1, $\frac{dy}{dt} = 2t - 4 = 0$ at $t = 2$, while $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2$. The tangent is horizontal at $(5, -4)$, the lowest point of the curve.

When both rates are 0 at the same time, the point stops for an instant and the quotient tells you nothing. The curve may have a sharp corner there or a perfectly smooth tangent; you have to look more closely, so state that the test does not apply.

The figure shows why "evaluate at a time" matters. The curve $x = t^2$, $y = t^3 - 3t$ passes through $(3, 0)$ twice. Its slope is $\frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t}$, which is $\sqrt{3}$ at $t = \sqrt{3}$ and $-\sqrt{3}$ at $t = -\sqrt{3}$: two tangent lines at one point. A formula in $x$ could never give both.

Curves given by a table

On the exam, $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are often known only through a table of values and derivatives. The same rules apply to the numbers.

Worked example 2. At $t = 4$, a curve has $x(4) = 2$, $y(4) = -1$, $x'(4) = -5$ and $y'(4) = 10$. Find the tangent line and the direction of motion at $t = 4$.

Slope: $\frac{y'(4)}{x'(4)} = \frac{10}{-5} = -2$. Tangent line: $y + 1 = -2(x - 2)$.

Direction: $x'(4) < 0$, so the point moves left; $y'(4) > 0$, so it moves up.

Common slips

SlipFix
Using $\frac{dy}{dt}$ as the slopeDivide by $\frac{dx}{dt}$: the slope is change in $y$ per unit of $x$
Dividing upside down$\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$: the $y$ rate on top, as in $\frac{dy}{dx}$
Using $t$ as the $x$-coordinateThe point is $(x(t_0), y(t_0))$; compute both
Calling $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ horizontalNo horizontal motion means a vertical tangent

On calculator questions, store $x'(t_0)$ and $y'(t_0)$ before dividing, and give the slope to three decimal places, with the quotient written out as your setup.
