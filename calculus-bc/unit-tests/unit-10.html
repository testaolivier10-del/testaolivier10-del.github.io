1. Does ∑n=0∞3n2n\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{3^n}{2^n} converge?

  1. Yes: to 11−3/2=−2\frac{1}{1 - 3/2} = -2
  2. Yes: it is geometric
  3. No: its ratio 23\frac{2}{3} is less than 1
  4. No: it is geometric with r=32r = \frac{3}{2}, and |r|≥1|r| \ge 1
Show the answer

3n2n=(32)n\frac{3^n}{2^n} = \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^n: geometric with r=32r = \frac{3}{2}. Since |r|≥1|r| \ge 1, the series diverges.

  • Yes: to 11−3/2=−2\frac{1}{1 - 3/2} = -2: The formula a1−r\frac{a}{1 - r} holds only when |r|<1|r| < 1. A sum of positive terms cannot be −2-2.
  • Yes: it is geometric: Being geometric is not enough: the ratio 32\frac{3}{2} is too big.
  • No: its ratio 23\frac{2}{3} is less than 1: The ratio is 3n+1/2n+13n/2n\frac{3^{n+1}/2^{n+1}}{3^n/2^n}=32= \frac{3}{2}, not 23\frac{2}{3}.
  • Correct: No: it is geometric with r=32r = \frac{3}{2}, and |r|≥1|r| \ge 1: Right: the terms grow, and the partial sums grow without bound.

2. What does the integral test say about ∑n=1∞1n\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}?

  1. It converges, since 1n→0\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0 as nn grows
  2. It converges: ∫1∞x−1/2dx\int_1^{\infty} x^{-1/2}\,dx=2= 2
  3. Nothing: 1x\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} is not decreasing
  4. It diverges, since ∫1∞x−1/2dx\int_1^{\infty} x^{-1/2}\,dx diverges
Show the answer

f(x)=1xf(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} is positive, continuous and decreasing on [1,∞)[1, \infty), and limb→∞(2b−2)\lim_{b \to \infty} \left(2\sqrt{b} - 2\right)=∞= \infty, so the series diverges by the integral test.

  • It converges, since 1n→0\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0 as nn grows: Terms approaching 0 never prove convergence.
  • It converges: ∫1∞x−1/2dx\int_1^{\infty} x^{-1/2}\,dx=2= 2: ∫1bx−1/2dx\int_1^{b} x^{-1/2}\,dx=2b−2= 2\sqrt{b} - 2, and b\sqrt{b} grows without bound as b→∞b \to \infty.
  • Nothing: 1x\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} is not decreasing: 1x\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} is positive, continuous and decreasing for x≥1x \ge 1.
  • Correct: It diverges, since ∫1∞x−1/2dx\int_1^{\infty} x^{-1/2}\,dx diverges: Right: the integral diverges, so the series does.

3. What does the direct comparison test say about ∑n=1∞1n2+5\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2 + 5}?

  1. It diverges, since 1n2+5<1n\frac{1}{n^2 + 5} < \frac{1}{n}
  2. It converges, since 0<1n2+5<1n20 < \frac{1}{n^2 + 5} < \frac{1}{n^2}
  3. It converges, since 1n2+5>1n3\frac{1}{n^2 + 5} > \frac{1}{n^3}
  4. It diverges, since n2+5>n2n^2 + 5 > n^2
Show the answer

For every nn, 0<1n2+5<1n20 < \frac{1}{n^2 + 5} < \frac{1}{n^2}, and ∑1n2\sum \frac{1}{n^2} converges (p=2>1p = 2 > 1), so the series converges by the direct comparison test.

  • It diverges, since 1n2+5<1n\frac{1}{n^2 + 5} < \frac{1}{n}: Smaller than a divergent series decides nothing.
  • Correct: It converges, since 0<1n2+5<1n20 < \frac{1}{n^2 + 5} < \frac{1}{n^2}: Right: smaller than a convergent pp-series with p=2p = 2.
  • It converges, since 1n2+5>1n3\frac{1}{n^2 + 5} > \frac{1}{n^3}: Larger than a convergent series decides nothing.
  • It diverges, since n2+5>n2n^2 + 5 > n^2: A larger denominator makes the terms smaller than 1n2\frac{1}{n^2}, which points to convergence.

  2. a2>a1a_2 > a_1, and from n=2n = 2 on the sizes decrease

5. Which argument shows that an=nn2+3a_n = \frac{n}{n^2 + 3} decreases for n≥2n \ge 2?

  1. The table values fall from n=2n = 2 to n=5n = 5
  2. The numerator nn grows more slowly than n2+3n^2 + 3, so the terms approach 0
  3. With f(x)=xx2+3f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 + 3}, f′(x)f'(x)=3−x2(x2+3)2<0= \frac{3 - x^2}{(x^2 + 3)^2} < 0 for x>3x > \sqrt{3}
  4. With f(x)=xx2+3f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 + 3}, f′(x)=12x<0f'(x) = \frac{1}{2x} < 0 for x>0x > 0
Show the answer

By the quotient rule, f′(x)f'(x)=(x2+3)−x(2x)(x2+3)2= \frac{(x^2 + 3) - x(2x)}{(x^2 + 3)^2}=3−x2(x2+3)2= \frac{3 - x^2}{(x^2 + 3)^2}, which is negative for x>3≈1.73x > \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73. So an+1<ana_{n+1} < a_n for every n≥2n \ge 2.

  • The table values fall from n=2n = 2 to n=5n = 5: Five values cannot show what happens for every larger nn; a derivative argument can.
  • The numerator nn grows more slowly than n2+3n^2 + 3, so the terms approach 0: Approaching 0 is a different condition from decreasing; a sequence can approach 0 while going up and down.
  • Correct: With f(x)=xx2+3f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 + 3}, f′(x)f'(x)=3−x2(x2+3)2<0= \frac{3 - x^2}{(x^2 + 3)^2} < 0 for x>3x > \sqrt{3}: Right: a negative derivative past 3\sqrt{3} covers every n≥2n \ge 2.
  • With f(x)=xx2+3f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 + 3}, f′(x)=12x<0f'(x) = \frac{1}{2x} < 0 for x>0x > 0: That differentiates the top and bottom separately; use the quotient rule.

6. What does the ratio test conclude about ∑n=1∞(−1)nn4n\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n n}{4^n}?

  1. It does not apply, since the terms change sign
  2. It diverges: the ratio an+1an\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} is negative
  3. It converges: the limit is 4
  4. It converges: |an+1an|→14\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| \to \frac{1}{4}
Show the answer

|an+1an|\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|=n+14n+1⋅4nn= \frac{n + 1}{4^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{4^n}{n}=n+14n→14<1= \frac{n + 1}{4n} \to \frac{1}{4} < 1, so the series converges by the ratio test.

  • It does not apply, since the terms change sign: The ratio test uses |an+1an|\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|, so it works on series with signs.
  • It diverges: the ratio an+1an\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} is negative: The test takes the absolute value of the ratio before the limit.
  • It converges: the limit is 4: That inverts the ratio; a limit of 4 would mean divergence.
  • Correct: It converges: |an+1an|→14\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| \to \frac{1}{4}: Right: the absolute value removes the sign, and 14<1\frac{1}{4} < 1.

7. For which values of pp is ∑n=1∞(−1)nnp\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n^p} conditionally convergent?

  1. 0<p≤10 < p \le 1
  2. p>1p > 1
  3. p≤0p \le 0
  4. p≥1p \ge 1
Show the answer

For p>0p > 0 the sizes 1np\frac{1}{n^p} decrease to 0, so the series converges. The absolute values form a pp-series, which diverges for p≤1p \le 1. So the convergence is conditional for 0<p≤10 < p \le 1 and absolute for p>1p > 1.

  • Correct: 0<p≤10 < p \le 1: Right: then it converges by the alternating series test, while ∑1np\sum \frac{1}{n^p} diverges.
  • p>1p > 1: For p>1p > 1, ∑1np\sum \frac{1}{n^p} converges, so the convergence is absolute.
  • p≤0p \le 0: For p≤0p \le 0 the sizes do not approach 0, and the series diverges.
  • p≥1p \ge 1: For p>1p > 1 the convergence is absolute; p=1p = 1 is the one conditional case in that range.

8. For an alternating series that meets the conditions of the test, S20=1.3527S_{20} = 1.3527 and a21=0.01a_{21} = 0.01. What does the error bound tell you about the sum SS?

  1. 1.3427≤S≤1.36271.3427 \le S \le 1.3627
  2. S=1.3527±0.01S = 1.3527 \pm 0.01 exactly
  3. S=1.3627S = 1.3627
  4. S≥1.3527S \ge 1.3527
Show the answer

|S−S20|≤a21=0.01|S - S_{20}| \le a_{21} = 0.01 means S20−0.01≤S≤S20+0.01S_{20} - 0.01 \le S \le S_{20} + 0.01, that is 1.3427≤S≤1.36271.3427 \le S \le 1.3627.

  • Correct: 1.3427≤S≤1.36271.3427 \le S \le 1.3627: Right: SS is within 0.010.01 of S20S_{20}.
  • S=1.3527±0.01S = 1.3527 \pm 0.01 exactly: The bound gives a range for SS, not two exact values.
  • S=1.3627S = 1.3627: The bound limits the error; it does not say the error equals 0.010.01.
  • S≥1.3527S \ge 1.3527: The sum may lie on either side of S20S_{20}, depending on the sign of the 21st term.

9. Why is the fifth-degree Maclaurin polynomial for cos⁡x\cos x the same as the fourth-degree one?

  1. The fifth-degree term is too small to matter
  2. cos⁡x\cos x has no derivatives past the fourth
  3. A polynomial for cos⁡x\cos x stops at degree 4
  4. d5dx5cos⁡x=−sin⁡x\frac{d^5}{dx^5}\cos x = -\sin x, which is 0 at x=0x = 0
Show the answer

The derivatives of cos⁡x\cos x cycle: cos⁡x,\cos x,−sin⁡x,-\sin x,−cos⁡x,-\cos x,sin⁡x,\sin x,cos⁡x,\cos x,−sin⁡x,-\sin x,…\ldots At 0, every odd-order derivative is 0, so the x5x^5 term is 0⋅x50 \cdot x^5 and P5=P4P_5 = P_4.

  • The fifth-degree term is too small to matter: The coefficient is exactly 0, not small.
  • cos⁡x\cos x has no derivatives past the fourth: cos⁡x\cos x has derivatives of every order; they cycle with period 4.
  • A polynomial for cos⁡x\cos x stops at degree 4: It can have any even degree; the odd-degree coefficients are 0.
  • Correct: d5dx5cos⁡x=−sin⁡x\frac{d^5}{dx^5}\cos x = -\sin x, which is 0 at x=0x = 0: Right: the x5x^5 coefficient is 0.

10. P3(x)=x−x36P_3(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{6} approximates sin⁡x\sin x. Using |d4dx4sin⁡x|≤1\left|\frac{d^4}{dx^4}\sin x\right| \le 1, which is the Lagrange error bound for |sin⁡(0.2)−P3(0.2)|\left|\sin(0.2) - P_3(0.2)\right|?

  1. (0.2)33!=1750\frac{(0.2)^3}{3!} = \frac{1}{750}
  2. (0.2)4=1625(0.2)^4 = \frac{1}{625}, with no factorial
  3. (0.2)44!=115000\frac{(0.2)^4}{4!} = \frac{1}{15000}
  4. (0.2)43!=13750\frac{(0.2)^4}{3!} = \frac{1}{3750}
Show the answer

|sin⁡(0.2)−P3(0.2)|≤14!(0.2)4\left|\sin(0.2) - P_3(0.2)\right| \le \frac{1}{4!}(0.2)^4=0.001624= \frac{0.0016}{24}=115000= \frac{1}{15000}.

  • (0.2)33!=1750\frac{(0.2)^3}{3!} = \frac{1}{750}: The bound for P3P_3 uses the next derivative: the fourth power and 4!4!.
  • (0.2)4=1625(0.2)^4 = \frac{1}{625}, with no factorial: Divide by 4!=244! = 24.
  • Correct: (0.2)44!=115000\frac{(0.2)^4}{4!} = \frac{1}{15000}: Right: the fourth derivative, 4!4!, and the fourth power.
  • (0.2)43!=13750\frac{(0.2)^4}{3!} = \frac{1}{3750}: The factorial matches the power: 4!4! with (0.2)4(0.2)^4.

11. A power series ∑an(x+1)n\sum a_n (x + 1)^n converges at x=3x = 3 and diverges at x=−7x = -7. What can you conclude about its radius of convergence RR?

  1. R=4R = 4
  2. R=6R = 6
  3. 4≤R≤64 \le R \le 6
  4. R≥6R \ge 6
Show the answer

The center is −1-1. Converging at x=3x = 3, a distance 4 from the center, means R≥4R \ge 4. Diverging at x=−7x = -7, a distance 6 away, means R≤6R \le 6. So 4≤R≤64 \le R \le 6.

  • R=4R = 4: RR could be larger than 4; the series could converge farther out.
  • R=6R = 6: RR could be smaller than 6, down to 4.
  • Correct: 4≤R≤64 \le R \le 6: Right: converging 4 from the center needs R≥4R \ge 4; diverging 6 away needs R≤6R \le 6.
  • R≥6R \ge 6: The series diverges at distance 6 from the center, so RR is at most 6.

12. How is the Taylor polynomial P4(x)P_4(x)=1= 1+x+ x+x22+ \frac{x^2}{2}+x36+ \frac{x^3}{6}+x424+ \frac{x^4}{24} related to the Maclaurin series for exe^x?

  1. It equals the series for xx between −1-1 and 11
  2. It is a partial sum: the series' first five terms
  3. It is the remainder left after the series
  4. It is unrelated: the series has different coefficients
Show the answer

The Maclaurin series for exe^x is ∑xnn!\sum \frac{x^n}{n!}, and P4P_4 is its partial sum through n=4n = 4: its first five terms.

  • It equals the series for xx between −1-1 and 11: The series has more nonzero terms; P4P_4 only approximates it.
  • Correct: It is a partial sum: the series' first five terms: Right: a Taylor polynomial is a partial sum of the Taylor series.
  • It is the remainder left after the series: P4P_4 is the beginning of the series, not what is left over.
  • It is unrelated: the series has different coefficients: The coefficients are the same, 1n!\frac{1}{n!}; the series keeps going.
