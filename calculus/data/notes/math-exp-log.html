Some quantities grow or shrink by the same percentage in each equal stretch of time: a savings account, a bacteria culture, a drug leaving the blood, the gap between a cup of coffee's temperature and the room's. These are modeled by exponential functions, and calculus has a favorite one, $e^x$. Its partner, the natural logarithm $\ln x$, lets you solve for a time hidden in an exponent. This page reviews both and the rules that connect them.

Exponential functions and the number $e$

An exponential function has the variable in the exponent: $f(x) = b^x$, with a positive base $b \ne 1$. Each step of 1 in $x$ multiplies the output by $b$. With $b > 1$ the function shows exponential growth; with $0 < b < 1$, exponential decay.

Calculus works best with one particular base, $e \approx 2.71828$. The reason will appear in Unit 2: $e^x$ is the function whose rate of change equals its own value, which makes every formula involving it clean. For now, know its graph and its rules:

  • $e^0 = 1$, so the graph passes through $(0, 1)$.
  • $e^x > 0$ for every $x$. The graph never touches the $x$-axis; the $x$-axis is a horizontal asymptote on the left.
  • All the exponent rules apply: $e^a e^b = e^{a + b}$, $\frac{e^a}{e^b} = e^{a - b}$, $(e^a)^b = e^{ab}$, $e^{-x} = \frac{1}{e^x}$.

The natural logarithm

The natural logarithm $\ln x$ answers one question: what power of $e$ gives $x$? $$\ln x = y \quad \text{means} \quad e^y = x$$ So $\ln e = 1$, $\ln 1 = 0$, and $\ln(e^5) = 5$. Since $e^y$ is always positive, $\ln x$ is defined only for $x > 0$.

Because $\ln$ undoes $e^x$ and $e^x$ undoes $\ln$, they cancel each other: $$e^{\ln x} = x \;(x > 0) \qquad \ln(e^x) = x \;(\text{every } x)$$

Log rules

Every log rule is an exponent rule read backward. Multiplying powers adds exponents, so the log of a product is a sum:

RuleExample
$\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$$\ln 12 = \ln 3 + \ln 4$
$\ln\frac{a}{b} = \ln a - \ln b$$\ln\frac{x^3}{y} = 3\ln x - \ln y$
$\ln(a^p) = p\ln a$$\ln\sqrt{x} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x$

And the rules that do not exist, which are the classic slips:

WrongWhy
$\ln(a + b) = \ln a + \ln b$a sum of logs comes from a product: $\ln a + \ln b = \ln(ab)$
$(\ln x)^2 = 2\ln x$$2\ln x = \ln(x^2)$, a different thing
$\frac{\ln a}{\ln b} = \ln(a - b)$there is no rule for a quotient of logs

Worked example 1. Simplify $\ln\left(x^2\sqrt{x}\right)$ and $e^{2\ln 3}$.

$x^2\sqrt{x} = x^2 \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{5/2}$, so $\ln\left(x^{5/2}\right) = \frac{5}{2}\ln x$.

$2\ln 3 = \ln 3^2 = \ln 9$, so $e^{2\ln 3} = e^{\ln 9} = 9$. Move the coefficient into the log before canceling $e$ and $\ln$.

Solving exponential and log equations

To free a variable from an exponent, isolate the exponential, then take $\ln$ of both sides. To free a variable from inside a log, isolate the log, then raise $e$ to both sides.

Worked example 2. Solve $3e^{2x} = 21$.

Isolate: $e^{2x} = 7$. Take $\ln$: $2x = \ln 7$. Solve: $x = \frac{\ln 7}{2}$.

A common slip is dividing by 2 inside the log: $\ln\frac{7}{2}$ is a different number.

Worked example 3. Solve $\ln(x - 1) = 3$.

Raise $e$ to both sides: $x - 1 = e^3$, so $x = e^3 + 1$.

Check the domain: $x - 1 = e^3 > 0$, so the solution is valid.

Growth and decay with $e$

A quantity that starts at $A$ and grows or decays continuously at a steady percentage rate is written $A e^{kt}$. The number $k$ sets the speed: $k > 0$ for growth, $k < 0$ for decay. Two questions come up constantly: when does it reach a level, and how long does it take to double or halve?

Worked example 4. A sample of $A(t) = 80e^{kt}$ grams halves every 5 hours. Find $k$.

$80e^{5k} = 40$, so $e^{5k} = \frac{1}{2}$, so $5k = \ln\frac{1}{2} = -\ln 2$.

$k = -\frac{\ln 2}{5} \approx -0.139$ per hour. The negative sign confirms decay.

On a calculator question, store $\ln 2$ and other intermediate values rather than rounding them, and give the final answer to three decimal places.
