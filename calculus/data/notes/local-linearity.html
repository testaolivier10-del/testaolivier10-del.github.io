Zoom in on the graph of any differentiable function, near any point, and it starts to look like a straight line: its tangent line. This is local linearity. It turns the tangent line into a tool for estimating values that are hard to compute, such as $\sqrt{26}$ or $f(2.1)$ when you only know $f$ at 2. The exam then asks one more question every time: is the estimate too big or too small?

The tangent line as an approximation

The tangent line at $x = a$ passes through $(a, f(a))$ with slope $f'(a)$. In point-slope form it is the linearization of $f$ at $a$:

$$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$$

For $x$ close to $a$, $f(x) \approx L(x)$. Read the formula as a story: start at the known height $f(a)$, then add the rate $f'(a)$ times the step $x - a$. It is the "rate times change" idea from the derivative in context, written as a line.

Worked example 1. Estimate $\sqrt{26}$.

Choose a nearby point where $\sqrt{x}$ is easy: $a = 25$, $f(25) = 5$.

$f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, so $f'(25) = \frac{1}{10}$.

$L(26) = 5 + \frac{1}{10}(26 - 25) = 5.1$. A calculator gives $\sqrt{26} \approx 5.0990$, so the estimate is close, and slightly too large.

Worked example 2. A table gives $f(5) = 1$ and $f'(5) = 2$. Estimate $f(4.8)$.

$L(x) = 1 + 2(x - 5)$, so $f(4.8) \approx 1 + 2(-0.2) = 0.6$.

The step $x - a = -0.2$ is negative because 4.8 is to the left of 5. Dropping that sign is a common slip.

Too big or too small? Concavity decides

A graph is concave up where it bends upward, like a cup, and concave down where it bends downward, like a cap. The second derivative tells which: $f'' > 0$ means $f'$ is increasing, so the slopes keep growing and the graph bends up; $f'' < 0$ means the graph bends down. (Unit 5 studies concavity in full; here we need just this much.)

A concave up graph lies above its tangent lines, so the line gives values that are too small: an underestimate. A concave down graph lies below its tangent lines, so the line gives an overestimate.

Worked example 3. Use the tangent line to $\ln x$ at $x = 1$ to estimate $\ln(1.2)$, and decide whether the estimate is too large or too small.

$\ln 1 = 0$ and the slope is $\frac{1}{1} = 1$, so $L(x) = x - 1$ and $\ln(1.2) \approx 0.2$.

The second derivative of $\ln x$ is $-\frac{1}{x^2}$, which is negative for every $x > 0$. The graph is concave down, so the tangent line lies above it, and $0.2$ is an overestimate. (Indeed $\ln 1.2 \approx 0.182$.)

The justification must name concavity. "Overestimate because $f$ is increasing" does not earn the point: whether $f$ is increasing decides which way the line tilts, not which side of the curve it sits on. The concavity must hold on the whole interval between $a$ and the point you estimate.

How good is the estimate?

The gap between the curve and the line is tiny near $a$ and grows as you move away. Using the tangent line at $x = 1$, $\ln 1.2$ is estimated as $0.2$ (true value about $0.182$), but $\ln 3$ is estimated as $2$ (true value about $1.099$). Always build the line at the known point closest to where you estimate.

Slips that cost points

  • Swapping the height and the slope in $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$.
  • Losing the sign of $x - a$ when estimating to the left of $a$.
  • Justifying over or under with the sign of $f'$, or with no reason.
  • Using a tangent line built at a point far from the estimate.
