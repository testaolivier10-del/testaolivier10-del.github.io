In Unit 4 you started from a position function $x(t)$ and differentiated: velocity $v = x'$, acceleration $a = v'$. Now run the film backward. Given the velocity, integrals recover how far the particle moved and where it is. Two different questions hide here, and the exam asks both: how far did it end up from its start, and how far did it actually travel?

Displacement: the integral of velocity

Because $v$ is the derivative of $x$, the Fundamental Theorem gives

$$\int_a^b v(t)\,dt = x(b) - x(a).$$

This net change in position is the displacement on $[a, b]$. On a velocity graph it is the signed area: area above the $t$-axis (moving right, or up, or forward) counts as positive, area below (moving left) counts as negative. So a trip right followed by an equal trip left has displacement 0.

Total distance: the integral of speed

A car's odometer does not subtract when you drive back home. Speed is $|v(t)|$, and the total distance traveled is

$$\int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt.$$

On the graph, that is the sum of the areas, every one counted as positive.

Worked example 1 (no calculator). A particle moves with $v(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3$ meters per second for $0 \le t \le 4$. Find its displacement and its total distance.

Antiderivative: $F(t) = \frac{t^3}{3} - 2t^2 + 3t$.

Displacement: $F(4) - F(0) = \frac{64}{3} - 32 + 12 = \frac{4}{3}$ meters.

Distance: $v = (t - 1)(t - 3)$ changes sign at $t = 1$ and $t = 3$, so split there. $F(1) - F(0) = \frac{4}{3}$; $F(3) - F(1) = 0 - \frac{4}{3} = -\frac{4}{3}$; $F(4) - F(3) = \frac{4}{3}$.

Add the sizes: $\frac{4}{3} + \frac{4}{3} + \frac{4}{3} = 4$ meters. The particle traveled 4 m but ended only $\frac{4}{3}$ m to the right of where it began.

Without a calculator you cannot integrate $|v|$ directly. Find where $v$ changes sign, integrate $v$ on each piece, and add the absolute values. Check every zero, not just whole-number times.

Position from a starting point

Displacement tells you the change. To know the position itself, you need one known position, then add the change:

$$x(t) = x(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} v(s)\,ds.$$

Worked example 2 (graph). A particle's velocity is made of segments through $(0, 0)$, $(2, 4)$, $(5, 4)$, $(8, -2)$ and $(10, -2)$, in m/s, and $x(0) = -3$. Find $x(10)$, the total distance, and the time it is farthest right.

Areas: 4 on $[0, 2]$, 12 on $[2, 5]$, 4 on $[5, 7]$ (where $v$ reaches 0), then 1 and 4 below the axis on $[7, 8]$ and $[8, 10]$.

$x(10) = -3 + (4 + 12 + 4 - 1 - 4) = -3 + 15 = 12$ m. Total distance: $4 + 12 + 4 + 1 + 4 = 25$ m.

The particle moves right while $v > 0$ and left after $v$ changes sign at $t = 7$, so it is farthest right at $t = 7$: $x(7) = -3 + 20 = 17$ m.

The same idea gives velocity from acceleration: $v(t) = v(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} a(s)\,ds$. With $a(t) = 6t$ and $v(0) = -3$, $v(2) = -3 + 12 = 9$ m/s.

With a calculator

When the velocity has no easy antiderivative, the calculator does the integral, and it can integrate $|v|$ directly. Write the setup every time, then the value to three decimal places:

  • Displacement: $\int_0^6 (4 - t + 2\sin t)\,dt \approx 6.080$ feet.
  • Total distance: $\int_0^6 |4 - t + 2\sin t|\,dt \approx 17.430$ feet.
  • Position, if $x(0) = 5$: $x(6) = 5 + \int_0^6 v(t)\,dt \approx 11.080$ feet.

Radian mode, and keep full precision until the final answer.

Common slips

  • Giving the displacement when the question asks for total distance, or the reverse.
  • Forgetting the initial position, or starting the integral at 0 when the known position is at another time.
  • Putting the absolute value outside: $\left|\int v\,dt\right|$ is the size of the displacement, not the distance.
  • Units: the integral of feet per second over seconds is feet.
