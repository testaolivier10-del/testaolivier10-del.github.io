Checking three conditions at one point is fine for one point. But the big theorems ahead ask whether a function is continuous on a whole interval, which contains infinitely many points. This page shows how to answer that quickly: know which functions are continuous where, and check only the few places where something can go wrong.

Continuous on an interval

A function is continuous on an open interval $(a, b)$ when it is continuous at every point of it. On a closed interval $[a, b]$ there is one extra detail. At the endpoints, the function may not even be defined outside the interval, so we ask only about the side inside it:

  • at the left endpoint $a$: $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$ (continuous from the right);
  • at the right endpoint $b$: $\lim_{x \to b^-} f(x) = f(b)$ (continuous from the left).

So $\sqrt{x}$ is continuous on $[0, 4]$: there is nothing to the left of 0, but $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \sqrt{x} = 0 = \sqrt{0}$.

Functions that are continuous on their domains

You do not have to check every point by hand. These families are continuous at every point where they are defined:

FamilyExampleContinuous on
polynomials$x^3 - 2x + 7$$(-\infty, \infty)$
rational functions$\frac{x + 1}{x^2 - 4}$every $x$ where the denominator is not 0
roots$\sqrt{x - 1}$where the radicand is $\ge 0$ (even roots)
exponentials$e^x$, $2^x$$(-\infty, \infty)$
logarithms$\ln x$$(0, \infty)$
sine and cosine$\sin x$, $\cos x$$(-\infty, \infty)$
tangent$\tan x$wherever $\cos x \ne 0$, such as $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$

Combinations keep continuity. Sums, differences and products of continuous functions are continuous. A quotient is continuous wherever its denominator is not 0. A composition $f(g(x))$ is continuous wherever $g$ is continuous and $f$ is continuous at the value $g(x)$.

Finding where a function is continuous

Worked example 1. Where is $q(x) = \ln(x + 2) + \frac{1}{x - 3}$ continuous?

$\ln(x + 2)$ needs $x + 2 > 0$, so $x > -2$; there it is continuous.

$\frac{1}{x - 3}$ is continuous except at $x = 3$.

The sum is continuous where both parts are: on $(-2, 3)$ and on $(3, \infty)$.

Worked example 2. Let $f(x) = e^x$ for $x < 0$ and $f(x) = x + 1$ for $x \ge 0$. Where is $f$ continuous?

Each piece is continuous on its own interval ($e^x$ and $x + 1$ are continuous everywhere). The only question is the break point $x = 0$.

$f(0) = 0 + 1 = 1$. $\lim_{x \to 0^-} e^x = e^0 = 1$ and $\lim_{x \to 0^+} (x + 1) = 1$. So $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = 1 = f(0)$.

All three conditions hold at 0, so $f$ is continuous on $(-\infty, \infty)$.

A short procedure

  1. Identify each part of the function and its family.
  2. List the trouble spots: zeros of denominators, inputs where an even root or a logarithm is undefined, zeros of $\cos x$ under a $\tan x$, and break points of piecewise rules.
  3. Away from the trouble spots, the function is continuous by the families and the combination rules.
  4. At each break point of a piecewise rule, check the three conditions.
  5. Write the answer as intervals, with a bracket at an endpoint only when the function is defined there and continuous from inside.

Why does this matter so much? The Unit 1 finale, and several theorems in later units, start with "if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$". To use them, you will name the reason $f$ is continuous on that closed interval, usually with a sentence from this page: "$f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[1, 4]$."
