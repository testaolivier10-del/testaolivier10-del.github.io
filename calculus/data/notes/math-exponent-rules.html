Calculus has one short rule that handles every power of $x$: $x^2$, $x^{10}$, $\frac{1}{x^3}$, $\sqrt{x}$, $\sqrt[3]{x^2}$. The catch is that the rule only sees powers. So before you use it, you rewrite fractions and roots as exponents. This page is the toolkit for that rewrite, and a list of the slips that cost the most points.

The rules, and why they hold

An exponent counts repeated multiplication: $x^3 = x \cdot x \cdot x$. Every rule follows from counting factors. Multiply $x^3$ by $x^2$ and you have $3 + 2 = 5$ factors of $x$. Divide $x^5$ by $x^2$ and two factors cancel, leaving three.

RuleExample
$x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m+n}$$x^4 \cdot x^{-1} = x^3$
$\frac{x^m}{x^n} = x^{m-n}$$\frac{x^2}{x^5} = x^{-3}$
$(x^m)^n = x^{mn}$$(x^3)^2 = x^6$
$(ab)^n = a^n b^n$$(2x)^3 = 8x^3$
$x^0 = 1$ (for $x \ne 0$)$5^0 = 1$
$x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$$x^{-2} = \frac{1}{x^2}$
$x^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^m = \sqrt[n]{x^m}$$x^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{x^2}$

The last two rows are the ones calculus leans on, so let us slow down on them.

Negative exponents mean "one over"

Why should $x^{-2}$ be $\frac{1}{x^2}$? Use the division rule on a fraction you already know: $\frac{x^3}{x^5} = \frac{1}{x^2}$ by canceling, and $\frac{x^3}{x^5} = x^{3 - 5} = x^{-2}$ by the rule. Both are right, so $x^{-2} = \frac{1}{x^2}$.

A negative exponent never makes a number negative. $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8}$, a small positive number.

The most common slip is moving too much. An exponent applies only to the base it touches:

  • $3x^{-2} = \frac{3}{x^2}$. Only $x$ carries the exponent; the 3 stays on top.
  • $(3x)^{-2} = \frac{1}{(3x)^2} = \frac{1}{9x^2}$. Now the parentheses put the 3 under the exponent too.

Worked example 1. Write $f(x) = \frac{5}{x^3} - \frac{1}{2x}$ as a sum of powers of $x$.

First term: $\frac{5}{x^3} = 5x^{-3}$.

Second term: $\frac{1}{2x} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1}$. The 2 is a number in the denominator, so it stays as $\frac{1}{2}$; it does not get an exponent.

So $f(x) = 5x^{-3} - \frac{1}{2}x^{-1}$.

Fractional exponents mean roots

What number should $x^{1/2}$ be? Square it: $\left(x^{1/2}\right)^2 = x^{1/2 \cdot 2} = x^1$. So $x^{1/2}$ is the number whose square is $x$, which is $\sqrt{x}$. In the same way, $x^{1/3} = \sqrt[3]{x}$.

A fractional exponent $\frac{m}{n}$ does two things: the denominator $n$ is a root, the numerator $m$ is a power. Take the root first; the numbers stay small.

Worked example 2. Evaluate $8^{2/3}$ and $16^{-3/4}$.

$8^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4$.

$16^{-3/4} = \frac{1}{16^{3/4}} = \frac{1}{\left(\sqrt[4]{16}\right)^3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}$.

Worked example 3. Write $g(x) = \frac{x^2 + 1}{\sqrt{x}}$ and $h(x) = x\sqrt{x}$ as sums of powers.

A fraction with a single term on the bottom splits over the top: $g(x) = \frac{x^2}{x^{1/2}} + \frac{1}{x^{1/2}} = x^{3/2} + x^{-1/2}$.

$h(x) = x^1 \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{3/2}$.

These are exactly the rewrites you will do before using the Unit 2 rules.

The slips that cost points

Exponent rules work on products of a single base. They do not work across a plus sign. Here are the classic wrong moves; each one can be caught by testing a number.

WrongRightTest with a number
$(x + y)^2 = x^2 + y^2$$x^2 + 2xy + y^2$$(1 + 2)^2 = 9$, not 5
$\sqrt{x^2 + 9} = x + 3$does not simplify$x = 4$: $\sqrt{25} = 5$, not 7
$\frac{1}{x + 3} = x^{-1} + 3^{-1}$$(x + 3)^{-1}$$x = 1$: $\frac{1}{4}$, not $\frac{4}{3}$

Testing with a number is a habit worth building. It takes ten seconds and catches most algebra errors before they spread through a problem.

Which form to give as an answer

Write powers when you are about to do calculus on an expression. For a final answer, either form is fine on the exam: $x^{-1/2}$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ are equally correct. Do not spend time simplifying further than the question asks; the scoring guidelines reward the correct value, and extra simplifying is where new errors appear.
