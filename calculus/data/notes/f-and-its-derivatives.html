This topic pulls Unit 5 together. You are given one of $f$, $f'$ or $f''$, as a graph, a table or a formula, and asked about the others: where $f$ rises, where it peaks, how it bends, whether a tangent line over- or underestimates. Every year one free-response question is built this way around a graph of $f'$. The skill is graphical analysis: reading the right feature of the right function and saying so by name.

Always ask which function the given information is about. "Positive" means something different for $f$, $f'$ and $f''$.

Working from a graph of $f'$

Worked example 1. The graph of $f'(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2$ on $[-1, 3.2]$ has a high point at $x = 0$, a low point at $x = 2$, and crosses the axis at $x \approx -0.73$, $x = 1$ and $x \approx 2.73$. Also $f(0) = 4$.

Relative maximum of $f$: at $x = 1$, because $f'$ changes from positive to negative there.

Concave up: on $(-1, 0)$ and $(2, 3.2)$, because $f'$ is increasing there.

Inflection points: at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$, because $f'$ changes from increasing to decreasing at 0 and from decreasing to increasing at 2.

At $x = 2$: $f'(2) = -2 < 0$, so $f$ is decreasing there, and it has a point of inflection.

Tangent line at $x = 0$: point $(0, 4)$, slope $f'(0) = 2$, so $y = 4 + 2x$.

Notice the pattern in every justification: it names $f'$ and says what $f'$ does. "The graph changes direction at 1" names nothing and earns nothing.

Working from a table

A table gives exact values at a few points. Use the tests that need only those values, and the theorems that connect them.

Worked example 2. $f$ is twice differentiable and $f''$ is continuous.

$x$$1$$3$$4$
$f(x)$$1$$4$$2$
$f'(x)$$0$$0$$-4$
$f''(x)$$2$$-3$$-1$

At $x = 1$: $f'(1) = 0$ and $f''(1) > 0$, so by the Second Derivative Test $f$ has a relative minimum.

Inflection in $(1, 3)$: $f''$ is continuous, $f''(1) > 0$ and $f''(3) < 0$, so $f''$ changes from positive to negative somewhere in $(1, 3)$: the graph has a point of inflection there.

Approximating $f(4.2)$: $y = 2 - 4(x - 4)$ gives $f(4.2) \approx 1.2$. Because $f''(4) < 0$ and $f''$ is continuous, $f$ is concave down near 4, the graph lies below the tangent line, and $1.2$ is an overestimate.

Working from words

Contexts describe derivatives in words. Translate before you calculate:

  • "Increasing at a decreasing rate": $f' > 0$ and $f'' < 0$.
  • "Decreasing, but more and more slowly": $f' < 0$ and $f'' > 0$.
  • "The rate of change is greatest": $f'$ has a maximum, which is where $f''$ changes from positive to negative, an inflection point of $f$.
  • For motion, position $s$: velocity $s'$ and acceleration $s''$. With $s' > 0$ and $s'' < 0$, the object moves forward and slows down.

A global fact from $f''$

Sometimes the sign of $f''$ holds everywhere, and that gives a global answer. If $f''(x) < 0$ for all $x$ and $f'(2) = 0$, then $f'$ is decreasing on the whole line: positive before 2 and negative after. So $f$ rises to $x = 2$ and falls after, and $f(2)$ is the absolute maximum, not just a relative one.

The two classic swaps

  • Zeros versus turns. On a graph of $f'$, zeros (where it crosses the axis) are candidates for extrema of $f$; turns (its peaks and valleys) are inflection points of $f$.
  • Sign versus direction. The sign of $f'$ gives the direction of $f$; the direction of $f'$ gives the concavity of $f$. "f is increasing because $f'$ is increasing" mixes them up.
