Some of the most memorable problems in calculus are pictures: a ladder sliding down a wall, a cone-shaped tank filling with water, a person walking away from a streetlight while the shadow grows. The calculus in them is short. What takes the time, and where most mistakes happen, is the geometry: writing the one equation that connects the quantities in the picture. This page gathers the geometry you need and shows the two tools that do most of the work.

A quick check catches many slips: areas have squared units and volumes have cubed units. If your "volume" formula has $r^2$ and nothing else with a length, something is missing.

The Pythagorean theorem

Whenever a picture has a right angle, the Pythagorean theorem links the three sides: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, where $c$ is the hypotenuse, opposite the right angle. Calculus problems use it as an equation between changing quantities.

Worked example 1. A 10-foot ladder leans against a wall. Let $x$ be the distance from the wall to its foot and $y$ the height of its top. Write an equation, and find $y$ when $x = 6$.

The wall meets the ground at a right angle, and the ladder is the hypotenuse: $x^2 + y^2 = 100$.

When $x = 6$: $36 + y^2 = 100$, so $y = 8$ feet.

The equation $x^2 + y^2 = 100$ holds at every moment as the ladder slides; that is what Unit 4 will use.

The same theorem gives the distance between two cars moving along perpendicular roads, the length of a kite string, and the height of an equilateral triangle.

Similar triangles

Two triangles are similar when they have the same three angles. Then one is a scaled copy of the other, and every pair of matching sides has the same ratio. Look for a small triangle sitting inside a big one, sharing an angle.

Worked example 2. A cone-shaped tank, point down, is 10 feet tall with a top radius of 4 feet. Write the volume of water as a function of its depth $h$.

The water is a small cone inside the tank. Viewed from the side, the water's triangle and the tank's triangle are similar: $\frac{r}{h} = \frac{4}{10}$, so $r = \frac{2}{5}h$.

Substitute into the cone formula: $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{2}{5}h\right)^2 h = \frac{4\pi}{75}h^3$.

Now $V$ depends on one variable only. Replacing $r$ with $\frac{2}{5}h$ before doing any calculus is the key step.

Worked example 3. A person 6 feet tall stands 10 feet from a lamppost 15 feet tall. Find the length $s$ of the shadow.

The light, the top of the person's head and the tip of the shadow line up. The big triangle has height 15 and base $10 + s$; the small triangle has height 6 and base $s$.

$\frac{6}{s} = \frac{15}{10 + s}$, so $6(10 + s) = 15s$, so $60 = 9s$, and $s = \frac{20}{3}$ feet.

The most common slip is using 10 as the big triangle's base instead of $10 + s$.

Right-triangle trigonometry

When an angle is part of the question, use the right-triangle ratios: $\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$, $\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$, $\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$. A balloon rising straight up from a point 50 meters from an observer makes an angle of elevation $\theta$ with $\tan\theta = \frac{h}{50}$.

A routine for picture problems

  • Draw the picture, even if one is given.
  • Label every quantity that changes with a letter and every fixed quantity with its number.
  • Find the right angle or the similar triangles, and write one equation.
  • If the formula has too many variables, use a ratio or the Pythagorean theorem to eliminate one.

Substitute a specific moment's numbers (like $x = 6$) only at the end. If you put them in too early, the equation stops describing the changing picture.
