You now know where a function rises and where it falls. Put two intervals side by side and a peak or valley appears between them. The First Derivative Test is that observation, stated carefully enough to use as a justification. It is one of the most scored sentences on the whole exam.

The test

Let $c$ be a critical point of $f$, and let $f$ be continuous at $c$.

  • If $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $c$, then $f$ has a relative maximum at $c$. The graph rises up to $c$, then falls.
  • If $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $c$, then $f$ has a relative minimum at $c$. The graph falls, then rises.
  • If $f'$ does not change sign at $c$, then $f$ has no relative extremum at $c$. The graph keeps going the same way.

Notice what the test does not use: the value $f'(c)$. It works the same at a point where $f'(c) = 0$ and at a corner or cusp where $f'(c)$ does not exist. What matters is the sign just before and just after.

Sign charts

A sign chart is a number line marked with the critical points and the sign of $f'$ on each interval between them. It is a good way to organize the work:

Worked example 1. Find the relative extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 4$.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x - 3)(x + 1)$, zero at $x = -1$ and $x = 3$.

Test values: $f'(-2) = 15 > 0$, $f'(0) = -9 < 0$, $f'(4) = 15 > 0$. Sign chart: $+$, then $-$ after $-1$, then $+$ after $3$.

At $x = -1$, $f'$ changes from positive to negative: relative maximum, $f(-1) = -1 - 3 + 9 + 4 = 9$.

At $x = 3$, $f'$ changes from negative to positive: relative minimum, $f(3) = 27 - 27 - 27 + 4 = -23$.

But the chart is scratch work. The scoring guidelines do not accept a sign chart by itself as a justification. Write the sentence: "$f'$ changes from positive to negative at $x = -1$, so $f$ has a relative maximum there."

Points where $f'$ does not exist

Worked example 2. $f(x) = x^{5/3} - 5x^{2/3}$. Find its relative extrema.

$f'(x) = \frac{5}{3}x^{2/3} - \frac{10}{3}x^{-1/3} = \frac{5x - 10}{3x^{1/3}} = \frac{5(x - 2)}{3x^{1/3}}$.

Critical points: $x = 2$ (numerator zero) and $x = 0$ ($f'$ undefined; $f$ is defined and continuous there).

Signs: for $x < 0$, both $x - 2$ and $x^{1/3}$ are negative, so $f' > 0$. For $0 < x < 2$, $f' < 0$. For $x > 2$, $f' > 0$.

So $f$ has a relative maximum at $x = 0$ ($f'$ changes from positive to negative at a cusp) and a relative minimum at $x = 2$.

Zeros that are not extrema

A squared factor in $f'$ is the usual reason a critical point is not an extremum. For $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$:

$$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2(x - 3)$$

The factor $4x^2$ is never negative, so the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $x - 3$. At $x = 0$, $f'$ is negative on both sides: no extremum, even though $f'(0) = 0$. At $x = 3$, $f'$ changes from negative to positive: a relative minimum.

Reading the test from a graph of $f'$

What the graph of $f'$ does at $x = c$What $f$ has at $x = c$
Crosses the axis going down ($+$ to $-$)relative maximum
Crosses the axis going up ($-$ to $+$)relative minimum
Touches the axis and turns back (no sign change)no extremum
Has a peak or valley above or below the axisno extremum (that is about concavity)

The sentence to write

Name the function, name the point, give the direction of the change:

"$f'$ changes from negative to positive at $x = 5$, so $f$ has a relative minimum at $x = 5$."

Answers that lose the point: "$f'(5) = 0$" (no sign change), "it changes sign" (which function? which direction?), or "the graph crosses the axis" (which graph?). And remember this test is local: it finds relative extrema, not absolute ones. For the absolute maximum on an interval, you need the next topic.
