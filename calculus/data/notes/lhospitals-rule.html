In Unit 1 you met limits like $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}$, where both the top and the bottom approach 0. The form $\frac{0}{0}$ tells you nothing by itself: the limit might be 1, or 7, or not exist. Back then you needed algebra tricks or a squeeze. Now that you have derivatives, one rule handles most of these limits, and it comes from the idea you just used for tangent line approximations.

Why slopes decide a $\frac{0}{0}$ limit

Suppose $f(a) = 0$ and $g(a) = 0$, both differentiable. Near $a$, each function is close to its tangent line:

$$f(x) \approx f'(a)(x - a), \qquad g(x) \approx g'(a)(x - a)$$

So their quotient is close to $\frac{f'(a)(x - a)}{g'(a)(x - a)} = \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}$. Both functions shrink to 0, but at rates set by their slopes, and the ratio of the slopes is what survives.

L'Hospital's rule

If $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to a}g(x) = 0$, or if both limits are infinite, then

$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

provided the limit on the right exists (or is infinite). The same holds for limits as $x \to \infty$. Three warnings:

  • Check the form first. The rule needs $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. On the exam, show it: write both limits.
  • Differentiate the top and the bottom separately. This is not the quotient rule.
  • Never write "$= \frac{0}{0}$". It is a form, not a number. Write "$\lim(1 - \cos x) = 0$ and $\lim x^2 = 0$" instead.

Worked example 1. Find $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{e^{3x} - 1}{x}$.

Form: $\lim_{x \to 0}(e^{3x} - 1) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to 0}x = 0$.

By L'Hospital's rule, the limit equals $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{3e^{3x}}{1} = 3$.

Worked example 2. Find $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2}$.

Form: both limits are 0. First application: $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{2x}$.

Check again: $\sin 0 = 0$ and $2 \cdot 0 = 0$, still $\frac{0}{0}$. Second application: $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\cos x}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$.

When the form is not indeterminate

Using the rule on a limit that is not $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ gives wrong answers. For $\lim_{x \to 1}\frac{x^2 + 1}{x + 1}$, the form is $\frac{2}{2}$, so the limit is simply 1. Blindly differentiating gives $\lim\frac{2x}{1} = 2$, which is wrong. A check of the form takes five seconds and saves the point.

From a table of values

Worked example 3. $f$ and $g$ are differentiable with $f(2) = 0$, $g(2) = 0$, $f'(2) = 5$ and $g'(2) = -2$. Find $\lim_{x \to 2}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$.

Differentiable functions are continuous, so $\lim_{x \to 2}f(x) = f(2) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to 2}g(x) = g(2) = 0$.

By L'Hospital's rule, the limit is $\lim_{x \to 2}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} = \frac{5}{-2} = -\frac{5}{2}$ (using that $f'$ and $g'$ are continuous at 2, as exam questions state or imply).

The form $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$: comparing growth

As $x \to \infty$, the rule compares how fast two functions grow.

LimitRule appliedResultMeaning
$\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{\ln x}{x}$$\frac{1/x}{1}$$0$$x$ outgrows $\ln x$
$\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{x^2}{e^x}$$\frac{2x}{e^x}$, then $\frac{2}{e^x}$$0$$e^x$ outgrows $x^2$
$\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{3x^2 + 1}{5x^2 - x}$$\frac{6x}{10x - 1}$, then $\frac{6}{10}$$\frac{3}{5}$same growth: ratio of leading coefficients

Slips that cost points

  • Applying the rule without showing that both limits are 0 (or both infinite).
  • Using the quotient rule instead of differentiating top and bottom separately.
  • Writing $\frac{0}{0} = $ a number, or "$= \frac{0}{0}$" as if it were a value.
  • Forgetting the chain rule inside: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(5x) = 5\cos(5x)$.
  • Stopping after one round when the new limit is still indeterminate.
