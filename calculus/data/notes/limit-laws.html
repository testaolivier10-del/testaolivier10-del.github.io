So far you have estimated limits by looking: at a graph, at a table. Now you will find them exactly, with rules. The good news is that the rules are the ones you would guess. Limits respect ordinary arithmetic. If you know where $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are heading, you know where $f(x) + g(x)$, $f(x)g(x)$ and many other combinations are heading. For most functions in this course, the rules boil down to one step: substitute.

The limit laws

Suppose $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$, and both of these limits exist. Then:

LawIn symbolsCondition
Sum and difference$\lim_{x \to a} \left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] = L \pm M$
Constant multiple$\lim_{x \to a} k f(x) = kL$
Product$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) g(x) = LM$
Quotient$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}$$M \ne 0$
Power$\lim_{x \to a} \left[f(x)\right]^n = L^n$$n$ a positive integer
Root$\lim_{x \to a} \sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{L}$$L > 0$ for even roots

Two simple limits start everything off: $\lim_{x \to a} k = k$ for a constant $k$, and $\lim_{x \to a} x = a$. Both are obvious from their graphs, a flat line and the line $y = x$.

Worked example 1. Given $\lim_{x \to 4} f(x) = 3$ and $\lim_{x \to 4} g(x) = -1$, find $\lim_{x \to 4} \frac{2f(x) - [g(x)]^2}{f(x) + g(x)}$.

Numerator: $2(3) - (-1)^2 = 6 - 1 = 5$, by the constant multiple, power and difference laws.

Denominator: $3 + (-1) = 2$, which is not 0, so the quotient law applies.

So the limit is $\frac{5}{2}$.

Direct substitution

Every polynomial is built from constants and $x$ using sums, products and constant multiples. The laws therefore give, for any polynomial $p$, $$\lim_{x \to a} p(x) = p(a)$$ The same holds for a rational function $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ as long as $q(a) \ne 0$, and for roots of these where the expression is defined. Finding a limit by evaluating at $a$ is called direct substitution. It is the first thing to try on every limit.

Worked example 2. Find $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^3 - 4x + 1}{x + 3}$.

This is a rational function, and the denominator at $x = 2$ is $5 \ne 0$. Substitute:

$$\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^3 - 4x + 1}{x + 3} = \frac{8 - 8 + 1}{2 + 3} = \frac{1}{5}$$

Why does this not contradict the idea that a limit ignores $f(a)$? It doesn't. For these well-behaved functions the limit and the value simply happen to agree. The laws prove they agree; the value is not being used as the definition.

When the laws are silent

The laws come with conditions, and the conditions are where exam questions live.

  • Denominator heading to 0, numerator not. For $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x + 1}{x - 2}$, the top heads to 3 and the bottom to 0. Numbers near 3 divided by numbers near 0 are huge in size, so there is no finite limit. Writing $\frac{3}{0}$ is never a value; say it in words.
  • Both top and bottom heading to 0. For $\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$, substitution gives a zero in both places, which tells you nothing yet. The limit might be any number, or might not exist. Topic 1.6 shows the algebra that settles it.
  • One of the limits does not exist. The laws need both limits to exist. If they do not, the laws do not apply, but the combination might still have a limit. Two jumps can cancel each other in a sum.

Worked example 3. $f$ jumps at $x = 1$: it heads to 2 from the left and 0 from the right. $g$ heads to 1 from the left and 3 from the right. Find $\lim_{x \to 1} [f(x) + g(x)]$ and $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)g(x)$.

Neither $f$ nor $g$ has a limit at 1, so use one side at a time.

Sum: from the left $2 + 1 = 3$; from the right $0 + 3 = 3$. So $\lim_{x \to 1} [f(x) + g(x)] = 3$.

Product: from the left $2 \cdot 1 = 2$; from the right $0 \cdot 3 = 0$. They differ, so $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)g(x)$ does not exist.

One-sided limits from formulas

The laws work for one-sided limits too. For a piecewise function, each one-sided limit uses the formula for that side.

Worked example 4. Let $f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 - 1 & x < 3 \\ 2x + 2 & x \ge 3 \end{cases}$. Find $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$.

Left: $\lim_{x \to 3^-} (x^2 - 1) = 9 - 1 = 8$. Right: $\lim_{x \to 3^+} (2x + 2) = 8$.

Both are 8, so $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 8$.

Limits of compositions

For $\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x))$, work from the inside out. First find $b = \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$. If $f$ is continuous at $b$ (its graph is unbroken there), then $f(g(x))$ heads to $f(b)$: $$\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f\left(\lim_{x \to a} g(x)\right) = f(b)$$ For example, $\lim_{x \to 3} \sqrt{x^2 + 7} = \sqrt{16} = 4$. With graphs, read the inside limit from the graph of $g$, then read $f$ near that number from the graph of $f$. If $f$ jumps at $b$, check which side the inside values approach from.
