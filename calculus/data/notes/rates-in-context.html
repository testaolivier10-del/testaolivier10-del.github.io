Motion is one story the derivative tells. It tells many others: how fast a medicine leaves the blood, how fast a town grows, how much one more kilogram of coffee adds to a roaster's cost. The calculus is identical in every case. What you must get right is the language: which quantity, increasing or decreasing, how fast, and in what units. This page also handles the second derivative in context, the rate at which a rate changes.

One pattern, many settings

QuantityIts derivative meansUnits of the derivative

The sentence is always built the same way: "At [input value], [the quantity] is increasing (or decreasing) at [the size of the derivative] [units]."

Worked example 1. A town's population is $P(t) = 500e^{0.02t}$ people, $t$ years after 2020. Find and interpret $P'(10)$.

By the chain rule, $P'(t) = 500 \cdot 0.02\,e^{0.02t} = 10e^{0.02t}$, so $P'(10) = 10e^{0.2} \approx 12.2$.

In 2030 the population is increasing at about 12.2 people per year.

Worked example 2. The concentration of a medicine is $C(t) = \frac{8t}{t^2 + 4}$ mg/L, $t$ hours after a dose. Find and interpret $C'(1)$ and $C'(3)$.

Quotient rule: $C'(t) = \frac{8(t^2 + 4) - 8t \cdot 2t}{(t^2 + 4)^2} = \frac{32 - 8t^2}{(t^2 + 4)^2}$.

$C'(1) = \frac{24}{25} = 0.96$: one hour after the dose, the concentration is increasing at 0.96 mg/L per hour.

$C'(3) = \frac{32 - 72}{169} = -\frac{40}{169} \approx -0.24$: after three hours it is decreasing at about 0.24 mg/L per hour.

Rates of rates

A derivative is a function, so it has its own rate of change, the second derivative. In context, its units carry the input unit twice: if $H$ is in °C and $t$ in minutes, then $H''(t)$ is in °C per minute per minute.

Read the two signs separately. The sign of $H'$ says whether the temperature rises or falls. The sign of $H''$ says whether that rate is growing or shrinking:

$Q'$$Q''$In words
$+$$+$increasing, faster and faster
$+$$-$increasing, more and more slowly
$-$$-$decreasing, faster and faster
$-$$+$decreasing, more and more slowly

Worked example 3. Soup cools by $H(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.04t}$ °C. Describe the cooling at $t = 10$.

$H'(t) = -2.8e^{-0.04t}$, so $H'(10) \approx -1.877$ °C per minute: the soup is cooling.

$H''(t) = 0.112e^{-0.04t}$, so $H''(10) \approx 0.075$ °C per minute per minute: positive, so the rate of cooling is easing off.

Together: at 10 minutes the soup is cooling at about 1.88 °C per minute, and the cooling is slowing down.

Watch for a function that is already a rate. If water leaks out of a tank at $L(t)$ liters per hour, then $L'(3) = -2$ means the leak rate is decreasing at 2 liters per hour per hour. It says nothing directly about the amount of water left.

Chained quantities

Sometimes one quantity depends on another that depends on time. A balloon's temperature reading depends on its altitude, and its altitude depends on time. The chain rule multiplies the rates, and the units cancel the same way:

$$\frac{d}{dt}T(h(t)) = T'(h(t)) \cdot h'(t), \qquad \frac{\text{°F}}{\text{foot}} \cdot \frac{\text{feet}}{\text{minute}} = \frac{\text{°F}}{\text{minute}}$$

Checking that the units cancel is a fast way to catch a setup error.

Slips that cost points

  • Reporting the amount ($H(10)$) when the question asks for its rate ($H'(10)$).
  • Giving a second derivative the units of the first ("°C per minute" for $H''$).
  • Reading the sign of $Q''$ as whether $Q$ itself increases.
  • Dropping the chain rule factor in models like $e^{-0.04t}$, which also flips the sign.
  • On a calculator question, rounding before the end, or giving fewer than three decimal places.
