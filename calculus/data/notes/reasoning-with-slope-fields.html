Once a slope field is drawn, you can read it like a weather map. It shows where solutions rise and fall, where they level off, and where they head in the long run, all without a formula. This page shows how to sketch the solution curve through a point, how to spot equilibrium solutions, and how to find the concavity of a solution from the differential equation itself.

Sketching the solution through a point

Put your pencil on the given point. The segment there shows which way to go. Move a little, look at the nearby segments, adjust, and keep going. Then go back to the point and do the same in the other direction. Rules that keep the sketch honest:

  • Pass exactly through the given point. The point $(0, 5)$ has $x = 0$ and $y = 5$; do not start at $(5, 0)$.
  • Follow the segments: the curve should be tangent to the segments it passes, never cutting across them.
  • Extend the curve across the whole field shown, to the left and to the right of the point.
  • Do not cross a row of horizontal segments that forms an equilibrium solution (next section).

Equilibrium solutions

Sometimes a whole horizontal line is made of flat segments. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3 - y}{2}$, every point with $y = 3$ has slope 0. Then the constant function $y = 3$ is itself a solution: its derivative is 0, and the right side is 0 too. A constant solution like this is an equilibrium solution.

To find them, set the right side equal to 0 and solve for $y$. The answer must work for every $x$. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2(y - 3)(y + 1)$ the equilibrium solutions are $y = 3$ and $y = -1$. The factor $x^2$ also makes the slope 0 along $x = 0$, but a vertical line is not a function of $x$, so it is not a solution.

Equilibrium solutions organize everything else. For the equations in this course, two solution curves never cross. So a solution that starts below $y = 3$ stays below it. In the figure, slopes below 3 are positive and get gentler near 3, so the curve through $(0, 1)$ rises and flattens toward 3. Above 3 the slopes are negative, so the curve through $(0, 5.5)$ falls toward 3. Both approach 3 as $x$ grows.

Not every equilibrium attracts. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 1}{2}$, slopes are positive above $y = 1$ and negative below it, so solutions move away from $y = 1$ on both sides. A solution through $(0, 2)$ rises faster and faster; one through $(0, 0)$ falls without bound.

Increasing, decreasing and extrema

The sign of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ says where a solution rises or falls, exactly as for any function. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = x(2 - y)$ and points below $y = 2$, the factor $2 - y$ is positive, so the slope has the sign of $x$: negative left of the $y$-axis, positive right of it. A solution crossing the $y$-axis below $y = 2$ therefore has a relative minimum there, because $\frac{dy}{dx}$ changes from negative to positive.

Concavity: the second derivative by the chain rule

To decide whether a solution curve is concave up or down, you need $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. Differentiate the right side of the equation with respect to $x$, remembering that $y$ is a function of $x$. So every $y$ you differentiate brings a factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$, and then you replace $\frac{dy}{dx}$ by the right side of the equation.

Worked example. A solution of $\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - x$ passes through $(1, 2)$. Is it concave up or down there?

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 2y\frac{dy}{dx} - 1$. The $2y\frac{dy}{dx}$ comes from the chain rule on $y^2$.

At $(1, 2)$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2^2 - 1 = 3$. So $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 2(2)(3) - 1 = 11$.

Since $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 11 > 0$, the solution curve is concave up at $(1, 2)$.

The classic slip is to write $\frac{d}{dx}(y^2) = 2y$, or even 0 "because there is no $x$". Both forget that $y$ changes as $x$ changes. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 1}{2}$ the correct second derivative is $\frac{1}{2}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 1}{4}$, not 0.

Worked example 2. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = x(2 - y)$, find $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ in terms of $x$ and $y$.

Product rule: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 1 \cdot (2 - y) + x \cdot \left(-\frac{dy}{dx}\right)$.

Substitute $\frac{dy}{dx} = x(2 - y)$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = (2 - y) - x^2(2 - y) = (2 - y)(1 - x^2)$.

Long-run behavior

In context, an equilibrium solution is often a value the quantity settles at. A drink with $\frac{dT}{dt} = -0.1(T - 65)$ warms or cools toward 65 °F, the room temperature. Read the long run from the signs: below the equilibrium the rate is positive, above it negative, so the temperature approaches 65 from either side.
