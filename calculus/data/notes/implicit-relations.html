A circle, an oval or a looping curve fails the vertical line test, so it is not the graph of a function $y = f(x)$. You already know how to find its slope with implicit differentiation. This topic asks what that slope formula tells you about the shape: where the curve is flat, where it stands straight up, and where it has a local high or low point.

The slope formula as a fraction

Differentiate both sides of the curve's equation with respect to $x$, write "$= 0$" (or the derivative of the right side), and solve. The result usually looks like a fraction in both $x$ and $y$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{N(x, y)}{D(x, y)}$$

Worked example 1. Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2 - xy + y^2 = 12$.

$\frac{d}{dx}$ of both sides: $2x - \left(y + x\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$. (The product rule on $xy$ gives two terms.)

Collect the $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms: $\frac{dy}{dx}(2y - x) = y - 2x$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 2x}{2y - x}$.

Horizontal and vertical tangent lines

A horizontal tangent needs slope 0, so the numerator must be 0 while the denominator is not. A vertical tangent line needs the slope to be undefined because the denominator is 0 while the numerator is not. In both cases the point must actually be on the curve.

Worked example 2. Find the points of $x^2 - xy + y^2 = 12$ with horizontal and with vertical tangent lines.

Horizontal: $y - 2x = 0$, so $y = 2x$. Substitute into the curve: $x^2 - 2x^2 + 4x^2 = 12$, so $3x^2 = 12$ and $x = \pm 2$. Points $(2, 4)$ and $(-2, -4)$; the denominator there is $6$ and $-6$, not 0.

Vertical: $2y - x = 0$, so $x = 2y$. Substitute: $4y^2 - 2y^2 + y^2 = 12$, so $y = \pm 2$. Points $(4, 2)$ and $(-4, -2)$; the numerator there is $-6$ and $6$, not 0.

The substitution step is the one students skip. The point $(1, 2)$ makes the numerator $y - 2x$ zero, but $1 - 2 + 4 = 3 \ne 12$, so it is not on the curve, and its "slope" means nothing.

When both parts are zero

For the curve $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$, implicit differentiation gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}$. At the origin, which is on the curve, both the numerator and the denominator are 0. The formula is undefined and decides nothing; this curve actually crosses itself there. Never write "$\frac{0}{0} = 0$" or "$\frac{0}{0} = 1$": that is a notation error.

High and low points of a curve

Near a point with a horizontal tangent, the curve is the graph of a function of $x$, so the Second Derivative Test still works. Find $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ by differentiating the fraction for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (quotient rule, treating $y$ as a function of $x$), then substitute the point and the value $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$.

Worked example 3. For $x^2 - xy + y^2 = 12$, find $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ at $(2, 4)$.

$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\left(\frac{dy}{dx} - 2\right)(2y - x) - (y - 2x)\left(2\frac{dy}{dx} - 1\right)}{(2y - x)^2}$.

At $(2, 4)$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ and $y - 2x = 0$: $\frac{(-2)(6) - 0}{36} = -\frac{1}{3}$.

A horizontal tangent where the curve is concave down: $(2, 4)$ is a local high point of the curve, and in fact its highest point.

What earns the points

  • Apply $\frac{d}{dx}$ to both sides and keep "$= 0$" on the right.
  • Use the product rule on every $xy$-type term and the chain rule on every power of $y$.
  • For a vertical tangent, say "the denominator is 0 and the numerator is not," never "$\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$."
  • Substitute candidate points back into the curve's equation.
