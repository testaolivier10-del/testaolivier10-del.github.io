Turning the strips sideways

In the last topic every rectangle stood up: it had width $\Delta x$ and reached from a bottom curve to a top curve. That works well when the top is one curve and the bottom is one curve all the way across. Some regions are not built like that.

Look at the region between the parabola $x = y^2$ and the line $x = y + 2$ in the figure. Slice it vertically and the bottom of each strip is the lower half of the parabola for $0 \le x \le 1$, but the line for $1 \le x \le 4$. You would need two integrals and the formulas $y = -\sqrt{x}$ and $y = \sqrt{x}$. Slice it horizontally instead, and every strip runs from the parabola on the left to the line on the right. One formula, one integral.

Right minus left

A horizontal strip sits at height $y$ and has a small thickness $\Delta y$. Its length is the distance from its left end to its right end, which is a difference of $x$-values:

$$\text{length} = x_{\text{right}}(y) - x_{\text{left}}(y).$$

Each strip has area $\left(x_{\text{right}} - x_{\text{left}}\right)\Delta y$. Stack the strips from the bottom of the region to the top and let them get thin:

$$A = \int_c^d \left(x_{\text{right}}(y) - x_{\text{left}}(y)\right)\,dy.$$

This is the same idea as top minus bottom, turned 90 degrees. The "larger" function is now the one farther to the right.

Two things change with the variable. The edges must be written as $x$ in terms of $y$, and the limits are $y$-values. If a curve is given as $y = \sqrt{x}$, solve for $x$ (here $x = y^2$ with $y \ge 0$). That is just the inverse function.

Worked example. Find the area of the region between $x = y^2$ and $x = y + 2$.

Limits. Set the edges equal: $y^2 = y + 2$, so $y^2 - y - 2 = 0$ and $(y - 2)(y + 1) = 0$. The curves meet at $y = -1$ and $y = 2$. (The points are $(1, -1)$ and $(4, 2)$, but the limits are their $y$-coordinates.)

Right edge. Test $y = 0$: the line gives $x = 2$ and the parabola gives $x = 0$. The line is on the right.

Integral.

$$A = \int_{-1}^{2} \left[(y + 2) - y^2\right]\,dy = \left[\frac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{2} = \frac{10}{3} - \left(-\frac{7}{6}\right) = \frac{9}{2}.$$

With vertical strips you would need $\int_0^1 2\sqrt{x}\,dx + \int_1^4 \left(\sqrt{x} - (x - 2)\right)\,dx = \frac{4}{3} + \frac{19}{6}$, which is also $\frac{9}{2}$. Same area, twice the work.

Either choice gives the same area if you set it up correctly. Pick the one with fewer pieces and easier antiderivatives.

Worked example. Find the area of the region bounded by $y = \ln x$, the $y$-axis, $y = 0$ and $y = 2$.

Rewrite. $y = \ln x$ means $x = e^y$. The left edge is the $y$-axis, $x = 0$.

Limits. The region runs from $y = 0$ to $y = 2$; they are given.

Integral. $A = \int_0^2 \left(e^y - 0\right)\,dy = e^2 - e^0 = e^2 - 1$. In $x$ this region would need $x$ from 0 to $e^2$ and a top edge that changes at $x = 1$: much harder.

Common slips

  • Limits in the wrong variable. In $dy$ the limits are $y$-values. Writing the $x$-coordinates of the crossings as limits gives a wrong number.
  • Left minus right. That gives a negative number. Test a $y$ between the limits in both edges; the larger $x$ is the right edge.
  • Edges not rewritten. $\sqrt{x}$ inside an integral in $dy$ makes no sense. Every edge must be $x$ in terms of $y$.
  • Parentheses. $(2 - y) - (y^2 - 4) = 6 - y - y^2$: subtract every term of the left edge.
