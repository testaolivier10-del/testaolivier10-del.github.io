So far every function came as $y = $ a formula in $x$. Many curves do not. The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ and the tilted oval $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$ mix $x$ and $y$ in one equation, and solving for $y$ is either messy or gives two formulas. Implicit differentiation finds the slope at a point straight from the equation, with no solving for $y$ first.

Explicit and implicit

A function is given explicitly when $y$ stands alone: $y = \sqrt{25 - x^2}$. It is given implicitly when an equation links $x$ and $y$, such as $x^2 + y^2 = 25$. The whole circle fails the vertical line test, so it is not the graph of one function. But zoom in near the point $(3, 4)$ and you see a small arc that does pass the test. On that arc, $y$ is a function of $x$, even if we never write its formula, and its slope is what we want.

The key step: the chain rule on $y$

Treat $y$ as an unknown function of $x$. Then a term like $y^2$ is a composite function: the inside is $y$, the outside is the square. By the chain rule,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(y^2\right) = 2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}$$

The factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is the derivative of the inside function. It appears every time you differentiate a term that holds $y$, and never for a term that holds only $x$:

TermDerivative with respect to $x$Why
$x^3$$3x^2$only $x$: no extra factor
$y^3$$3y^2\frac{dy}{dx}$chain rule, inside $y$
$\sin y$$\cos y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}$chain rule, inside $y$
$e^y$$e^y\frac{dy}{dx}$chain rule, inside $y$
$xy$$y + x\frac{dy}{dx}$product rule; the $y$ factor gives $\frac{dy}{dx}$
$7$$0$a constant

The routine

  1. Apply $\frac{d}{dx}$ to both sides of the equation and write the new equation in full, with its right side (often $= 0$).
  2. Move every term that holds $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to one side and everything else to the other.
  3. Factor out $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and divide.
  4. For a slope at a point, substitute both coordinates after differentiating.

Worked example 1. Find the slope of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at $(3, 4)$.

Differentiate both sides: $2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$.

Solve: $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{2x}{2y} = -\frac{x}{y}$.

At $(3, 4)$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{3}{4}$. Check with the picture: at the upper right of the circle the curve falls as $x$ grows, so a negative slope makes sense.

Worked example 2. Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$, and the slope at $(1, 2)$.

Check the point first: $1 + 2 + 4 = 7$, so $(1, 2)$ is on the curve.

Differentiate both sides, using the product rule on $xy$: $2x + \left(y + x\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$.

Collect: $(x + 2y)\frac{dy}{dx} = -(2x + y)$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{2x + y}{x + 2y}$.

At $(1, 2)$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{2 + 2}{1 + 4} = -\frac{4}{5}$. The tangent line is $y - 2 = -\frac{4}{5}(x - 1)$.

Worked example 3. Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $e^y + y = x^2$.

Differentiate: $e^y\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x$. Both terms on the left hold $y$, so both get $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

Factor: $(e^y + 1)\frac{dy}{dx} = 2x$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{e^y + 1}$.

Reading the answer

The formula for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ usually holds both $x$ and $y$. That is not a flaw: on the circle, the points $(3, 4)$ and $(3, -4)$ share $x = 3$ but have slopes $-\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{3}{4}$. You need both coordinates to know which point you mean, and the point must lie on the curve.

A fraction for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ also shows special points. Where the numerator is 0 and the denominator is not, the slope is 0 and the tangent line is flat. Where the denominator is 0 and the numerator is not, the slope is undefined and the tangent line is vertical, as at $(-5, 0)$ and $(5, 0)$ on the circle. Always check that such a point is actually on the curve.

Slips that cost points

  • Forgetting $\frac{dy}{dx}$ on a $y$-term. Writing $2x + 2y = 0$ for the circle loses the whole problem.
  • Skipping the product rule. $\frac{d}{dx}(xy)$ is $y + x\frac{dy}{dx}$, two terms, not $\frac{dy}{dx}$ alone.
  • Differentiating one side only. Apply $\frac{d}{dx}$ to both sides, and keep the "$= 0$".
  • Substituting too early. Put in the point's coordinates only after differentiating; a number has derivative 0.
  • Mixing notations. Keep $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (or $y'$) separate from $dy$ and from $\frac{dy}{dt}$.
