Substitution and the basic rules cover most integrals, but a few fractions need a little algebra first. This page covers the two rewrites on the AB exam: polynomial long division, and completing the square to reach an arctangent.

Long division: when the top is "too big"

In $\frac{x^2 + 3x + 5}{x + 1}$ the top has degree 2 and the bottom degree 1. No basic rule fits, and substitution with $u = x + 1$ is clumsy. Divide, exactly as with numbers: $\frac{17}{5} = 3 + \frac{2}{5}$.

Worked example 1. Find $\int \frac{x^2 + 3x + 5}{x + 1}\,dx$.

Divide $x^2 + 3x + 5$ by $x + 1$. First term: $x$, since $x(x + 1) = x^2 + x$. Subtract: $2x + 5$ remains.

Next term: $2$, since $2(x + 1) = 2x + 2$. Subtract: $3$ remains, the remainder.

So $\frac{x^2 + 3x + 5}{x + 1} = x + 2 + \frac{3}{x + 1}$. (Check: $(x + 1)(x + 2) + 3 = x^2 + 3x + 5$.)

Integrate each piece: $\frac{x^2}{2} + 2x + 3\ln|x + 1| + C$.

The figure shows what the division means: far from $x = -1$, the curve is nearly the line $y = x + 2$, and the remainder $\frac{3}{x + 1}$ is the shrinking gap.

A quick version works when the bottom divides neatly into the top: $\frac{2x + 3}{x + 1} = \frac{2(x + 1) + 1}{x + 1} = 2 + \frac{1}{x + 1}$, and $\frac{x^2}{x^2 + 1} = \frac{(x^2 + 1) - 1}{x^2 + 1} = 1 - \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}$.

Completing the square: reaching arctan

The rule $\int \frac{1}{1 + u^2}\,du = \arctan u + C$ handles a sum of squares on the bottom. A quadratic with no real zeros can always be written as one. To complete the square in $x^2 + bx + c$, take half of $b$, square it, and balance:

$$x^2 - 2x + 10 = (x^2 - 2x + 1) + 9 = (x - 1)^2 + 9.$$

You also need the scaled version. With $u = x - h$ and $a > 0$:

$$\int \frac{1}{u^2 + a^2}\,du = \frac{1}{a}\arctan\frac{u}{a} + C.$$

(Check by differentiating: $\frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{1/a}{1 + u^2/a^2} = \frac{1}{a^2 + u^2}$.)

Worked example 2. Evaluate $\int_1^4 \frac{1}{x^2 - 2x + 10}\,dx$.

Complete the square: $(x - 1)^2 + 9$, so $u = x - 1$ and $a = 3$.

Antiderivative: $\frac{1}{3}\arctan\frac{x - 1}{3}$.

Evaluate: $\frac{1}{3}\left(\arctan 1 - \arctan 0\right) = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{12}$.

Two habits prevent most errors: never integrate the top and bottom of a fraction separately, and differentiate your answer to check it. The factor $\frac{1}{a}$ in the arctan form is the piece most often lost.

When the bottom factors into different linear pieces, such as $\frac{1}{(x - 1)(x + 2)}$, the method of partial fractions splits it apart. That method is part of the BC course only and does not appear on the AB exam.
