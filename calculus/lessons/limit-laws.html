Unit 1 · Topic 1.5 Beta

Limit Laws

The limit laws let you split a limit over sums, differences, constant multiples, products, quotients (with a nonzero denominator limit), powers, roots and compositions.

Practice 1: Implementing Mathematical Processes

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Graphs and tables estimate limits. Rules find them exactly. The limit laws say that limits respect arithmetic: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, and the same for differences, products, quotients, powers and roots. For polynomials and most everyday functions, they collapse into the easiest rule in calculus: just substitute.

How it works, step by step

  1. limx→af(x)=L\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L and limx→ag(x)=M\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M both exist→the limit of f±gf \pm g, fgfg and kfkf is L±ML \pm M, LMLM and kLkL
  2. The denominator's limit MM is not 0→the limit of fg\frac{f}{g} is LM\frac{L}{M}
  3. Polynomials and rational functions are built from x and constants with these operations→their limits come from substitution, as long as the denominator is not 0 at aa
  4. A denominator heads to 0→the quotient law is silent: a nonzero top means no finite limit; a top also heading to 0 needs the algebra of Topic 1.6

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Sum, difference, constant multiple, product: if both limits exist, combine them the same way. lim(f+g)\lim (f + g)=limf+limg= \lim f + \lim g.
  • Quotient: limfg\lim \frac{f}{g}=limflimg= \frac{\lim f}{\lim g} only when limg≠0\lim g \ne 0.
  • Powers and roots: lim[f(x)]n=Ln\lim [f(x)]^n = L^n, and limf(x)=L\lim \sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{L} when L>0L > 0.
  • Direct substitution: for a polynomial, or a rational function with a nonzero denominator at aa, the limit is the value at aa.
  • For a composite, find the inside limit first: if g(x)→bg(x) \to b and ff is continuous at bb, then f(g(x))→f(b)f(g(x)) \to f(b).
  • For a piecewise function, use each side's formula for its one-sided limit.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If neither limf\lim f nor limg\lim g exists, then lim(f+g)\lim (f + g) does not exist.

What actually happens: The laws need both limits to exist; when they do not, the laws are silent, not negative. Check the sum one side at a time: two jumps can cancel, as with ff and gg at x=1x = 1, where the sum's limit is 3.

1. What is limx→1[f(x)+g(x)]\lim_{x \to 1} \left[f(x) + g(x)\right]?

  1. 33
  2. It does not exist, because neither limx→1f(x)\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) nor limx→1g(x)\lim_{x \to 1} g(x) exists.
  3. 55
  4. 22
Show the answer

Work one side at a time. From the left, f(x)→2f(x) \to 2 and g(x)→1g(x) \to 1, so f(x)+g(x)→3f(x) + g(x) \to 3. From the right, f(x)→0f(x) \to 0 and g(x)→3g(x) \to 3, so the sum →3\to 3. Both sides give 3, so the limit is 3, even though ff and gg each jump.

  • Correct: 33: Left: 2+12 + 1. Right: 0+30 + 3. Both 3.
  • It does not exist, because neither limx→1f(x)\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) nor limx→1g(x)\lim_{x \to 1} g(x) exists.: The sum law needs both limits to exist, so it cannot be used here; but the sum can still have a limit. Check each side of the sum directly.
  • 55: 5 mixes sides: ff's left-hand limit 2 plus gg's right-hand limit 3. Each one-sided limit of the sum uses the same side for both functions.
  • 22: 2 is the left-hand limit of ff alone; the question asks about the sum f+gf + g.

  1. It does not exist.
  2. 22, from 2⋅12 \cdot 1
  3. 00
  4. 33
Show the answer

From the left, f(x)g(x)→2⋅1=2f(x)g(x) \to 2 \cdot 1 = 2. From the right, f(x)g(x)→0⋅3=0f(x)g(x) \to 0 \cdot 3 = 0. The one-sided limits differ, so the limit does not exist.

  • Correct: It does not exist.: Left 2, right 0.
  • 22, from 2⋅12 \cdot 1: 2 is only the left-hand limit of the product.
  • 00: 0 is only the right-hand limit (and f(1)g(1)f(1)g(1)).
  • 33: 3 is the limit of the sum, not the product.

6. Which limit can be found by direct substitution?

  1. limx→0x2+1x+4\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x + 4}
  2. limx→3x2−9x−3\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}
  3. limx→2x+1x−2\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x + 1}{x - 2}
  4. limx→1x−5\lim_{x \to 1} \sqrt{x - 5}
Show the answer

A rational function can be evaluated by substitution where its denominator is not 0. At x=0x = 0 the denominator is 4, so the limit is 14\frac{1}{4}.

  • Correct: limx→0x2+1x+4\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x + 4}: Denominator 4 at x=0x = 0: substitute.
  • limx→3x2−9x−3\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}: At x=3x = 3 the denominator is 0, so substitution gives no value; Topic 1.6 handles this.
  • limx→2x+1x−2\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x + 1}{x - 2}: At x=2x = 2 the denominator is 0.
  • limx→1x−5\lim_{x \to 1} \sqrt{x - 5}: x−5\sqrt{x - 5} is not defined for xx near 1.

7. What can you conclude about limx→2x+1x−2\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x + 1}{x - 2}?

  1. It is not a finite number: the top approaches 3 and the bottom approaches 0.
  2. It equals 0, because the bottom approaches 0 and the quotient shrinks with it.
  3. It equals 3, the limit of the top.
  4. It equals 30\frac{3}{0}.
Show the answer

The quotient law fails because the denominator's limit is 0. With the top near 3 and the bottom near 0, the quotient grows without bound in size, so it has no finite limit.

  • Correct: It is not a finite number: the top approaches 3 and the bottom approaches 0.: Nonzero over a number near 0: unbounded.
  • It equals 0, because the bottom approaches 0 and the quotient shrinks with it.: A small denominator makes a quotient large.
  • It equals 3, the limit of the top.: The denominator still matters.
  • It equals 30\frac{3}{0}.: 30\frac{3}{0} is not a number; that is not a limit value.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The limit laws let you split a limit over sums, differences, constant multiples, products, quotients (with a nonzero denominator limit), powers, roots and compositions. Together they justify direct substitution for polynomials and for rational functions where the denominator is not 0. When a denominator heads to 0, look closer: a nonzero numerator means no finite limit.
