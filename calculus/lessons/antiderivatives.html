Antiderivatives and Indefinite Integrals

Every derivative rule read backward gives an antidifferentiation rule.

Practice 1: Implementing Mathematical ProcessesPractice 4: Communication and Notation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Tell someone a car's speed at every moment and ask where it is. They cannot say, not without knowing where it started. Knowing a derivative pins a function down only up to a starting value, and that missing piece is the +C+ C that students lose points for forgetting.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A constant has derivative 0→if F′=fF' = f, then (F+C)′=f(F + C)' = f for every constant CC
  2. Two antiderivatives of f on an interval→differ by a constant, so the whole family is F(x) + C
  3. Write the family as ∫f(x)dx\int f(x)\,dx→∫f(x)dx=F(x)+C\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C: an indefinite integral always ends in +C+ C
  4. You also know one point on the graph→substitute it to find CC and pick out the one function

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Indefinite integral: ∫f(x)dx=F(x)+C\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C, the family of all antiderivatives. No limits, a family, never a number.
  • Power rule backward: ∫xndx\int x^n\,dx=xn+1n+1+C= \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C for n≠−1n \ne -1; and ∫1xdx=ln⁡|x|+C\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| + C.
  • Rewrite before integrating: roots as powers, products expanded, a fraction over a single term split up.
  • Check every answer by differentiating it. Find CC from a known point by substituting it.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: ∫sin⁡xdx=cos⁡x+C\int \sin x\,dx = \cos x + C, because sine and cosine go together.

What actually happens: ddxcos⁡x=−sin⁡x\frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x, so ∫sin⁡xdx=−cos⁡x+C\int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x + C. Differentiate your answer to check the sign.

6. Which is ∫1xdx\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx?

  1. ln⁡|x|+C\ln|x| + C
  2. x00+C\frac{x^0}{0} + C
  3. −1x2+C-\frac{1}{x^2} + C
  4. 1ln⁡x+C\frac{1}{\ln x} + C
Show the answer

For x>0x > 0, (ln⁡x)′=1x(\ln x)' = \frac{1}{x}; for x<0x < 0, (ln⁡(−x))′=1x(\ln(-x))' = \frac{1}{x} too. So ∫1xdx=ln⁡|x|+C\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| + C.

  • Correct: ln⁡|x|+C\ln|x| + C: Correct: ddxln⁡|x|=1x\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x} for x≠0x \ne 0.
  • x00+C\frac{x^0}{0} + C: The power rule needs n≠−1n \ne -1; for x−1x^{-1} it would divide by 0. The antiderivative is ln⁡|x|\ln|x|.
  • −1x2+C-\frac{1}{x^2} + C: That is the derivative of 1x\frac{1}{x}, the wrong direction.
  • 1ln⁡x+C\frac{1}{\ln x} + C: The derivative of 1ln⁡x\frac{1}{\ln x} is −1x(ln⁡x)2-\frac{1}{x(\ln x)^2}, not 1x\frac{1}{x}.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Every derivative rule read backward gives an antidifferentiation rule. The indefinite integral ∫f(x)dx=F(x)+C\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C is the whole family of antiderivatives, so it always carries +C+ C. Rewrite the integrand into basic forms first, check by differentiating, and use a known point to find CC.
