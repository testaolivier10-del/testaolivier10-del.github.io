The Fundamental Theorem and Accumulation Functions

An accumulation function g(x) = ∫ from a to x of f(t) dt starts at 0 and grows at the rate f(x) : for continuous f , g′(x) = f(x) , so g is an antiderivative of f .

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Picture a paint roller moving along a wall, painting everything below a curve. Stop it at any position xx and you can measure the painted area so far. Now ask how fast that area grows as the roller moves. The answer is startlingly simple, and it ties the two halves of calculus together: the area grows at a rate equal to the height of the curve.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Define g(x)=∫axf(t)dtg(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt→g(x)g(x) is the area accumulated under ff from aa up to xx, and g(a)=0g(a) = 0
  2. Move xx a tiny step hh to the right→the area grows by a thin strip about f(x)f(x) tall and hh wide, so g(x+h)−g(x)h≈f(x)\frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} \approx f(x)
  3. Let h→0h \to 0→g′(x)=f(x)g'(x) = f(x): the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, so gg is an antiderivative of ff
  4. The upper limit is a function u(x)u(x)→the chain rule adds a factor: ddx∫au(x)f(t)dt=f(u(x))u′(x)\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt = f(u(x))\,u'(x)

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • An accumulation function g(x)=∫axf(t)dtg(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt is the area so far; g(a)=0g(a) = 0.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: if ff is continuous, g′(x)=f(x)g'(x) = f(x). The constant lower limit drops out.
  • An antiderivative of ff is any FF with F′=fF' = f. Every accumulation function of ff is one.
  • Upper limit u(x)u(x): ddx∫au(x)f(t)dt=f(u(x))⋅u′(x)\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt = f(u(x)) \cdot u'(x). Forgetting u′(x)u'(x) is the classic slip.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: ddx∫2xf(t)dt=f(x)−f(2)\frac{d}{dx}\int_2^x f(t)\,dt = f(x) - f(2), because the lower limit must be subtracted.

What actually happens: The lower limit is a constant: it decides where the accumulation starts, not how fast it grows. The derivative is just f(x)f(x).

6. ff is continuous and g(x)=∫2xf(t)dtg(x) = \int_2^x f(t)\,dt. Which is g′(x)g'(x)?

  1. f(x)−2f(x) - 2
  2. f(x)f(x)
  3. f(x)−f(2)f(x) - f(2)
  4. f′(x)f'(x)
Show the answer

For continuous ff and constant aa, ddx∫axf(t)dt=f(x)\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x). The lower limit only fixes where g=0g = 0.

  • f(x)−2f(x) - 2: The 2 is a limit of integration, not something subtracted from ff.
  • Correct: f(x)f(x): Correct: the Fundamental Theorem gives the integrand at the upper limit; the constant lower limit drops out.
  • f(x)−f(2)f(x) - f(2): The lower limit is a constant. It shifts the value of gg but not its derivative: no f(2)f(2) term.
  • f′(x)f'(x): That is g″(x)g''(x). One derivative of gg gives ff itself.
