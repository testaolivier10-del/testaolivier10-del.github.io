Limits at Infinity and Horizontal Asymptotes

A limit at infinity describes where f(x) heads as x → ∞ or x → −∞ ; a finite one gives a horizontal asymptote.

Question set for this topic

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A cup of hot coffee cools quickly at first, then more and more slowly, settling toward room temperature but never quite reaching it. Questions about the long run, where a quantity heads as time goes on and on, are limits at infinity. They describe where a graph levels off, and which of two growing quantities wins.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. xx grows without bound→terms like 1x\frac{1}{x}, 5x2\frac{5}{x^2} and e−xe^{-x} shrink to 0
  2. You divide the top and bottom of a quotient by the dominant term→every other term becomes one of those shrinking terms, and the limit can be read off
  3. limx→∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L or limx→−∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L is finite→y=Ly = L is a horizontal asymptote; the graph levels off there (and may cross it in the middle)
  4. Two functions both grow without bound→the faster one dominates: as x→∞x \to \infty, ln⁡x\ln x loses to any power xpx^p (p>0p > 0), and xpx^p loses to exe^x

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • limx→∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L means f(x)f(x) approaches LL as xx grows without bound. A finite limit at ∞\infty or −∞-\infty gives a horizontal asymptote y=Ly = L.
  • For a rational function, divide by the highest power of xx in the denominator. Equal degrees: ratio of leading coefficients. Bottom higher: 0. Top higher: no horizontal asymptote.
  • x2=|x|\sqrt{x^2} = |x|. As x→−∞x \to -\infty, x2=−x\sqrt{x^2} = -x, which can flip the sign of the answer.
  • Dominant terms: in the long run ln⁡x\ln x loses to every positive power, and exe^x beats every power.
  • Vertical asymptote: limx→cf(x)=±∞\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = \pm\infty. Horizontal asymptote: limx→±∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = L. Do not mix them up.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A graph never crosses its horizontal asymptote, and the limit at −∞-\infty is the same as at ∞\infty.

What actually happens: A horizontal asymptote only describes the far ends: 2x2+xx2+1\frac{2x^2 + x}{x^2 + 1} crosses y=2y = 2 at x=2x = 2. And the two ends can differ: 4xx2+1\frac{4x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}} approaches 4 on the right and −4-4 on the left.

1. Divide by the highest power of xx to find limx→∞f1(x)\lim_{x \to \infty} f_1(x).

No calculator. Type the exact value (a fraction, π, e or a root is fine).

Show the answer

2. Which function has the horizontal asymptote y=0y = 0?

  1. f2f_2
  2. f1f_1
  3. f3f_3
  4. f4f_4
Show the answer

In f2f_2 the denominator has the higher degree. Dividing by x2x^2: 4x+1x21−3x2→01\frac{\frac{4}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}}{1 - \frac{3}{x^2}} \to \frac{0}{1}=0= 0.

  • Correct: f2f_2: Degree 1 over degree 2: the denominator dominates, so the quotient approaches 0.
  • f1f_1: Equal degrees give y=32y = \frac{3}{2}.
  • f3f_3: The numerator has the higher degree, so f3f_3 is unbounded.
  • f4f_4: Equal degrees give y=6−3=−2y = \frac{6}{-3} = -2.

3. Why is limx→−∞h(x)=−4\lim_{x \to -\infty} h(x) = -4 rather than 44?

  1. For x<0x < 0, x2+1\sqrt{x^2 + 1} is negative, so the quotient changes sign
  2. The constant 1 under the root becomes −1-1 when xx is negative
  3. Limits at −∞-\infty are the negatives of limits at ∞\infty
  4. For x<0x < 0, x2=−x\sqrt{x^2} = -x, so h(x)≈4x−x=−4h(x) \approx \frac{4x}{-x} = -4
Show the answer

x2=|x|\sqrt{x^2} = |x|. When xx is negative, |x|=−x|x| = -x, so for large negative xx, 4xx2+1\frac{4x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}≈4x−x\approx \frac{4x}{-x}=−4= -4.

  • For x<0x < 0, x2+1\sqrt{x^2 + 1} is negative, so the quotient changes sign: A square root is never negative; it is the numerator 4x4x that is negative.
  • The constant 1 under the root becomes −1-1 when xx is negative: The 1 stays 1; it just becomes small next to x2x^2.
  • Limits at −∞-\infty are the negatives of limits at ∞\infty: Not in general: f1f_1 above has the same limit 32\frac{3}{2} in both directions.
  • Correct: For x<0x < 0, x2=−x\sqrt{x^2} = -x, so h(x)≈4x−x=−4h(x) \approx \frac{4x}{-x} = -4: The root is |x||x|, which equals −x-x for negative xx, while the numerator 4x4x is negative.

4. How many horizontal asymptotes does the graph of hh have?

  1. Two: y=4y = 4 and y=−4y = -4
  2. One: y=4y = 4
  3. One: y=0y = 0
  4. Zero, since hh is defined everywhere
Show the answer

A horizontal asymptote comes from a finite limit at ∞\infty or at −∞-\infty. Here they differ, 4 and −4-4, so there are two.

  • Correct: Two: y=4y = 4 and y=−4y = -4: Each direction gives its own finite limit.
  • One: y=4y = 4: The left side levels off at −4-4, a second asymptote.
  • One: y=0y = 0: h(0)=0h(0) = 0, but asymptotes describe behavior far out, not at the origin.
  • Zero, since hh is defined everywhere: Being defined everywhere does not stop a graph from leveling off.

5. Find limx→∞x3ex\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^3}{e^x}.

  1. ∞\infty
  2. 11
  3. 00
  4. 33
Show the answer

An exponential with base greater than 1 eventually grows faster than any power of xx. So exe^x dominates x3x^3 and the quotient approaches 0.

  • ∞\infty: x3x^3 is larger only for moderate xx; in the long run exe^x wins.
  • 11: The two do not grow at the same rate.
  • Correct: 00: exe^x passes x3x^3 (near x≈4.5x \approx 4.5) and then leaves it far behind.
  • 33: The exponent 3 does not appear in the limit.

6. Can a graph cross its own horizontal asymptote? Consider f(x)f(x)=2x2+xx2+1= \frac{2x^2 + x}{x^2 + 1}, whose horizontal asymptote is y=2y = 2.

  1. No: a graph stays off its asymptote
  2. Yes: f(2)=2f(2) = 2, yet f(x)→2f(x) \to 2 as x→∞x \to \infty
  3. No: f(x)>2f(x) > 2 for each xx, so it stays above
  4. Yes, but then y=2y = 2 is no longer an asymptote
Show the answer

f(x)=2f(x) = 2 when 2x2+x=2x2+22x^2 + x = 2x^2 + 2, so at x=2x = 2. A horizontal asymptote describes behavior far out, as x→±∞x \to \pm\infty, and says nothing about the middle of the graph.

  • No: a graph stays off its asymptote: Vertical asymptotes cannot be crossed (the function is unbounded there), but horizontal ones can.
  • Correct: Yes: f(2)=2f(2) = 2, yet f(x)→2f(x) \to 2 as x→∞x \to \infty: f(2)=8+25=2f(2) = \frac{8 + 2}{5} = 2: the graph crosses y=2y = 2, and still approaches it far out.
  • No: f(x)>2f(x) > 2 for each xx, so it stays above: f(0)=0<2f(0) = 0 < 2, so the graph is sometimes below.
  • Yes, but then y=2y = 2 is no longer an asymptote: The asymptote is defined by the limit at infinity, which is still 2.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

A limit at infinity describes where f(x)f(x) heads as x→∞x \to \infty or x→−∞x \to -\infty; a finite one gives a horizontal asymptote. Divide by the dominant term: for rational functions compare degrees. Watch x2=|x|\sqrt{x^2} = |x| on the left. In the long run ln⁡x\ln x grows slower than any power and exe^x faster than any power.
