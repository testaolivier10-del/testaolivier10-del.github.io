The Product Rule

When two factors both change, the product changes by each factor's change times the other factor: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′ .

Question set for this topic

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A store's revenue is price times the number of items sold. If the price rises while sales fall, how fast is revenue changing? Both factors are moving at once, and the answer is not the product of their rates. The product rule tells you exactly how two changing factors combine.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Both factors of f(x)g(x)f(x)g(x) change as xx changes→the change in the product has two main parts: gg times the change in ff, and ff times the change in gg
  2. The extra piece ΔfΔg\Delta f \, \Delta g is a product of two small changes→after dividing by Δx\Delta x it still goes to 0
  3. Divide by Δx\Delta x and take the limit→(fg)′=f′g+fg′(fg)' = f'g + fg'
  4. Three factors→differentiate one factor at a time and add: (fgk)′=f′gk+fg′k+fgk′(fgk)' = f'gk + fg'k + fgk'

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The product rule: ddx(f(x)g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left( f(x)g(x) \right)=f′(x)g(x)+f(x)g′(x)= f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).
  • In words: derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second.
  • The derivative of a product is NOT the product of the derivatives: (x⋅x)′=2x(x \cdot x)' = 2x, but 1⋅1=11 \cdot 1 = 1.
  • From a table: (fg)′(a)(fg)'(a)=f′(a)g(a)+f(a)g′(a)= f'(a)g(a) + f(a)g'(a) uses both the value columns and the derivative columns.
  • If one factor is a constant, the product rule reduces to the constant multiple rule.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: ddx(f(x)g(x))=f′(x)g′(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left( f(x)g(x) \right) = f'(x)g'(x).

What actually happens: Try f(x)=g(x)=xf(x) = g(x) = x: the product is x2x^2, whose derivative is 2x2x, but f′g′=1f'g' = 1. Each factor's change is multiplied by the other factor's value, and the two contributions add: (fg)′=f′g+fg′(fg)' = f'g + fg'.

6. Let f(x)=xf(x) = x and g(x)=xg(x) = x. Which comparison of (f(x)g(x))′\left( f(x)g(x) \right)' and f′(x)g′(x)f'(x)g'(x) is correct?

  1. (fg)′=2x(fg)' = 2x and f′g′=1f'g' = 1; they differ, so (fg)′≠f′g′(fg)' \ne f'g'
  2. Both equal 2x2x, so (fg)′=f′g′(fg)' = f'g' for these two functions
  3. Both equal 11, because f′=1f' = 1 and g′=1g' = 1 for these functions
  4. (fg)′=1(fg)' = 1 and f′g′=2xf'g' = 2x; they differ, so (fg)′≠f′g′(fg)' \ne f'g'
Show the answer

fg=x2fg = x^2, whose derivative is 2x2x; f′g′=1⋅1=1f'g' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1. By the product rule, (fg)′=1⋅x+x⋅1=2x(fg)' = 1 \cdot x + x \cdot 1 = 2x.

  • Correct: (fg)′=2x(fg)' = 2x and f′g′=1f'g' = 1; they differ, so (fg)′≠f′g′(fg)' \ne f'g': One counterexample is enough.
  • Both equal 2x2x, so (fg)′=f′g′(fg)' = f'g' for these two functions: f′g′=1⋅1=1f'g' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1, not 2x2x.
  • Both equal 11, because f′=1f' = 1 and g′=1g' = 1 for these functions: (fg)′=(x2)′=2x(fg)' = (x^2)' = 2x, not 1.
  • (fg)′=1(fg)' = 1 and f′g′=2xf'g' = 2x; they differ, so (fg)′≠f′g′(fg)' \ne f'g': That swaps the two results.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

When two factors both change, the product changes by each factor's change times the other factor: (fg)′=f′g+fg′(fg)' = f'g + fg'. It is never just f′g′f'g'. For three factors, differentiate one at a time and add the three terms. From a table, the rule needs the values as well as the derivatives.
