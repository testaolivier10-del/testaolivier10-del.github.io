Particular Solutions from Initial Conditions

A particular solution satisfies the differential equation and an initial condition.

Practice 1: Implementing Mathematical ProcessesPractice 3: Justification

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Separation of variables gives a family of curves, one for each constant. A real problem wants one curve: the temperature of this coffee, which was 90 °C at noon. That one fact, an initial condition, picks out a single member of the family. Use it the moment the constant appears, and watch the domain: some solutions shoot off to infinity and simply stop.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Separate and integrate→an equation with one constant CC
  2. Substitute the initial condition right away→a number for CC, before any rearranging
  3. Solve for yy, choosing the sign or branch that passes through the given point→one function, the particular solution
  4. The formula breaks somewhere (division by 0, a negative under a root, a log of 0)→the domain is the largest open interval around the initial x where the solution is defined and differentiable

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • An initial condition y(x0)=y0y(x_0) = y_0 says the solution passes through (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0). Put x0x_0 in for xx and y0y_0 in for yy.
  • Use it as soon as CC appears. Solving for yy first invites algebra slips and wrong constants.
  • After a square root, keep the sign that matches y0y_0: y(1)=−3y(1) = -3 needs the negative root.
  • State the domain: an open interval containing x0x_0 on which the formula is defined and its derivative exists.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: From ln⁡|y|=sin⁡x+C\ln|y| = \sin x + C: y=esin⁡x+Cy = e^{\sin x} + C, and y(0)=2y(0) = 2 gives C=1C = 1, so y=esin⁡x+1y = e^{\sin x} + 1.

What actually happens: Exponentiating gives y=Aesin⁡xy = Ae^{\sin x}, not esin⁡x+Ce^{\sin x} + C. Better, use the condition first: ln⁡2=sin⁡0+C\ln 2 = \sin 0 + C, so C=ln⁡2C = \ln 2 and yy=esin⁡x+ln⁡2= e^{\sin x + \ln 2}=2esin⁡x= 2e^{\sin x}.

3. What is the domain of the particular solution y=−2x2+7y = -\sqrt{2x^2 + 7}?

  1. x≥1x \ge 1
  2. All real numbers
  3. x>−72x > -\frac{7}{2}
  4. |x|≤72|x| \le \sqrt{\frac{7}{2}}
Show the answer

2x2+72x^2 + 7 is never below 7, so yy is defined and never 0, and dydx=2xy\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{y} exists for every real xx.

  • x≥1x \ge 1: The initial point is inside the domain; the domain is not cut off there.
  • Correct: All real numbers: 2x2+7≥7>02x^2 + 7 \ge 7 > 0, so the root is defined and y≠0y \ne 0 for every xx.
  • x>−72x > -\frac{7}{2}: 2x2+72x^2 + 7 is positive for every xx, so nothing is cut off.
  • |x|≤72|x| \le \sqrt{\frac{7}{2}}: That would need 7−2x2≥07 - 2x^2 \ge 0; here the sign is ++.

4. When is the best moment to use the initial condition in separation of variables?

  1. Before integrating, to choose which antiderivative to use.
  2. At the end, after solving for yy and then adding CC.
  3. It is not needed for a separable equation.
  4. As soon as CC appears: substitute the point, find CC, then solve for yy.
Show the answer

Substituting right after integrating finds CC in the simplest form and avoids sign and algebra slips.

  • Before integrating, to choose which antiderivative to use.: You cannot find C before it exists.
  • At the end, after solving for yy and then adding CC.: A CC added after solving gives the wrong family.
  • It is not needed for a separable equation.: Without it you have a family, not one solution.
  • Correct: As soon as CC appears: substitute the point, find CC, then solve for yy.: CC is found in the simplest equation, before rearranging.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

A particular solution satisfies the differential equation and an initial condition. Separate, integrate, add CC, substitute the initial point immediately, then solve for yy with the sign that fits. Give its domain: the open interval around the initial xx where it is defined and differentiable.
