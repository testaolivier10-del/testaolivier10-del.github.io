The Fundamental Theorem and Definite Integrals

4 min read · freeNot practiced

Share to Google Classroom (opens in a new tab)

Topic 6.4 showed that an accumulation function ∫axf(t)dt\int_a^x f(t)\,dt is an antiderivative of ff. This page turns that around into a way to compute definite integrals exactly, and into the idea the exam uses most: the integral of a rate is the net change.

From one antiderivative to the integral

Let FF be any antiderivative of a continuous ff, so F′=fF' = f. The accumulation function g(x)g(x)=∫axf(t)dt= \int_a^x f(t)\,dt is another one. Two functions with the same derivative on an interval differ by a constant (a consequence of the Mean Value Theorem), so

g(x)=F(x)+k.g(x) = F(x) + k.

At x=ax = a: g(a)=0g(a) = 0, so k=−F(a)k = -F(a). Now put x=bx = b:

∫abf(x)dx\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)−F(a)= F(b) - F(a) (the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, part 2).

The shorthand F(x)|abF(x)\Big|_a^b means F(b)−F(a)F(b) - F(a). Any antiderivative works; a constant added to FF cancels in the subtraction.

Worked example 1. Find ∫13(3x2−2)dx\int_1^3 (3x^2 - 2)\,dx.

Find a function whose derivative is 3x2−23x^2 - 2: F(x)=x3−2xF(x) = x^3 - 2x (check: F′(x)=3x2−2F'(x) = 3x^2 - 2).

F(3)=27−6=21F(3) = 27 - 6 = 21 and F(1)=1−2=−1F(1) = 1 - 2 = -1.

∫13(3x2−2)dx\int_1^3 (3x^2 - 2)\,dx=21−(−1)= 21 - (-1)=22= 22. Keep the parentheses: subtracting a negative is where most slips happen.

Antiderivatives come from reading derivative rules backward: sin⁡x\sin x for cos⁡x\cos x, ln⁡x\ln x for 1x\frac{1}{x} (with x>0x > 0), exe^x for exe^x, arctan⁡x\arctan x for 11+x2\frac{1}{1 + x^2}. The next topic organizes these rules.

The integral of a rate is the net change

Apply part 2 to f′f', whose antiderivative is ff itself:

∫abf′(x)dx=f(b)−f(a).\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a).

This is the net change theorem: the integral of a rate of change is the total change. If P(t)P(t) is a population and ∫030P′(t)dt\int_0^{30} P'(t)\,dt=12= 12 thousand, the population grew by 12 thousand over those 30 years, whatever it did in between. Rearranged:

f(b)f(b)=f(a)= f(a)+∫abf′(x)dx+ \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx: value at the end = value at the start + accumulated change.

Worked example 2. The graph of f′f' is constant 2 on [0,3][0, 3], falls in a straight line from (3,2)(3, 2) to (5,−2)(5, -2), and is constant −2-2 on [5,8][5, 8]. f(2)=5f(2) = 5. Find f(5)f(5), f(0)f(0) and the absolute maximum of ff.

f(5)f(5)=f(2)= f(2)+∫25f′(x)dx+ \int_2^5 f'(x)\,dx=5= 5+(2+1−1)+ (2 + 1 - 1)=7= 7.

f(0)f(0): going backward, f(0)f(0)=f(2)−∫02f′(x)dx= f(2) - \int_0^2 f'(x)\,dx=5−4= 5 - 4=1= 1.

Maximum: f′f' changes from positive to negative at x=4x = 4. Candidates: f(0)=1f(0) = 1, f(4)=5+3=8f(4) = 5 + 3 = 8, f(8)f(8)=5+(2+1−1−6)= 5 + (2 + 1 - 1 - 6)=1= 1. The absolute maximum is 8, at x=4x = 4.

When there is no formula: the calculator

Some rates, such as 2.5sin⁡(t210)−0.52.5\sin\left(\frac{t^2}{10}\right) - 0.5, have no antiderivative made of familiar functions. The net change idea still works; the calculator evaluates the integral. Write the full setup:

D(8)=D(0)+∫08D′(t)dt=10+∫08(2.5sin⁡(t210)−0.5)dt≈9.413 inches.D(8) = D(0) + \int_0^8 D'(t)\,dt = 10 + \int_0^8 \left(2.5\sin\left(\frac{t^2}{10}\right) - 0.5\right)dt \approx 9.413 \text{ inches}.

Use radian mode, keep full precision until the end, and give three decimal places.

Interpreting in context

A complete interpretation of ∫abr(t)dt\int_a^b r(t)\,dt names the quantity, its units and the interval: "∫26D′(t)dt\int_2^6 D'(t)\,dt is the change in the depth of the snow, in inches, from t=2t = 2 to t=6t = 6 hours." Three things to avoid: calling it a rate (inches per hour), calling it the amount at the end (that also needs the starting value), and leaving out the time interval.
