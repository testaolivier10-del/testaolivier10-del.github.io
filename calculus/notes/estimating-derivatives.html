3Differentiation: Composite, Implicit, and Inverse Functions

The definition of the derivative needs a formula: you substitute a+ha + h, simplify and take a limit. Real data rarely comes with a formula. A thermometer gives readings every few minutes; a graph gives a picture. This page shows how to estimate f′(a)f'(a) from a table, from a graph and with a calculator, and how to say what the estimate means.

From a table: a difference quotient you can compute

The derivative is the limit of difference quotients as the interval shrinks. With a table, you cannot shrink the interval past the data, so you stop at the best interval the table offers and use that quotient as the estimate.

To estimate f′(a)f'(a) from a table:

  1. Pick two inputs on either side of aa, as close to aa as the table allows: c<a<bc < a < b. If aa itself is in the table, the closest inputs just before and just after it are a good choice.
  2. Compute f(b)−f(c)b−c\frac{f(b) - f(c)}{b - c}, subtracting in the same order on top and bottom.
  3. Attach units: output units per input unit.

The interval must contain aa. A quotient from data far away describes a different stretch of the function. At the first or last entry of a table there is only one side, so use the closest one-sided quotient.

Worked example 1. A loaf of bread cools on a rack. Its temperature B(t)B(t), in degrees Fahrenheit, is recorded at tt minutes.

tt (minutes)04101420
B(t)B(t) (degrees Fahrenheit)18015212110588

Estimate B′(12)B'(12) and interpret it.

Inputs on either side of 12: t=10t = 10 and t=14t = 14.

Quotient: B′(12)B'(12)≈B(14)−B(10)14−10\approx \frac{B(14) - B(10)}{14 - 10}=105−1214= \frac{105 - 121}{4}=−4= -4 degrees Fahrenheit per minute.

Meaning: at t=12t = 12 minutes, the bread's temperature is decreasing at about 4 degrees Fahrenheit per minute.

Notice what the answer is: a rate, with "per minute" in its units. A frequent slip is to give a temperature instead, such as averaging B(10)B(10) and B(14)B(14) to get 113. That estimates B(12)B(12), the value, not B′(12)B'(12), the rate.

Notice, too, how the sentence is built. It names the time (t=12t = 12), the quantity (the bread's temperature), the direction (decreasing, from the negative sign) and the units. A sentence that says only "the slope is −4-4" leaves out everything a reader needs.

Uneven spacing is fine

Tables on the exam often have unequal gaps. Use the actual widths. With data at t=9t = 9 and t=12t = 12, the width is 3, whatever the other gaps are. In Worked example 1, the intervals are 4, 6, 4 and 6 minutes wide; each quotient divides by its own width.

The symmetric difference quotient

If you know ff at a−ha - h and a+ha + h, the quotient across the point,

f(a+h)−f(a−h)2h,\frac{f(a + h) - f(a - h)}{2h},

is the symmetric difference quotient. Its denominator is the full width 2h2h. It equals the average of the left quotient f(a)−f(a−h)h\frac{f(a) - f(a - h)}{h} and the right quotient f(a+h)−f(a)h\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}, and their errors partly cancel, so it is usually much closer to f′(a)f'(a) than either one.

Worked example 2. A function has f(2.9)=24.389f(2.9) = 24.389, f(3)=27f(3) = 27 and f(3.1)=29.791f(3.1) = 29.791. Estimate f′(3)f'(3).

Left: 27−24.3890.1\frac{27 - 24.389}{0.1}=26.11= 26.11. Right: 29.791−270.1\frac{29.791 - 27}{0.1}=27.91= 27.91.

Symmetric: 29.791−24.3890.2\frac{29.791 - 24.389}{0.2}=5.4020.2= \frac{5.402}{0.2}=27.01= 27.01, the average of the two. (These are values of x3x^3, whose derivative at 3 turns out to be exactly 27.)

From a graph

When you have a graph, the derivative at aa is the slope of the tangent line at (a,f(a))(a, f(a)). If the tangent line is drawn, read two points on it where it crosses grid lines and compute rise over run. If it is not drawn, lay a ruler along the curve so that it just touches at the point, then read two points on the ruler. Choose points far apart on the line: small reading errors matter less over a long run.

A graph also tells you the sign of f′(a)f'(a) at a glance: positive where the graph rises, negative where it falls, zero where its tangent line is level.

With a calculator

A graphing calculator has a numerical derivative command. Give it the function and the input, and it returns a decimal estimate of f′(a)f'(a), computed from a symmetric difference quotient with a very small hh. On calculator questions:

  • Write the setup, such as ddx(ex1+x2)|x=2\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left( \frac{e^x}{1 + x^2} \right)\right|_{x=2}, then the value. A bare number earns little.
  • Give three decimal places: 0.2960.296. Store intermediate values instead of rounding them.
  • Use radian mode whenever a trigonometric function appears.

Every one of these is an approximation of a limit. The exam asks for the estimate, its setup and its units; it does not expect the exact value. The next topic asks when a derivative exists at all.
