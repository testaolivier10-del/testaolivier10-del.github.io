One differential equation appears in more places than any other: dydt=ky\frac{dy}{dt} = ky. It says the rate of change is proportional to the amount present. Bacteria with room to grow, an account earning continuous interest, a medicine cleared by the kidneys, a radioactive sample: each changes faster when there is more of it. This page solves the equation once, so you can quote the result, and shows how to read and fit the model.

The solution, once and for all

Separate: dyy=kdt\frac{dy}{y} = k\,dt. Integrate: ln⁡|y|=kt+C\ln|y| = kt + C. Exponentiate: y=Aekty = Ae^{kt}. At t=0t = 0, y=Ae0=Ay = Ae^0 = A, so the constant AA is the starting amount y0=y(0)y_0 = y(0):

dydt=ky,y(0)=y0givesy=y0ekt.\frac{dy}{dt} = ky, \quad y(0) = y_0 \qquad \text{gives} \qquad y = y_0e^{kt}.

This is the exponential model. You may quote it on the exam when a problem says the rate of change is proportional to the amount.

What kk means

Rearrange the equation: k=dy/dtyk = \frac{dy/dt}{y}. So kk is the rate of change as a fraction of the current amount. If k=0.05k = 0.05 per year, the quantity grows at 5% of its current size per year, at every moment. If k=−0.12k = -0.12 per hour, it shrinks at 12% of its current size per hour. That is why kk is called the growth constant (k>0k > 0) or the decay constant (k<0k < 0).

Units follow from the fraction: dydt\frac{dy}{dt} is in "amount per hour" and yy in "amount", so kk is in "per hour". The product ktkt has no units, as an exponent must.

Finding kk from data

Worked example 1. A culture grows at a rate proportional to its size. There are 400 bacteria at t=0t = 0 and 1200 at t=3t = 3 hours. Find kk, the model, and the population at t=6t = 6.

The model is P=400ektP = 400e^{kt}. Use the second count: 1200=400e3k1200 = 400e^{3k}.

Divide first: e3k=3e^{3k} = 3. Take natural logs: 3k=ln⁡33k = \ln 3, so k=ln⁡33≈0.366k = \frac{\ln 3}{3} \approx 0.366 per hour.

Model: P(t)=400eln⁡33tP(t) = 400e^{\frac{\ln 3}{3}t}. Then P(6)P(6)=400e2ln⁡3= 400e^{2\ln 3}=400⋅9= 400 \cdot 9=3600= 3600 bacteria.

Notice the pattern: 3 hours multiplies the population by 3, so 6 hours multiplies it by 9. A linear guess (add 800 every 3 hours, giving 2000) is the trap.

Worked example 2 (calculator). A medicine leaves the blood by dAdt=−0.12A\frac{dA}{dt} = -0.12A, with A(0)=250A(0) = 250 mg and tt in hours. Find the half-life and the time when 50 mg remain.

The model is A=250e−0.12tA = 250e^{-0.12t}. Half-life: 125=250e−0.12t125 = 250e^{-0.12t}, so e−0.12t=12e^{-0.12t} = \frac{1}{2} and t=ln⁡20.12≈5.776t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.12} \approx 5.776 hours.

50 mg: 50=250e−0.12t50 = 250e^{-0.12t}, so e−0.12t=0.2e^{-0.12t} = 0.2 and t=ln⁡50.12≈13.412t = \frac{\ln 5}{0.12} \approx 13.412 hours.

Keep full precision in the calculator and round to three decimal places only at the end.

Cooling and warming

Newton's law of cooling, dTdt=k(T−Tr)\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_r) with room temperature TrT_r, is an exponential model for the difference T−TrT - T_r, not for TT. So T−TrT - T_r=(T0−Tr)ekt= (T_0 - T_r)e^{kt}, or TT=Tr+(T0−Tr)ekt= T_r + (T_0 - T_r)e^{kt}. A pie at 90 °F in a 20 °F freezer has T−20=70ektT - 20 = 70e^{kt}; if it is at 55 °F after 10 minutes, then 35=70e10k35 = 70e^{10k} and k=−ln⁡210k = -\frac{\ln 2}{10} per minute.

Common slips

  • Thinking linearly: an exponential multiplies by the same factor in equal times; it does not add the same amount.
  • Taking the log before dividing: from 1200=400e3k1200 = 400e^{3k}, divide by 400 first.
  • Losing the sign of kk for decay, or using the rate at t=0t = 0 when a later rate is asked: dAdt=kA(t)\frac{dA}{dt} = kA(t) changes with time.
  • Applying the model to TT instead of T−TrT - T_r in a cooling problem.
