Every limit so far asked what happens as xx approaches a number. Now let xx run off forever, to the right or to the left. Where does f(x)f(x) head? The answer describes the far ends of a graph, and it settles which of two growing quantities wins in the long run.

Limits at infinity and horizontal asymptotes

limx→∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L means that as xx grows without bound, f(x)f(x) gets as close to LL as you like. limx→−∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L means the same as xx decreases without bound. When either limit is a finite number LL, the line y=Ly = L is a horizontal asymptote: the graph levels off toward it.

The basic fact behind everything here: as x→∞x \to \infty, 1x→0\frac{1}{x} \to 0, and so do 5x2\frac{5}{x^2}, 1x\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} and e−x=1exe^{-x} = \frac{1}{e^x}. A fixed number divided by something growing without bound shrinks to 0.

A horizontal asymptote is about the far ends only. In the middle the graph may cross it: 2x2+xx2+1\frac{2x^2 + x}{x^2 + 1} equals 2 at x=2x = 2 and still approaches 2 as x→∞x \to \infty. Vertical asymptotes are different: next to one the function is unbounded, and for a rational function the line itself is outside the domain, so the graph runs along it instead of crossing it.

Rational functions: divide by the dominant power

For large xx, only the highest-power terms matter. To make that precise, divide the top and the bottom by the highest power of xx in the denominator.

Worked example 1. Find limx→∞3x2−x2x2+5\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - x}{2x^2 + 5}.

Divide every term by x2x^2: 3−1x2+5x2\frac{3 - \frac{1}{x}}{2 + \frac{5}{x^2}}.

As x→∞x \to \infty, 1x→0\frac{1}{x} \to 0 and 5x2→0\frac{5}{x^2} \to 0, so the limit is 32\frac{3}{2}. The graph has the horizontal asymptote y=32y = \frac{3}{2}.

Square roots: watch the sign

x2\sqrt{x^2} is |x||x|, not xx. For positive xx that makes no difference. For negative xx it does: x2=−x\sqrt{x^2} = -x.

Worked example 2. Find both limits at infinity of h(x)=4xx2+1h(x) = \frac{4x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}.

For large |x||x|, x2+1≈x2=|x|\sqrt{x^2 + 1} \approx \sqrt{x^2} = |x|.

As x→∞x \to \infty: |x|=x|x| = x, so h(x)≈4xx=4h(x) \approx \frac{4x}{x} = 4.

As x→−∞x \to -\infty: |x|=−x|x| = -x, so h(x)≈4x−x=−4h(x) \approx \frac{4x}{-x} = -4.

Two horizontal asymptotes: y=4y = 4 and y=−4y = -4.

A related trick handles ∞−∞\infty - \infty forms such as x2+6x−x\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} - x: multiply by the conjugate to get 6xx2+6x+x\frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x}, then divide by xx. The limit is 61+1=3\frac{6}{1 + 1} = 3.

Exponentials, logarithms and the race to infinity

Some functions grow without bound, but at very different speeds. In the long run:

slowest ln⁡x\ln x, then x\sqrt{x}, then xx, then x3x^3, then exe^x fastest

"Slower" means the quotient of the slower function over the faster one approaches 0: limx→∞ln⁡xx\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{\sqrt{x}}=0= 0 and limx→∞x3ex\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^3}{e^x}=0= 0. A logarithm loses to every positive power of xx, a lower power loses to a higher one, and an exponential like exe^x beats every power. "Eventually" matters: x3x^3 is bigger than exe^x for xx between about 1.9 and 4.5, and after that it falls hopelessly behind.

Worked example 3. Find limx→∞3e2x+xe2x−5\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3e^{2x} + x}{e^{2x} - 5}.

The dominant term in both the top and the bottom is e2xe^{2x}. Divide everything by it:

3+xe2x1−5e2x\frac{3 + \frac{x}{e^{2x}}}{1 - \frac{5}{e^{2x}}}

The exponential beats xx, so xe2x→0\frac{x}{e^{2x}} \to 0; and 5e2x→0\frac{5}{e^{2x}} \to 0. The limit is 3.

On the left the story changes: limx→−∞ex=0\lim_{x \to -\infty} e^x = 0, so y=0y = 0 is a horizontal asymptote of exe^x on that side only. Bounded oscillations also fade when divided by something growing: −1x≤sin⁡xx≤1x-\frac{1}{x} \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le \frac{1}{x} for x>0x > 0, so by the squeeze theorem limx→∞sin⁡xx\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sin x}{x}=0= 0.
