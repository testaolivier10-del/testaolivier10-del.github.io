You can now write a differential equation, check a proposed solution and sketch solutions from a slope field. This page finds solutions by formula. The method, separation of variables, works whenever the variables can be pulled apart. On the AP® Calculus AB exam it is the only solving method you need.

When an equation is separable

A differential equation dydx=F(x,y)\frac{dy}{dx} = F(x, y) is separable when the right side is a product of a function of xx alone and a function of yy alone:

dydx=g(x)h(y).\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y).

The method in four steps

  1. Separate. Multiply and divide so that every yy is on the side with dydy and every xx is on the side with dxdx: 1h(y)dy=g(x)dx\frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx. Both dydy and dxdx stay in numerators.
  2. Integrate both sides. ∫1h(y)dy\int \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy=∫g(x)dx= \int g(x)\,dx.
  3. Add the constant now. Each side has its own constant, but C2−C1C_2 - C_1 is just one constant, so write a single +C+ C, usually on the xx side. Do it in this step, not later.
  4. Solve for yy if you can. The result, with CC still in it, is the general solution: a whole family of curves.

Worked example 1. Find the general solution of dydx=2xy2\frac{dy}{dx} = 2xy^2.

Separate: divide by y2y^2 and multiply by dxdx: y−2dy=2xdxy^{-2}\,dy = 2x\,dx.

Integrate: ∫y−2dy=∫2xdx\int y^{-2}\,dy = \int 2x\,dx, so −1y=x2+C-\frac{1}{y} = x^2 + C.

Solve: take reciprocals and change the sign: y=−1x2+Cy = -\frac{1}{x^2 + C}.

Check one case: with C=1C = 1, y=−(x2+1)−1y = -(x^2 + 1)^{-1} and dydx\frac{dy}{dx}=2x(x2+1)−2= 2x(x^2 + 1)^{-2}=2xy2= 2xy^2. It works.

Look at what would happen if you waited to add the constant: −1y=x2-\frac{1}{y} = x^2 gives y=−1x2y = -\frac{1}{x^2}, and then "+C+ C" gives y=−1x2+Cy = -\frac{1}{x^2} + C. That family is different, and for C≠0C \ne 0 it does not satisfy the equation. The constant must go in when you integrate.

Logarithms and the absolute value

Many separable equations put 1y\frac{1}{y} on the left. Its antiderivative is ln⁡|y|\ln|y|, with the absolute value. Keep it; it decides which solutions you find.

Worked example 2. Find the general solution of dydx=xy\frac{dy}{dx} = xy.

Separate: dyy=xdx\frac{dy}{y} = x\,dx (this assumes y≠0y \ne 0; see below).

Integrate: ln⁡|y|=x22+C\ln|y| = \frac{x^2}{2} + C.

Exponentiate both sides: |y||y|=ex2/2+C= e^{x^2/2 + C}=eCex2/2= e^{C}e^{x^2/2}.

Remove the absolute value: y=±eCex2/2y = \pm e^{C}e^{x^2/2}. The number ±eC\pm e^{C} can be any nonzero constant; call it AA. So y=Aex2/2y = Ae^{x^2/2}.

Finally, y=0y = 0 also satisfies dydx=xy\frac{dy}{dx} = xy (both sides are 0), but dividing by yy hid it. Allowing A=0A = 0 brings it back. General solution: y=Aex2/2y = Ae^{x^2/2} for any constant AA.

Two algebra facts do most of the work here. First, ea+b=eaebe^{a + b} = e^{a}e^{b}, which turns ex2/2+Ce^{x^2/2 + C} into a constant times ex2/2e^{x^2/2}; it is not ex2/2+Ce^{x^2/2} + C. Second, ln⁡|y|=u\ln|y| = u means |y|=eu|y| = e^{u}; you undo a logarithm by exponentiating the whole other side.

Shifted variables

The cooling equation dTdt=−0.05(T−20)\frac{dT}{dt} = -0.05(T - 20) separates as dTT−20=−0.05dt\frac{dT}{T - 20} = -0.05\,dt. The whole quantity T−20T - 20 stays together in the denominator, and its antiderivative is ln⁡|T−20|\ln|T - 20|:

ln⁡|T−20|=−0.05t+C,T=20+Ae−0.05t.\ln|T - 20| = -0.05t + C, \qquad T = 20 + Ae^{-0.05t}.

Common slips

  • Leaving an xx on the dydy side or a yy on the dxdx side. If the variables will not separate, the method does not apply.
  • Missing +C+ C, or adding it only after solving for yy.
  • Writing ln⁡y\ln y instead of ln⁡|y|\ln|y|, which loses the negative solutions.
  • Writing eu+C=eu+Ce^{u + C} = e^{u} + C. It is eCeue^{C}e^{u}, a constant times eue^{u}.
