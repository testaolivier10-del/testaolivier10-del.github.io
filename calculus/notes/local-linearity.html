Zoom in on the graph of any differentiable function, near any point, and it starts to look like a straight line: its tangent line. This is local linearity. It turns the tangent line into a tool for estimating values that are hard to compute, such as 26\sqrt{26} or f(2.1)f(2.1) when you only know ff at 2. The exam then asks one more question every time: is the estimate too big or too small?

The tangent line as an approximation

The tangent line at x=ax = a passes through (a,f(a))(a, f(a)) with slope f′(a)f'(a). In point-slope form it is the linearization of ff at aa:

L(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)

For xx close to aa, f(x)≈L(x)f(x) \approx L(x). Read the formula as a story: start at the known height f(a)f(a), then add the rate f′(a)f'(a) times the step x−ax - a. It is the "rate times change" idea from the derivative in context, written as a line.

Worked example 1. Estimate 26\sqrt{26}.

Choose a nearby point where x\sqrt{x} is easy: a=25a = 25, f(25)=5f(25) = 5.

f′(x)=12xf'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}, so f′(25)=110f'(25) = \frac{1}{10}.

L(26)L(26)=5+110(26−25)= 5 + \frac{1}{10}(26 - 25)=5.1= 5.1. A calculator gives 26≈5.0990\sqrt{26} \approx 5.0990, so the estimate is close, and slightly too large.

Worked example 2. A table gives f(5)=1f(5) = 1 and f′(5)=2f'(5) = 2. Estimate f(4.8)f(4.8).

L(x)=1+2(x−5)L(x) = 1 + 2(x - 5), so f(4.8)f(4.8)≈1+2(−0.2)\approx 1 + 2(-0.2)=0.6= 0.6.

The step x−a=−0.2x - a = -0.2 is negative because 4.8 is to the left of 5. Dropping that sign is a common slip.

Too big or too small? Concavity decides

A graph is concave up where it bends upward, like a cup, and concave down where it bends downward, like a cap. The second derivative tells which: f″>0f'' > 0 means f′f' is increasing, so the slopes keep growing and the graph bends up; f″<0f'' < 0 means the graph bends down. (Unit 5 studies concavity in full; here we need just this much.)

A concave up graph lies above its tangent lines, so the line gives values that are too small: an underestimate. A concave down graph lies below its tangent lines, so the line gives an overestimate.

Worked example 3. Use the tangent line to ln⁡x\ln x at x=1x = 1 to estimate ln⁡(1.2)\ln(1.2), and decide whether the estimate is too large or too small.

ln⁡1=0\ln 1 = 0 and the slope is 11=1\frac{1}{1} = 1, so L(x)=x−1L(x) = x - 1 and ln⁡(1.2)≈0.2\ln(1.2) \approx 0.2.

The second derivative of ln⁡x\ln x is −1x2-\frac{1}{x^2}, which is negative for every x>0x > 0. The graph is concave down, so the tangent line lies above it, and 0.20.2 is an overestimate. (Indeed ln⁡1.2≈0.182\ln 1.2 \approx 0.182.)

The justification must name concavity. "Overestimate because ff is increasing" does not earn the point: whether ff is increasing decides which way the line tilts, not which side of the curve it sits on. The concavity must hold on the whole interval between aa and the point you estimate.

How good is the estimate?

The gap between the curve and the line is tiny near aa and grows as you move away. Using the tangent line at x=1x = 1, ln⁡1.2\ln 1.2 is estimated as 0.20.2 (true value about 0.1820.182), but ln⁡3\ln 3 is estimated as 22 (true value about 1.0991.099). Always build the line at the known point closest to where you estimate.

Slips that cost points

  • Swapping the height and the slope in L(x)L(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)= f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).
  • Losing the sign of x−ax - a when estimating to the left of aa.
  • Justifying over or under with the sign of f′f', or with no reason.
  • Using a tangent line built at a point far from the estimate.
