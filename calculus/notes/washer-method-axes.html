Washer Method About the Axes

4 min read · freeNot practiced

Share to Google Classroom (opens in a new tab)

Slices with a hole

The disc method worked because every rectangle in the region stood on the axis. Spin it, and it sweeps out a solid disc. Now take a region that does not reach the axis, such as the area between y=xy = \sqrt{x} and y=1y = 1 for 1≤x≤41 \le x \le 4. Every vertical rectangle starts at height 1 and goes up to x\sqrt{x}. Spin that rectangle about the xx-axis and it sweeps out a ring: a disc of radius x\sqrt{x} with a hole of radius 1 cut out of the middle. That ring is a washer.

Two radii, both from the axis

A washer has two radii, and both start at the axis of rotation:

  • the outer radius RR reaches the edge of the region farther from the axis;
  • the inner radius rr reaches the edge nearer to the axis, where the hole ends.

The washer's area is the big disc minus the hole: πR2−πr2\pi R^2 - \pi r^2. So the washer method says

V=π∫ab(R(x)2−r(x)2)dx.V = \pi\int_a^b \left(R(x)^2 - r(x)^2\right)\,dx.

About the xx-axis, with ff on top and gg on the bottom (both nonnegative), R=f(x)R = f(x) and r=g(x)r = g(x). A disc is just a washer with r=0r = 0.

Worked example. The region bounded by y=xy = \sqrt{x}, y=1y = 1 and x=4x = 4 is revolved about the xx-axis. Find the volume.

Limits. x=1\sqrt{x} = 1 at x=1x = 1; the line x=4x = 4 closes the region. So 1≤x≤41 \le x \le 4.

Radii. The curve is farther from the axis: R=xR = \sqrt{x}. The line is nearer: r=1r = 1.

Volume.

V=π∫14[(x)2−12]dx=π∫14(x−1)dx=π[x22−x]14=π(4+12)=9π2.V = \pi\int_1^4 \left[(\sqrt{x})^2 - 1^2\right]\,dx = \pi\int_1^4 (x - 1)\,dx = \pi\left[\frac{x^2}{2} - x\right]_1^4 = \pi\left(4 + \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{9\pi}{2}.

Difference of squares, not square of the difference

The most common washer error is writing π∫(R−r)2dx\pi\int (R - r)^2\,dx. It treats the region's height as the radius of a solid disc, as if the region had been slid down to the axis. In the example it gives π∫14(x−1)2dx\pi\int_1^4 (\sqrt{x} - 1)^2\,dx =7π6= \frac{7\pi}{6}, about a quarter of the true volume. The ring is far from the axis, so it sweeps out much more than a disc of the same width would.

About the yy-axis

Revolving about the yy-axis gives horizontal washers stacked in yy. Write each curve as xx in terms of yy. The curve farther from the yy-axis (larger xx) gives RR, the nearer one gives rr, and the limits are yy-values.

Worked example. The region between y=2xy = 2x and y=x2y = x^2 is revolved about the yy-axis. Find the volume.

Rewrite. y=2xy = 2x becomes x=y2x = \frac{y}{2}; y=x2y = x^2 becomes x=yx = \sqrt{y}. They meet at y=0y = 0 and y=4y = 4.

Radii. At y=1y = 1: 1=1\sqrt{1} = 1 and 12=0.5\frac{1}{2} = 0.5. The parabola is farther from the yy-axis, so R=yR = \sqrt{y} and r=y2r = \frac{y}{2}. (About the xx-axis it was the other way round: the line was farther. Which curve is "outer" depends on the axis.)

Volume. VV =π∫04(y−y24)dy= \pi\int_0^4 \left(y - \frac{y^2}{4}\right)\,dy =π(8−163)= \pi\left(8 - \frac{16}{3}\right) =8π3= \frac{8\pi}{3}.

Checklist

  1. Sketch the region and the axis. Does the region touch the axis all along? If not, use washers.
  2. Draw one rectangle perpendicular to the axis. Label its far end and near end.
  3. RR = distance from the axis to the far end; rr = distance to the near end.
  4. Write π∫(R2−r2)\pi\int (R^2 - r^2) with the right variable and limits, then integrate.

On a calculator, type RR and rr in parentheses, square each, subtract, and multiply by π\pi, for example π∫−bb[(2+cos⁡x)2−(x2)2]dx\pi\int_{-b}^{b} \left[(2 + \cos x)^2 - (x^2)^2\right]\,dx. Store the crossings and give three decimal places.
