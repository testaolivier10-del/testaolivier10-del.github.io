2Differentiation: Definition and Fundamental Properties

The last topic described breaks in a graph. This topic turns "no break" into a precise test you can apply to a graph, a table or a formula, and write up as a justification. The test has three conditions, and every one of them matters.

The definition

A function ff is continuous at x=cx = c when all three of these hold:

  1. f(c)f(c) is defined. (There is a point on the graph at x=cx = c.)
  2. limx→cf(x)\lim_{x \to c} f(x) exists. (The curve arrives at one height from both sides.)
  3. limx→cf(x)\lim_{x \to c} f(x)=f(c)= f(c). (The point is exactly where the curve arrives.)

Each condition catches a different kind of break. Check them in order: the first one that fails is your reason.

First condition to failWhat the graph showsKind of discontinuity
1: f(c)f(c) undefinedno point at x=cx = cremovable (a hole) or infinite, depending on the limit
2: the limit does not existthe two sides arrive at different heights, or grow without boundjump or infinite
3: limit ≠f(c)\ne f(c)a hole with the point placed elsewhereremovable

Checking a formula

Worked example 1. Let f(x)=x2−4x−2f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} for x≠2x \ne 2 and f(2)=5f(2) = 5. Is ff continuous at x=2x = 2?

Condition 1: f(2)=5f(2) = 5, so it is defined.

Condition 2: for x≠2x \ne 2, f(x)f(x)=(x−2)(x+2)x−2= \frac{(x - 2)(x + 2)}{x - 2}=x= x+2+ 2, so limx→2f(x)=4\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 4. The limit exists.

Condition 3: 4≠54 \ne 5. It fails.

Conclusion: ff is not continuous at 2, because limx→2f(x)\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)=4≠5= 4 \ne 5=f(2)= f(2). Had the rule been f(2)=4f(2) = 4, all three conditions would hold.

Checking a piecewise function

At a break point, the one-sided limits come from different pieces. Find each one, then compare.

Worked example 2. Let g(x)=x2−1g(x) = x^2 - 1 for x<2x < 2, g(2)=3g(2) = 3, and g(x)=4x−5g(x) = 4x - 5 for x>2x > 2. Show that gg is continuous at x=2x = 2.

Condition 1: g(2)=3g(2) = 3.

Condition 2: limx→2−g(x)\lim_{x \to 2^-} g(x)=22−1= 2^2 - 1=3= 3 and limx→2+g(x)\lim_{x \to 2^+} g(x)=4(2)−5= 4(2) - 5=3= 3. The one-sided limits are equal, so limx→2g(x)=3\lim_{x \to 2} g(x) = 3.

Condition 3: limx→2g(x)\lim_{x \to 2} g(x)=3= 3=g(2)= g(2).

All three hold, so gg is continuous at x=2x = 2.

Notice the write-up. Each condition gets its own sentence with its own number. "The pieces match" or "there is no break" is a conclusion, not a justification.

Reading a graph or a table

On a graph, ask three questions at x=cx = c. Is there a closed dot? Does the curve arrive at the same height from both sides? Is the dot at that height? A closed dot at the end of one piece (a jump) passes the first question and fails the second. A dot floating above a hole passes the first two and fails the third.

A table can suggest continuity: if f(c)=2f(c) = 2 is listed and the values on both sides close in on 2, continuity at cc is likely. As always, a table estimates the limit; it does not prove it.

Why continuity matters

If ff is continuous at cc, then limx→cf(x)\lim_{x \to c} f(x)=f(c)= f(c). That single fact is behind direct substitution: polynomials are continuous everywhere, so their limits are their values. It also works in reverse. If you know a function is continuous at 4 and you can find its limit there, you know its value.

Worked example 3. ff is continuous at x=4x = 4 and limx→4(f(x)+2x)\lim_{x \to 4} (f(x) + 2x)=13= 13. Find f(4)f(4).

By the limit laws, limx→4f(x)\lim_{x \to 4} f(x)=13−limx→42x= 13 - \lim_{x \to 4} 2x=13−8= 13 - 8=5= 5.

Continuity at 4 gives f(4)f(4)=limx→4f(x)= \lim_{x \to 4} f(x)=5= 5.

Later in the course, big theorems start with "if ff is continuous". Each time, you will need to say why the function is continuous before you use the theorem. This definition is where that habit begins.
