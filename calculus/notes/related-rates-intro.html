Introduction to Related Rates

3 min read · freeNot practiced

Share to Google Classroom (opens in a new tab)

Many quantities change together. As a balloon fills, its radius and its volume both grow. As two cars drive away from a corner, the distance between them grows. An equation links the quantities at every instant, and differentiating that equation with respect to time links their rates. That is the whole idea of related rates; this page sets up the method, and the next topic solves full problems.

Everything is a function of time

In A=πr2A = \pi r^2 there is no tt in sight. But if the circle is a spreading ripple, then rr depends on tt, and so does AA. To find how fast AA changes, differentiate both sides with respect to tt. The right side is a composite function, π[r(t)]2\pi[r(t)]^2, so the chain rule applies:

dAdt=2πr⋅drdt\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r \cdot \frac{dr}{dt}

This is the same move as implicit differentiation, with tt in place of xx. Every variable that changes gets its rate as a factor; constants such as π\pi do not.

The equation explains something you can see on a pond. Even if the ripple's radius grows at a steady 3 cm per second, the area grows faster and faster, because the factor 2πr2\pi r grows. At r=5r = 5 cm the area grows at 2π(5)(3)=30π2\pi(5)(3) = 30\pi cm² per second; at r=10r = 10 cm, at 60π60\pi cm² per second.

The basic move, on common formulas

RelationDifferentiated with respect to tt
circle: A=πr2A = \pi r^2dAdt=2πrdrdt\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r\frac{dr}{dt}
sphere: V=43πr3V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3dVdt=4πr2drdt\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}
cube: V=s3V = s^3dVdt=3s2dsdt\frac{dV}{dt} = 3s^2\frac{ds}{dt}
rectangle: A=xyA = xydAdt=dxdty+xdydt\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{dx}{dt}y + x\frac{dy}{dt}

Worked example 1. A spherical balloon's radius grows at 2 cm per second. How fast does its volume grow when r=3r = 3 cm?

Relation: V=43πr3V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3. Differentiate with respect to tt: dVdt=4πr2drdt\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}.

Now substitute the instant: r=3r = 3 and drdt=2\frac{dr}{dt} = 2. dVdt=4π(9)(2)=72π\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi(9)(2) = 72\pi cm³ per second.

Worked example 2. Air is pumped into the balloon at 100 cm³ per second. How fast is the radius growing when r=5r = 5 cm?

Same equation, different unknown: 100=4π(25)drdt100 = 4\pi(25)\frac{dr}{dt}, so drdt\frac{dr}{dt}=100100π= \frac{100}{100\pi}=1π= \frac{1}{\pi}≈0.32\approx 0.32 cm per second.

Two rules that prevent most errors

Differentiate first, substitute second. The values of one instant (like r=3r = 3) are true only at that instant. If you put them into the equation before differentiating, they become constants with derivative 0, and the rates you need vanish.

Signs come from the words. "Increasing at 2 cm per second" means drdt=2\frac{dr}{dt} = 2; "decreasing at 2 cm per second" or "shrinking by 2 cm each second" means drdt=−2\frac{dr}{dt} = -2. A distance to a point that something approaches is decreasing, so its rate is negative.

Worked example 3. Car A drives east from an intersection at 60 mph and car B drives north at 45 mph. When A is 30 miles and B is 40 miles from the intersection, how fast is the distance between them growing?

Relation: x2+y2=z2x^2 + y^2 = z^2. Differentiate: 2xdxdt+2ydydt2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt}=2zdzdt= 2z\frac{dz}{dt}.

At this instant z=900+1600=50z = \sqrt{900 + 1600} = 50. Substitute: 30(60)+40(45)30(60) + 40(45)=50dzdt= 50\frac{dz}{dt}, so dzdt=360050=72\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{3600}{50} = 72 mph.

If car B were instead driving toward the intersection, dydt=−45\frac{dy}{dt} = -45 and dzdt\frac{dz}{dt}=1800−180050= \frac{1800 - 1800}{50}=0= 0: at that instant the distance would not be changing.

Slips that cost points

  • Differentiating with respect to xx or rr instead of tt, so no rates appear.
  • Dropping the chain rule factor: writing 4πr24\pi r^2 for dVdt\frac{dV}{dt}.
  • Substituting values before differentiating.
  • Entering a decreasing rate as positive.
  • Missing the product rule on a product of two changing quantities.
