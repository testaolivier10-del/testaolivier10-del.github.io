Once a slope field is drawn, you can read it like a weather map. It shows where solutions rise and fall, where they level off, and where they head in the long run, all without a formula. This page shows how to sketch the solution curve through a point, how to spot equilibrium solutions, and how to find the concavity of a solution from the differential equation itself.

Sketching the solution through a point

Put your pencil on the given point. The segment there shows which way to go. Move a little, look at the nearby segments, adjust, and keep going. Then go back to the point and do the same in the other direction. Rules that keep the sketch honest:

  • Pass exactly through the given point. The point (0,5)(0, 5) has x=0x = 0 and y=5y = 5; do not start at (5,0)(5, 0).
  • Follow the segments: the curve should be tangent to the segments it passes, never cutting across them.
  • Extend the curve across the whole field shown, to the left and to the right of the point.
  • Do not cross a row of horizontal segments that forms an equilibrium solution (next section).

Equilibrium solutions

Sometimes a whole horizontal line is made of flat segments. For dydx=3−y2\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3 - y}{2}, every point with y=3y = 3 has slope 0. Then the constant function y=3y = 3 is itself a solution: its derivative is 0, and the right side is 0 too. A constant solution like this is an equilibrium solution.

To find them, set the right side equal to 0 and solve for yy. The answer must work for every xx. For dydx=x2(y−3)(y+1)\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2(y - 3)(y + 1) the equilibrium solutions are y=3y = 3 and y=−1y = -1. The factor x2x^2 also makes the slope 0 along x=0x = 0, but a vertical line is not a function of xx, so it is not a solution.

Equilibrium solutions organize everything else. When the right side of the equation is a smooth formula in xx and yy, as in every slope field on this page, two solution curves never cross or touch. (A right side with y\sqrt{y}, such as a draining tank's dhdt=−15h\frac{dh}{dt} = -\frac{1}{5}\sqrt{h}, is the exception at h=0h = 0: the tank empties and the depth stays at 0.) So a solution that starts below y=3y = 3 stays below it. In the figure, slopes below 3 are positive and get gentler near 3, so the curve through (0,1)(0, 1) rises and flattens toward 3. Above 3 the slopes are negative, so the curve through (0,5.5)(0, 5.5) falls toward 3. Both approach 3 as xx grows.

Not every equilibrium attracts. For dydx=y−12\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 1}{2}, slopes are positive above y=1y = 1 and negative below it, so solutions move away from y=1y = 1 on both sides. A solution through (0,2)(0, 2) rises faster and faster; one through (0,0)(0, 0) falls without bound.

Increasing, decreasing and extrema

The sign of dydx\frac{dy}{dx} says where a solution rises or falls, exactly as for any function. For dydx=x(2−y)\frac{dy}{dx} = x(2 - y) and points below y=2y = 2, the factor 2−y2 - y is positive, so the slope has the sign of xx: negative left of the yy-axis, positive right of it. A solution crossing the yy-axis below y=2y = 2 therefore has a relative minimum there, because dydx\frac{dy}{dx} changes from negative to positive.

Concavity: the second derivative by the chain rule

To decide whether a solution curve is concave up or down, you need d2ydx2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}. Differentiate the right side of the equation with respect to xx, remembering that yy is a function of xx. So every yy you differentiate brings a factor dydx\frac{dy}{dx}, and then you replace dydx\frac{dy}{dx} by the right side of the equation.

Worked example. A solution of dydx=y2−x\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - x passes through (1,2)(1, 2). Is it concave up or down there?

Differentiate both sides with respect to xx: d2ydx2=2ydydx−1\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 2y\frac{dy}{dx} - 1. The 2ydydx2y\frac{dy}{dx} comes from the chain rule on y2y^2.

At (1,2)(1, 2): dydx=22−1=3\frac{dy}{dx} = 2^2 - 1 = 3. So d2ydx2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=2(2)(3)−1= 2(2)(3) - 1=11= 11.

Since d2ydx2=11>0\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 11 > 0, the solution curve is concave up at (1,2)(1, 2).

The classic slip is to write ddx(y2)=2y\frac{d}{dx}(y^2) = 2y, or even 0 "because there is no xx". Both forget that yy changes as xx changes. For dydx=y−12\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 1}{2} the correct second derivative is 12dydx=y−14\frac{1}{2}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 1}{4}, not 0.

Worked example 2. For dydx=x(2−y)\frac{dy}{dx} = x(2 - y), find d2ydx2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} in terms of xx and yy.

Product rule: d2ydx2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=1⋅(2−y)+x⋅(−dydx)= 1 \cdot (2 - y) + x \cdot \left(-\frac{dy}{dx}\right).

Substitute dydx=x(2−y)\frac{dy}{dx} = x(2 - y): d2ydx2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=(2−y)−x2(2−y)= (2 - y) - x^2(2 - y)=(2−y)(1−x2)= (2 - y)(1 - x^2).

Long-run behavior

In context, an equilibrium solution is often a value the quantity settles at. A drink with dTdt=−0.1(T−65)\frac{dT}{dt} = -0.1(T - 65) warms or cools toward 65 °F, the room temperature. Read the long run from the signs: below the equilibrium the rate is positive, above it negative, so the temperature approaches 65 from either side.
