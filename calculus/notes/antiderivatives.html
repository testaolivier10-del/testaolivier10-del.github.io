To use the Fundamental Theorem you need antiderivatives, and lots of them. This page collects the basic rules, introduces the notation for "all the antiderivatives at once," and shows how a single known point picks out the function you want.

A family, not a single function

x3/3−xx^3/3 - x has derivative x2−1x^2 - 1. So does x3/3−x+5x^3/3 - x + 5, and x3/3−x−2x^3/3 - x - 2: a constant has derivative 0. On an interval, these are the only antiderivatives; two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant. Graphically they are vertical shifts of one curve, as the figure shows.

The indefinite integral names the whole family:

∫(x2−1)dx=x33−x+C.\int (x^2 - 1)\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3} - x + C.

CC is the constant of integration. Leaving it off says "this one function," which is wrong: the scoring guidelines take a point for a missing +C+ C.

Constant factors come out and sums split: ∫(kf+g)dx\int (kf + g)\,dx=k∫fdx+∫gdx= k\int f\,dx + \int g\,dx. Products and quotients do not split, so rewrite them first.

Worked example 1. Find ∫(x+4x3−2cos⁡x)dx\int \left(\sqrt{x} + \frac{4}{x^3} - 2\cos x\right)dx.

Rewrite as powers: x=x1/2\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2} and 4x3=4x−3\frac{4}{x^3} = 4x^{-3}.

∫x1/2dx\int x^{1/2}\,dx=x3/23/2= \frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}=23x3/2= \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}. ∫4x−3dx\int 4x^{-3}\,dx=4⋅x−2−2= 4\cdot\frac{x^{-2}}{-2}=−2x2= -\frac{2}{x^2}. ∫2cos⁡xdx=2sin⁡x\int 2\cos x\,dx = 2\sin x.

Answer: 23x3/2−2x2−2sin⁡x+C\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{2}{x^2} - 2\sin x + C. Check: differentiating gives back x1/2+4x−3−2cos⁡xx^{1/2} + 4x^{-3} - 2\cos x.

Two more rewrites: expand a power of a sum, (x2+1)2=x4+2x2+1(x^2 + 1)^2 = x^4 + 2x^2 + 1, and split a fraction over a single term, x3−2xx=x2−2\frac{x^3 - 2x}{x} = x^2 - 2. Never integrate the top and bottom of a fraction separately.

Picking one function: use a known point

Worked example 2. f′(x)=3x2−4x+1f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4x + 1 and f(2)=5f(2) = 5. Find f(x)f(x).

Antidifferentiate: f(x)=x3−2x2+x+Cf(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 + x + C.

Substitute the point right away: f(2)f(2)=8−8+2+C= 8 - 8 + 2 + C=2+C= 2 + C=5= 5, so C=3C = 3.

f(x)=x3−2x2+x+3f(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 + x + 3.

With a second derivative, antidifferentiate twice; each step brings its own constant, and each needs its own piece of information, such as f′(0)f'(0) and then f(0)f(0).

Some functions have no formula for an antiderivative

Every continuous function has antiderivatives (the accumulation function ∫axf(t)dt\int_a^x f(t)\,dt is one), but some cannot be written with familiar functions: e−x2e^{-x^2} and sin⁡(x2)\sin(x^2) are examples. On the exam, integrals of such functions appear on calculator questions, and the calculator evaluates them numerically.
