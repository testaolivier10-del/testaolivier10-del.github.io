1.1 Can Change Happen at an Instant?

An average rate of change, (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a) , is a slope over an interval, with units of output per input. A rate at one instant cannot be a quotient, since nothing changes in zero time; instead, the averages over shrinking intervals close in on one number, the instantaneous rate of change. The next topic gives that closing in a name and a notation.

  • The average rate of change of ff over [a,b][a, b] is f(b)−f(a)b−a\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}: the slope of the line through (a,f(a))(a, f(a)) and (b,f(b))(b, f(b)). Its units are output units per input unit.
  • A rate of change at one instant cannot be computed as a quotient, because the interval would have length 0.
  • The instantaneous rate of change at t=at = a is the number the average rates over shorter and shorter intervals around aa close in on.
  • From a table, estimate the rate at an instant with the shortest interval that contains it; from both sides, average the two closest rates.
  • A negative rate means the quantity is decreasing; its size tells how fast.

1.3 Reading Limits from a Graph

Read a graph's limit one side at a time. The left-hand and right-hand limits are the heights the graph heads to from each side. If they exist and agree, that is the limit; if they differ, or the outputs are unbounded or oscillate, the limit does not exist. Dots give the value f(a) , which never affects the limit.

  • limx→a−f(x)\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) uses only x<ax < a; limx→a+f(x)\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) uses only x>ax > a. Follow the graph's heights on that side to the edge at x=ax = a.
  • limx→af(x)=L\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L exactly when both one-sided limits equal LL.
  • A limit fails to exist when the one-sided limits differ (a jump), when the outputs grow without bound, or when they oscillate.
  • Filled and open dots tell you f(a)f(a); they never decide the limit.
  • A justification names both one-sided limits: "From the left f(x)→3f(x) \to 3; from the right f(x)→1f(x) \to 1; since 3≠13 \ne 1, the limit does not exist."

1.4 Estimating Limits from Tables

A table estimates a limit when it lists outputs for inputs closing in on a from both sides. If both sides settle on the same number, that is the estimate; if they differ, grow without bound or never settle, the limit appears not to exist. A table suggests; it does not prove, so check closer inputs or use algebra when in doubt.

  • To estimate limx→af(x)\lim_{x \to a} f(x), evaluate ff at inputs like a−0.1,a−0.01,a−0.001a - 0.1, a - 0.01, a - 0.001 and a+0.001,a+0.01,a+0.1a + 0.001, a + 0.01, a + 0.1.
  • Read each side separately, exactly as on a graph: the left-hand limit uses inputs below aa, the right-hand limit inputs above aa.
  • The estimate is the number the outputs approach, not the last value in the table. A value f(a)f(a) in the table never decides the limit.
  • A table can mislead if it stops too early. If the values have not settled, test inputs even closer to aa.

1.5 Limit Laws

The limit laws let you split a limit over sums, differences, constant multiples, products, quotients (with a nonzero denominator limit), powers, roots and compositions. Together they justify direct substitution for polynomials and for rational functions where the denominator is not 0. When a denominator heads to 0, look closer: a nonzero numerator means no finite limit.

  • Sum, difference, constant multiple, product: if both limits exist, combine them the same way. lim(f+g)\lim (f + g)=limf+limg= \lim f + \lim g.
  • Quotient: limfg\lim \frac{f}{g}=limflimg= \frac{\lim f}{\lim g} only when limg≠0\lim g \ne 0.
  • Powers and roots: lim[f(x)]n=Ln\lim [f(x)]^n = L^n, and limf(x)=L\lim \sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{L} when L>0L > 0.
  • Direct substitution: for a polynomial, or a rational function with a nonzero denominator at aa, the limit is the value at aa.
  • For a composite, find the inside limit first: if g(x)→bg(x) \to b and ff is continuous at bb, then f(g(x))→f(b)f(g(x)) \to f(b).
  • For a piecewise function, use each side's formula for its one-sided limit.

1.6 Limits by Algebra: Factor, Rationalize, Simplify

When direct substitution gives the indeterminate form 0/0 , rewrite: factor and cancel, multiply by a conjugate, or simplify a complex fraction. The simplified expression matches the original except at x = a , so it has the same limit, which substitution now finds. Write every step with "lim" and true equals signs.

  • 00\frac{0}{0} is an indeterminate form: it means "rewrite first", never 0, 1 or "does not exist".
  • Canceling is legal in a limit because the limit never uses x=ax = a, and for x≠ax \ne a the canceled factor is not 0.
  • Toolkit: factor and cancel; multiply by a conjugate to clear a square root; combine a complex fraction; expand powers completely.
  • After simplifying, a nonzero number over 0 means no finite limit (unbounded); check one-sided signs if asked.
  • Keep "limx→a\lim_{x \to a}" on every line until you substitute; never write 00=…\frac{0}{0} = \ldots.

an indeterminate form: the limit is not yet known → they share a factor x − a (for polynomials), possibly hidden behind a square root or a fraction → the new expression equals the old one for every x ≠ a , so it has the same limit → the limit appears (or a nonzero over zero shows there is no finite limit)

1.7 Choosing a Way to Find a Limit

Substitute first and read the result. A number is the limit. 0/0 means rewrite: factor and cancel, multiply by a conjugate, or combine fractions, then substitute again. A nonzero number over 0 means the function is unbounded there. At a break point, compare the one-sided limits.

  • Substitute first. It is the fastest test, and its result tells you what to do next.
  • 00\frac{0}{0} is an indeterminate form: not an answer, a signal to rewrite.
  • A nonzero number over 0 means the function is unbounded near cc; no algebra will make the limit a number.
  • At a break point, the two sides can follow different rules. Find both one-sided limits.
  • Write the limit symbol on every line until you substitute: the simplified expression equals the original only for x≠cx \ne c.

1.8 The Squeeze Theorem

If g(x) ≤ f(x) ≤ h(x) near c and g and h have the same limit L at c , then f has limit L too. Bounds usually come from −1 ≤ sin ≤ 1 . The squeeze gives lim as x → 0, (sin x)/x = 1 in radians, and (sin (kx))/x → k .

  • Squeeze theorem: a function trapped between two functions with the same limit LL at cc also has limit LL at cc.
  • Both bounds must approach the same value. Bounds with different limits give no conclusion.
  • Build bounds from −1≤sin⁡θ≤1-1 \le \sin\theta \le 1 and −1≤cos⁡θ≤1-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1, multiplied by a factor that is not negative (use |x||x|, not xx).
  • The special limit limx→0sin⁡xx\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}=1= 1 comes from the squeeze. Match the inside: sin⁡(kx)x\frac{\sin(kx)}{x}=k⋅sin⁡(kx)kx→k= k \cdot \frac{\sin(kx)}{kx} \to k.
  • A justification states the inequality, shows the two bounds have the same limit, and names the squeeze theorem.

1.9 Limits Across Graphs, Tables and Formulas

A limit can be read from a graph (the height the curve approaches), estimated from a table (values on both sides), found exactly from a formula (algebra), and stated in words or notation. Move between them: estimate with a table, confirm with a formula, and check the sides when one function sits inside another.

  • The same limit can be read from a graph, a table, a formula or words. Each answer must agree.
  • On a graph, the limit is the height the curve approaches, not the closed dot at the point.
  • A table suggests a limit; a formula confirms it. Cleverly chosen inputs can make a table mislead, as with sin⁡(πx)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right).
  • Translate words exactly: "from values less than 4" is x→4−x \to 4^-; the number the outputs approach goes after the equals sign.
  • For f(g(x))f(g(x)), find the inner limit first and note the side it comes from.

1.10 Types of Discontinuity

A discontinuity is a point where the graph breaks. Removable: the limit exists but the value is missing or different (a hole). Jump: finite one-sided limits that differ. Infinite: unbounded near the point (a vertical asymptote). Classify by finding the one-sided limits and comparing them with f(c) .

  • A function is continuous at x=cx = c when limx→cf(x)\lim_{x \to c} f(x) exists and equals f(c)f(c); its graph has no break there. A discontinuity is a point where this fails.
  • Removable: the limit exists, but the point is missing or misplaced (a hole).
  • Jump: both one-sided limits are finite and different.
  • Infinite: the function is unbounded near cc (a vertical asymptote).
  • To classify, find the one-sided limits first, then compare them with f(c)f(c).

1.11 Continuity at a Point

f is continuous at c when f(c) is defined, lim as x → c, f(x) exists, and the two are equal. Check the three conditions in order and state each one with its number. Continuity at c is exactly what makes lim as x → c, f(x) = f(c) .

  • Continuous at cc means: (1) f(c)f(c) is defined, (2) limx→cf(x)\lim_{x \to c} f(x) exists, (3) limx→cf(x)\lim_{x \to c} f(x)=f(c)= f(c).
  • Check the conditions in order. The first one that fails tells you the reason, and the kind of discontinuity.
  • At the break point of a piecewise function, find both one-sided limits from the pieces, then compare with the value the function is given there.
  • A justification shows all three conditions with numbers: "f(2)=3f(2) = 3, limx→2f(x)=3\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 3, so limx→2f(x)\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)=f(2)= f(2)."
  • If ff is continuous at cc, its limit at cc is just f(c)f(c). That is why direct substitution works.

1.12 Continuity on an Interval

A function is continuous on an interval when it is continuous at every point of it, with one-sided continuity at closed endpoints. The familiar function families are continuous on their domains, and sums, products, quotients and compositions keep that. So you only need to check where a function is undefined and where a piecewise rule switches.

  • Continuous on an interval: continuous at every point of it. At a closed endpoint, only the one-sided limit from inside must match the value.
  • Polynomials, exe^x, sin⁡x\sin x and cos⁡x\cos x are continuous on (−∞,∞)(-\infty, \infty).
  • Rational functions, roots, ln⁡x\ln x and tan⁡x\tan x are continuous on their domains: avoid zeros of denominators, negative inputs to even roots, inputs to ln⁡\ln that are not positive, and zeros of cos⁡x\cos x for tan⁡x\tan x.
  • Sums, products, quotients (denominator not 0) and compositions of continuous functions are continuous.
  • For a piecewise function, each piece is handled by the rules above; check each break point with the three conditions.

1.13 Removing a Discontinuity

A removable discontinuity is removed by defining f(c) to be the limit at c . A piecewise function is made continuous at a break point by choosing constants so the two one-sided limits and the value agree; each break point gives one equation. Jumps and infinite discontinuities cannot be removed by choosing a value.

  • To remove a removable discontinuity, find L=limx→cf(x)L = \lim_{x \to c} f(x) and define f(c)=Lf(c) = L.
  • At a break point of a piecewise function, set the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit and the value equal. Each break point gives one equation.
  • A constant can only move its own piece. If the value at the point and the other piece already disagree, no constant fixes it.
  • A jump or an infinite discontinuity cannot be removed: the limit does not exist, so no value can equal it.
  • Write the result as a check: substitute your constant back and show both sides give the same number.

1.14 Infinite Limits and Vertical Asymptotes

An infinite limit, lim as x → c, f(x) = ±∞ , says f grows without bound near c ; the limit does not exist as a number. Find each side's sign from the signs of the numerator and the shrinking denominator. Any infinite one-sided limit at c makes x = c a vertical asymptote; a canceled factor makes a hole instead.

  • limx→cf(x)=∞\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = \infty means f(x)f(x) grows beyond every bound as xx nears cc. It is an infinite limit: the limit does not exist, and this says how.
  • Find each side by signs: a nonzero numerator over a denominator approaching 0 from the positive or negative side.
  • If the two sides agree, write limx→cf(x)=∞\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = \infty (or −∞-\infty). If they disagree, give each one-sided limit.
  • x=cx = c is a vertical asymptote when at least one one-sided limit at cc is ∞\infty or −∞-\infty.
  • For a rational function, cancel common factors first: a canceled factor gives a hole, a factor left in the denominator gives an asymptote.

1.15 Limits at Infinity and Horizontal Asymptotes

A limit at infinity describes where f(x) heads as x → ∞ or x → −∞ ; a finite one gives a horizontal asymptote. Divide by the dominant term: for rational functions compare degrees. Watch √x² = |x| on the left. In the long run ln x grows slower than any power and e^x faster than any power.

  • limx→∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L means f(x)f(x) approaches LL as xx grows without bound. A finite limit at ∞\infty or −∞-\infty gives a horizontal asymptote y=Ly = L.
  • For a rational function, divide by the highest power of xx in the denominator. Equal degrees: ratio of leading coefficients. Bottom higher: 0. Top higher: no horizontal asymptote.
  • x2=|x|\sqrt{x^2} = |x|. As x→−∞x \to -\infty, x2=−x\sqrt{x^2} = -x, which can flip the sign of the answer.
  • Dominant terms: in the long run ln⁡x\ln x loses to every positive power, and exe^x beats every power.
  • Vertical asymptote: limx→cf(x)=±∞\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = \pm\infty. Horizontal asymptote: limx→±∞f(x)=L\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = L. Do not mix them up.

terms like 1/x , 5/x² and e^(−x) shrink to 0 → every other term becomes one of those shrinking terms, and the limit can be read off → y = L is a horizontal asymptote; the graph levels off there (and may cross it in the middle) → the faster one dominates: as x → ∞ , ln x loses to any power x^p ( p > 0 ), and x^p loses to e^x

1.16 The Intermediate Value Theorem

If f is continuous on [a, b] and d is between f(a) and f(b) , then f(c) = d for at least one c in (a, b) . A justification states both hypotheses with reasons and numbers, names the theorem, and concludes "for some c in (a, b) ". Without continuity on the closed interval, the conclusion can fail.

  • Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT): if ff is continuous on [a,b][a, b] and dd is between f(a)f(a) and f(b)f(b), then f(c)=df(c) = d for at least one cc in (a,b)(a, b).
  • Hypotheses come first: continuity on the closed interval, with a reason ("polynomial", "stated in the problem"), and dd between the endpoint values, with the numbers.
  • The conclusion is existence: at least one cc. The theorem does not say where, or how many.
  • With a table, apply the theorem on each pair of neighboring inputs; non-overlapping intervals give separate solutions.
  • Finding cc is a separate job: algebra, or a calculator (three decimal places).

its graph has no break from (a, f(a)) to (b, f(b)) → the horizontal line y = d separates the two endpoints of the graph → it must cross the line: f(c) = d for at least one c in (a, b) → the theorem promises nothing; the value may or may not be reached
