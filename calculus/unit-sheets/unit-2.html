2.1 Average and Instantaneous Rates of Change

An average rate of change is a change in output over a change in input, the slope of a secant line. Writing the second input as a + h gives the difference quotient. Letting h → 0 shrinks the interval to one instant, and the limit of the difference quotient, when it exists, is the instantaneous rate of change.

  • The average rate of change of ff on [a,b][a, b] is f(b)−f(a)b−a\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}: the change in output over the change in input, with units of output per input.
  • A secant line joins two points of a graph. Its slope is the average rate of change between them.
  • The difference quotient f(a+h)−f(a)h\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} is the average rate on the interval from aa to a+ha + h; hh can be negative.
  • The instantaneous rate of change at x=ax = a is limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)h\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}, when that limit exists. Simplify the quotient first; substituting h=0h = 0 at the start gives 00\frac{0}{0}.

The average rate of change of ff between x=ax = a and x=a+hx = a + h: f(a+h)−f(a)h\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} for h≠0h \ne 0. Its limit as h→0h \to 0 is the instantaneous rate of change at aa.

A line through two points of a graph. Its slope is the average rate of change of the function between those two inputs.

2.2 The Derivative and Its Notation

The derivative f′(a) is the limit of the difference quotient as h → 0 : the instantaneous rate of change of f at a and the slope of the tangent line there. Found at every x , it is the function f′(x) = dy/dx . The tangent line at a is y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) .

  • The derivative of ff at aa is f′(a)f'(a)=limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)h= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}, or equivalently limx→af(x)−f(a)x−a\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}. Both are the same limit of secant slopes.
  • The tangent line at x=ax = a passes through (a,f(a))(a, f(a)) with slope f′(a)f'(a): y=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).
  • Done at every xx, the limit gives the derivative function f′(x)f'(x). In Leibniz notation, y=f(x)y = f(x) has derivative dydx\frac{dy}{dx}, and ddx\frac{d}{dx} means "the derivative of".
  • A derivative has the units of a rate: output units per input unit.

2.3 Estimating a Derivative at a Point

With data instead of a formula, estimate f′(a) with a difference quotient from two table inputs on either side of a , as close as possible, and give the units. A symmetric quotient is the best simple estimate; a calculator's numerical derivative does the same with a tiny h , reported to three decimal places.

  • From a table, estimate f′(a)f'(a) by f(b)−f(c)b−c\frac{f(b) - f(c)}{b - c} with c<a<bc < a < b: the two closest data points on either side of aa.
  • At the end of a table, only one side exists: use the closest one-sided quotient.
  • The symmetric difference quotient f(a+h)−f(a−h)2h\frac{f(a + h) - f(a - h)}{2h} averages the left and right quotients and is usually the most accurate.
  • A numerical derivative on a graphing calculator gives a decimal estimate of f′(a)f'(a); report it to three decimal places.
  • From a graph, read the slope of the tangent line from two points on it.

2.4 Differentiability and Continuity

A function is differentiable at a when f′(a) exists. Differentiability implies continuity, but not the other way round: corners, cusps and vertical tangents are continuous points with no derivative, and any discontinuity also rules a derivative out. At a break in a piecewise function, check continuity, then compare the one-sided slopes.

  • ff is differentiable at aa when f′(a)f'(a) exists: the limit of the difference quotient is a finite number.
  • Differentiable implies continuous. In a justification, say it: "ff is differentiable at aa, so ff is continuous at aa."
  • Continuous does NOT imply differentiable. A continuous graph fails to have a derivative at a corner (one-sided slopes differ), a cusp (slopes become infinite in opposite directions) or a vertical tangent line.
  • Not continuous implies not differentiable: a jump, hole or asymptote rules out a derivative.
  • For a piecewise function at the break: check continuity first, then compare the left and right slopes.

f(a + h) − f(a) = (f(a + h) − f(a))/h ⋅h tends to f′(a)⋅0 = 0 → lim as x → a, f(x) = f(a) : f is continuous at a → the two-sided limit does not exist and f′(a) does not exist, even though f may be continuous → there is no finite slope, so f′(a) does not exist

differentiable
A function is differentiable at x=ax = a when f′(a)f'(a)=limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)h= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} exists as a finite number, and differentiable on an interval when it is differentiable at every point of it. Differentiable implies continuous; the converse is false.
corner
A point where a continuous graph has no derivative: a corner (the left and right slopes are different numbers, as for |x||x| at 0), a cusp (the slopes grow without bound in opposite directions, as for x2/3x^{2/3} at 0) or a vertical tangent line (as for x3\sqrt[3]{x} at 0).

2.5 The Power Rule

The power rule says d/dx (x^r) = rx^(r − 1) for every real exponent r . Rewrite roots and fractions as powers of x first, subtract 1 carefully with negative and fractional exponents, and remember the rule needs the variable in the base, not in the exponent.

  • The power rule: ddx(xr)=rxr−1\frac{d}{dx}\left( x^r \right) = r x^{r - 1} for any real number rr. Bring the exponent down, then subtract 1 from it.
  • Rewrite first: x=x1/2\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}, 1x4=x−4\frac{1}{x^4} = x^{-4}, 1x3=x−1/3\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x}} = x^{-1/3}.
  • With negative exponents, "one less" means more negative: −4−1=−5-4 - 1 = -5.
  • Special cases: ddx(x)=1\frac{d}{dx}(x) = 1, and the slope of x2x^2 at xx is 2x2x.
  • The rule needs the variable in the base and a fixed number in the exponent.

in each case the exponent comes down in front and the new exponent is one less → the power rule: d/dx (x^r) = rx^(r − 1) → rewrite it with a fractional or negative exponent before using the rule → it is not a power of x , and the power rule does not apply

power rule
For any real number rr, ddx(xr)=rxr−1\frac{d}{dx}\left( x^r \right) = r x^{r - 1}: bring the exponent down and lower it by one. Roots and reciprocals are rewritten as powers first, such as x=x1/2\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2} and 1x2=x−2\frac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}.

2.6 Constant, Sum, Difference and Constant Multiple Rules

Constants added to a function disappear when you differentiate; constant factors stay. Derivatives of sums and differences are sums and differences of derivatives. With the power rule, these rules differentiate any polynomial term by term, and they work on tables of values as well as formulas.

  • Constant rule: ddx(c)=0\frac{d}{dx}(c) = 0. Adding a constant shifts a graph up or down and changes no slope.
  • Constant multiple rule: ddx(kf(x))=kf′(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left( kf(x) \right) = kf'(x).
  • Sum and difference rules: ddx(f(x)±g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left( f(x) \pm g(x) \right)=f′(x)±g′(x)= f'(x) \pm g'(x).
  • Together with the power rule: ddx(4x3−5x+2)\frac{d}{dx}\left( 4x^3 - 5x + 2 \right)=12x2−5= 12x^2 - 5.
  • These rules work on values too: if h=3f−2gh = 3f - 2g, then h′(2)=3f′(2)−2g′(2)h'(2) = 3f'(2) - 2g'(2).

its derivative is 0: d/dx (c) = 0 → every slope is multiplied by k : (kf)′ = kf′ → rates add: (f + g)′ = f′ + g′ , and (f − g)′ = f′ − g′ → differentiate it term by term with the power rule

The basic derivative rules: the constant rule ddx(c)=0\frac{d}{dx}(c) = 0; the constant multiple rule ddx(kf(x))=kf′(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left( kf(x) \right) = kf'(x); and the sum and difference rules ddx(f(x)±g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left( f(x) \pm g(x) \right)=f′(x)±g′(x)= f'(x) \pm g'(x).

2.7 Derivatives of sin x, cos x, e to the x and ln x

The derivatives of sine and cosine are cosine and negative sine (in radians); e^x is its own derivative; and ln x has derivative 1/x for x > 0 . With the sum and constant multiple rules they combine freely, and any limit of the form lim as h → 0, (f(a + h) − f(a))/h can be read as f′(a) and evaluated with them.

  • Learn the four: ddx(sin⁡x)=cos⁡x\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x, ddx(cos⁡x)=−sin⁡x\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x, ddx(ex)=ex\frac{d}{dx}\left( e^x \right) = e^x, ddx(ln⁡x)=1x\frac{d}{dx}(\ln x) = \frac{1}{x} for x>0x > 0.
  • The trigonometric formulas hold only in radians.
  • The power rule does not apply to exe^x: the variable is in the exponent.
  • A limit as a derivative: limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)h\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} is f′(a)f'(a). Name ff and aa, then use the derivative formula.

2.8 The Product Rule

When two factors both change, the product changes by each factor's change times the other factor: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′ . It is never just f′g′ . For three factors, differentiate one at a time and add the three terms. From a table, the rule needs the values as well as the derivatives.

  • The product rule: ddx(f(x)g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left( f(x)g(x) \right)=f′(x)g(x)= f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g′(x)+ f(x)g'(x).
  • In words: derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second.
  • The derivative of a product is NOT the product of the derivatives: (x⋅x)′=2x(x \cdot x)' = 2x, but 1⋅1=11 \cdot 1 = 1.
  • From a table: (fg)′(a)(fg)'(a)=f′(a)g(a)+f(a)g′(a)= f'(a)g(a) + f(a)g'(a) uses both the value columns and the derivative columns.
  • If one factor is a constant, the product rule reduces to the constant multiple rule.

2.9 The Quotient Rule

The derivative of f/g is (f′g − fg′)/g² , derived from the product rule by writing f = f/g ⋅g . Keep the numerator in order and square the denominator. When the denominator is a single term, dividing first and using the power rule is often simpler.

  • The quotient rule: ddx(f(x)g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left( \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \right)=f′(x)g(x)−f(x)g′(x)(g(x))2= \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{\left( g(x) \right)^2}, where g(x)≠0g(x) \ne 0.
  • Memory aid: "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared."
  • The order in the numerator matters: swapping it flips the sign of the answer.
  • The derivative of a quotient is NOT the quotient of the derivatives.
  • For 3x2−5x\frac{3x^2 - 5}{x}, divide first: 3x−5x−13x - 5x^{-1}, then use the power rule.

quotient rule

2.10 Derivatives of tan, cot, sec and csc
