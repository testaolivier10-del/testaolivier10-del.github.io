7.1 Writing Differential Equations

A differential equation states how fast a quantity changes. Translate words carefully: "the rate of change of y " is dy/dt , "proportional to" is " k times", and the sign of the right side tells you where y rises or falls. One known rate fixes k .

  • A differential equation relates a derivative to the variables: dydt=0.4(50−y)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0.4(50 - y).
  • "The rate of change of yy is proportional to yy" is dydt=ky\frac{dy}{dt} = ky. The derivative is on the left; the quantity is on the right.
  • "Proportional to the difference between TT and 20" is dTdt=k(T−20)\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - 20).
  • The sign of dydt\frac{dy}{dt} says where yy increases or decreases; you can read it before knowing any formula for yy.
  • kk has units: for dPdt=kP\frac{dP}{dt} = kP with tt in hours, kk is "per hour".

write the rate as a derivative and the dependence as a formula: dP/dt = kP → dy/dt = k⋅(that quantity) ; "inversely proportional" puts the quantity in the denominator → the sign of dy/dt is known, so you know where the quantity grows and where it shrinks → solve for k , then predict the rate at any other value

7.2 Verifying Solutions to Differential Equations

To verify a solution to a differential equation, differentiate the function, substitute into both sides and show they agree for every x in an interval. One equation can have infinitely many solutions, and a solution may hold only on an interval where everything is defined.

  • A solution to a differential equation is a function, not a number. It makes the equation true for every xx in an interval.
  • To verify a solution: differentiate, substitute into both sides, simplify each side, compare.
  • Checking a single point is not enough: two different expressions can agree at one xx.
  • One equation can have a whole family of solutions, such as y=Ce3xy = Ce^{3x} for dydx=3y\frac{dy}{dx} = 3y.
  • A solution may live on an interval only: y=1xy = \frac{1}{x} solves dydx=−y2\frac{dy}{dx} = -y^2 on x>0x > 0 and on x<0x < 0, not across x=0x = 0.

compute every derivative the equation uses → simplify each side separately, never moving terms across the = sign → the function is a solution on that interval → the function is not a solution there → one differential equation usually has infinitely many solutions

solution to a differential equation

7.3 Sketching Slope Fields

A slope field draws dy/dx as a short segment at each point. Substitute the point's coordinates, get the slope, draw the segment. Look for where the slope is 0 or undefined, and for rows or columns of parallel segments.

  • At each point, compute dydx\frac{dy}{dx} and draw a short segment with that slope, centered on the point.
  • Slope 0 gives a horizontal segment; a slope that is undefined (division by 0) gives a vertical one.
  • If dydx\frac{dy}{dx} depends only on xx, segments in each vertical column are parallel. If it depends only on yy, segments in each horizontal row are parallel.
  • Find where the slope is 0 first: those lines organize the whole field.
  • Make steep slopes look steep and gentle ones gentle; the sign matters most.

each point of the plane gets a slope → you get the number the segment through that point must have as its slope → positive slopes rise to the right, negative ones fall, zero is flat → the field shows how every solution curve bends

7.4 Reasoning with Slope Fields

Trace a solution through a point by following the segments across the field. Rows of flat segments are equilibrium solutions, which other solutions approach or leave but never cross. The sign of dy/dx gives direction; d²y/dx² , found with the chain rule, gives concavity.

  • Sketch a solution through a point by following the segments both ways, all the way across the field; never cut across the segments.
  • An equilibrium solution y=cy = c makes dydx=0\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 for every xx. Solve "right side =0= 0" for yy.
  • The sign of dydx\frac{dy}{dx} says where a solution rises or falls; long-run behavior shows whether curves approach an equilibrium or move away.
  • Concavity: d2ydx2=ddx(dydx)\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right). Every yy is a function of xx, so ddx(y)=dydx\frac{d}{dx}(y) = \frac{dy}{dx}; then substitute the equation.

it runs along the segment there and keeps following the segments in both directions → y = c is an equilibrium solution, a row of flat segments → a curve that starts below an equilibrium line stays below it → its sign at a point tells the concavity of the solution there

7.6 General Solutions by Separation of Variables

For a separable equation, put y with dy and x with dx , integrate both sides, and add one constant immediately. Keep ln |y| ; when you exponentiate, the constant absorbs the sign. The general solution is a family of curves, one for each constant.

  • Separable means the right side is a product (or quotient) of a function of xx and a function of yy. dydx=x+y\frac{dy}{dx} = x + y is not separable.
  • Separate completely: only yy and dydy on one side, only xx and dxdx on the other. dydy and dxdx stay in the numerators.
  • Integrate both sides and write +C+ C once, right away. Adding it after solving for yy gives a different, wrong family.
  • ∫dyy=ln⁡|y|\int \frac{dy}{y} = \ln|y|. Then |y|=eCe…|y| = e^{C}e^{\dots}, and ±eC\pm e^{C} becomes a constant AA that may be any sign.

the equation is separable: 1/(h(y)) dy = g(x) dx → each side becomes an antiderivative; the two constants combine into one C → keep the absolute value; exponentiating gives |y| = e^Ce^(G(x)) , so y = Ae^(G(x)) with A any constant → the general solution: a family of curves, one for each constant

7.7 Particular Solutions from Initial Conditions

A particular solution satisfies the differential equation and an initial condition. Separate, integrate, add C , substitute the initial point immediately, then solve for y with the sign that fits. Give its domain: the open interval around the initial x where it is defined and differentiable.

  • An initial condition y(x0)=y0y(x_0) = y_0 says the solution passes through (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0). Put x0x_0 in for xx and y0y_0 in for yy.
  • Use it as soon as CC appears. Solving for yy first invites algebra slips and wrong constants.
  • After a square root, keep the sign that matches y0y_0: y(1)=−3y(1) = -3 needs the negative root.
  • State the domain: an open interval containing x0x_0 on which the formula is defined and its derivative exists.

an equation with one constant C → a number for C , before any rearranging → one function, the particular solution → the domain is the largest open interval around the initial x where the solution is defined and differentiable

7.8 Exponential Models

When the rate of change is proportional to the amount, dy/dt = ky and y = y_0e^kt . k > 0 is growth, k < 0 decay; k is the rate as a fraction of the amount per unit time. Find k by dividing two values and taking ln ; doubling time and half-life are (ln 2)/(|k|) .

  • dydt=ky\frac{dy}{dt} = ky and y(0)=y0y(0) = y_0 give y=y0ekty = y_0e^{kt}. You may quote this result.
  • k=dy/dtyk = \frac{dy/dt}{y}: the rate of change as a fraction of the current amount, in "per unit time" (per hour, per year).
  • Doubling time (k>0k > 0) and half-life (k<0k < 0) are both ln⁡2|k|\frac{\ln 2}{|k|}; they do not depend on the starting amount.
  • To find kk from two values, divide first: y1y0=ekt1\frac{y_1}{y_0} = e^{kt_1}, then take ln⁡\ln.
  • Cooling toward room temperature TrT_r is exponential in T−TrT - T_r, not in TT.

dy/dt = ky → y = y_0e^kt , the exponential model → growth or decay; |k| is the rate as a fraction of the amount, per unit time → e^(kt_1) = (y_1)/(y_0) , so k = 1/(t_1) ln (y_1)/(y_0)
