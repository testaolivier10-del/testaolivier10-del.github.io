Adult & Child Foreign Body Airway Obstruction (1 year and over)

Signs of choking — can the patient cough, speak or cry?
Yes — mild / partial
Encourage forceful coughing. Do not do anything else. Stay with them and watch for it becoming severe
No — severe / complete
5 back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts — repeat the cycle
↓ continue
A cough moves more air than any thrust you can deliver. Intervening on a patient who is still coughing effectively can turn a partial obstruction into a complete one.
2025 AHA change. Back blows now come first, for adults as well as children — this used to be abdominal thrusts alone. In a cohort of 709 choking patients, back blows relieved more obstructions, with fewer injuries, than thrusts. If you cannot get your arms around the abdomen — late pregnancy, or significant obesity — give 5 chest thrusts in place of the abdominal thrusts.
Result of the cycle?
Object expelled
Reassess airway and breathing, monitor, transport for evaluation
Patient becomes unresponsive
Lower to the ground and begin CPR — look in the mouth before each set of breaths and remove an object only if you can see it

Infant Foreign Body Airway Obstruction (under 1 year)

Infant choking — can they cry, cough or make any sound?
Yes — mild / partial
Let them cough. Do not intervene. Keep them calm and watch closely
No — severe / complete
5 back blows between the shoulder blades, head lower than the chest
↓ continue
No abdominal thrusts under 1 year. An infant’s liver sits low and largely unprotected by the rib cage, and abdominal thrusts can tear it.
2025 AHA change. The chest thrusts are delivered with the heel of one hand, not two fingers. Two fingers did not reliably reach the depth needed, and the technique was dropped for infant compressions as well.
Turn the infant over, 5 chest thrusts — heel of one hand on the lower sternum
↓
Object out?
No, still responsive
Repeat 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until it clears or the infant goes unresponsive
Infant becomes unresponsive
Begin CPR — look in the mouth before each set of breaths, and remove an object only if you can see it. Never sweep blindly

Anaphylaxis Recognition & Management

Allergic reaction after a likely exposure — is it anaphylaxis?
Anaphylaxis needs more than one body system, or hypotension after a known allergen. Skin findings alone — hives, flushing, itching — are an allergic reaction, not anaphylaxis, however dramatic they look.
Skin only — hives, itching, flushing
Allergic reaction, not anaphylaxis. Supportive care, transport, and reassess often — it can progress
Skin plus airway, breathing or circulation — or hypotension alone after a known allergen
Anaphylaxis: stridor, hoarseness, tongue or throat swelling, wheeze, vomiting, dizziness, hypotension
↓ treat now
Epinephrine into the lateral thigh — the patient’s own auto-injector or your own supply, per standing orders or medical direction
↓
Call for ALS and start moving to the ambulance now — do not wait to see whether it works
↓
High-flow oxygen, supine with legs raised if hypotensive, sitting up if breathing is the problem
↓
Improving?
No, after about 5 minutes
Repeat epinephrine per protocol, continue support, transport emergency traffic
Yes
Keep transporting and keep watching — a biphasic reaction can return hours later, so this patient goes to hospital regardless

Scene safe, BSI taken

30:2, 100–120/min, 2–2.4 in. deep, full recoil
↓ continue
Pulse, not breathing
Rescue breaths, 1 every 6 seconds. Recheck the pulse every 2 minutes — start CPR if it is lost
Attach the AED as soon as it arrives, analyze, shock if advised
↓
Resume compressions immediately after the shock, or immediately if no shock is advised
↓
Switch compressors every 2 minutes, at the AED’s rhythm check
↓
Continue until ROSC, handoff to higher care, or you are physically unable to continue
The AED prompting a re-analysis is a pause in the cycle, not a reason to stop resuscitating. Every two minutes it asks you to stand clear, it analyzes, and compressions resume either way.

Shock Recognition & General Management

Assess perfusion: skin color, temperature and moisture; pulse rate and quality; mental status; cap refill
↓
Treat the cause you can treat — control severe bleeding first of all
↓
Oxygen titrated to 94–98%, keep the patient warm, supine unless injuries dictate otherwise
↓
Compensated or decompensated?
Compensated — tachycardic, anxious, still mentating, BP holding
Prompt transport. Reassess vitals every 5 minutes: the blood pressure holds until it suddenly does not, and catching that moment is the whole point of reassessing
Decompensated — hypotensive, altered, weak or absent peripheral pulses
Load and go. Request ALS intercept, assist ventilation if breathing is inadequate, and do the rest of the assessment en route
The management above the fork is the same either way, which is why it sits above the fork. What changes is urgency: a decompensated patient has already spent the reserve the compensated one is still using.

START has four categories: Minor, Delayed, Immediate and Deceased. There is no Expectant tag in START — that belongs to SALT, below. Under START a patient who is breathing is never tagged black.

SALT Triage

SALT — Sort, Assess, Lifesaving interventions, Treatment/Transport — is the other system taught nationally, and the one that has an Expectant category. It differs from START in two ways that matter: it sorts by voice command before touching anyone, and it allows a handful of interventions during triage rather than after it.
STEP 1 — SORT, by voice, from where you stand
Walks when asked
Assess third
↓ to step 2
Waves or makes a purposeful movement
Assess second
↓ to step 2
Still, or has an obvious life threat
Assess first
↓ to step 2
STEP 2 — LIFESAVING INTERVENTIONS, done now, during triage: control major hemorrhage; open the airway (two rescue breaths for a child); chest decompression and auto-injector antidotes where in scope
↓
STEP 3 — ASSESS and assign
Not breathing after the airway is opened
DEAD
Injuries incompatible with survival given the resources you actually have
EXPECTANT — reassess if resources improve
Obeys commands, has a peripheral pulse, not in respiratory distress, major bleeding controlled — but injuries are not minor
DELAYED
Any of those missing
IMMEDIATE
Injuries are minor
MINIMAL
Expectant is not a synonym for dead, and it is not permanent. It means this patient cannot be saved with what is on scene right now — and if more crews arrive, the category is meant to be revisited.
