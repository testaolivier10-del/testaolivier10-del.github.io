Abdominal pain: assessment and care

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

An 81-year-old woman tells you her stomach "feels a bit off." Her pulse is 78 and she is not complaining much. Her medication list includes a beta blocker. Nothing about her looks dramatic, and that is the trap.

Abdominal emergencies are easy to underestimate, most of all in older patients. The assessment is built to catch shock early, to ask the questions patients do not volunteer, and to keep you from being reassured by an exam that finds little.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findYou are called for abdominal pain. These patients often vomit.You doStart with the scene size-up and put on the protection you need before you approach. Keep suction within reach from the start.
  2. You findIn the primary assessment the patient is anxious or confused, with pale, cool, moist skin, a rapid pulse and rapid breathing.You doRecognize early shock before you take a single vital sign. Make the patient a high priority, give oxygen if it is indicated, and plan prompt transport.
  3. You findThe patient is stable enough for a history.You doUse OPQRST and SAMPLE, then do the physical exam and take vital signs, repeating them every 5 minutes in an unstable patient.
  4. You findThe patient is a woman of childbearing age.You doAsk directly and in private: where she is in her cycle, whether her period is late, any bleeding outside her period, and whether she could be pregnant. A yes to any of these raises suspicion of ectopic pregnancy; a set of no answers does not rule it out.
  5. You findYou are ready to examine the abdomen and the patient has told you which area hurts.You doInspect first, then palpate, leaving the painful area for last. Once you find pain or another abnormality in an area, stop palpating there. Watch for guarding: arms drawn across the belly or muscles tensed before you touch.
  6. You findThe patient is nauseated and asks for water or an antacid.You doGive nothing by mouth. Let the patient take a position of comfort unless shock or an airway problem overrides it. Give oxygen if the patient is hypoxic or in distress, aiming for a saturation of at least 94%, and transport promptly.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Shock first. Altered mental status, anxiety, pale cool moist skin, a fast pulse and fast breathing show up before the blood pressure drops.
  • Ask about pregnancy outright, in private. Do not wait for the patient to raise it.
  • Palpate the painful area last, and stop once you have found tenderness. Pressing on it first can hide what the other quadrants would have told you.
  • Older patients feel pain less and can have far higher mortality from the same condition. Beta blockers can mask the fast pulse of shock, so a normal pulse may not be reassuring.
  • Nothing by mouth for any patient with abdominal pain.
  • A normal exam does not clear the patient. The absence of abnormal findings does not mean the condition is not serious.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: An older patient with mild belly pain, a soft abdomen and a normal pulse is stable and low priority.

What is actually true: Older patients perceive pain less, and many take beta blockers that hold the pulse down even in shock. Mild complaints and normal-looking numbers can hide a serious condition, so they are assessed carefully and transported.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A patient points to the right lower quadrant as the place that hurts. In what order do you palpate the abdomen?

The painful area is palpated last, because pressing on it first can mask the patient's response in the other quadrants. Palpation is still part of the exam; you simply stop pressing an area once you have found pain there.

2A 79-year-old man with abdominal pain is pale and slightly confused. His pulse is 72. He takes a beta blocker. What does the pulse tell you?

Beta blockers can mask the tachycardia that normally goes with shock, so a normal-looking pulse might actually mean shock. Pale skin and confusion are early signs of shock on their own, and a normal rate does not cancel them.

3A patient with abdominal pain and nausea asks for a few sips of water. What do you do?

A patient with abdominal pain is given nothing by mouth. Vomiting is common and can threaten the airway, and the patient may need surgery. Small sips seem harmless, which is what makes that option tempting, but the rule has no small-amount exception.

4A 24-year-old woman with lower abdominal pain says her period is not late and she is sure she is not pregnant. What does that tell you?

A yes to the menstrual and pregnancy questions raises suspicion, but a set of no answers does not rule out an ectopic pregnancy. Treating it as ruled out is the tempting mistake.

5Before you take any vital signs, which set of findings in a patient with abdominal pain points to early shock?

Early shock shows as altered mental status or anxiety, pale cool moist skin, and a rapid pulse and breathing. A rigid abdomen suggests peritonitis, which is serious, but it is not itself the sign of shock.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Assess abdominal pain in the standard order, protecting yourself from vomit and looking for early shock in the primary assessment: altered mental status, anxiety, pale cool moist skin, fast pulse and breathing. Take an OPQRST and SAMPLE history, ask women of childbearing age about their cycle and pregnancy directly and in private, and repeat vital signs every 5 minutes in an unstable patient. Inspect, then palpate the painful area last and stop once tenderness is found. Be wary in older patients, whose pain is blunted and whose beta blockers can hide the pulse of shock. Care is supportive: protect the airway with suction ready, oxygen if indicated to at least 94%, position of comfort, nothing by mouth, and prompt transport even when the exam finds little.
