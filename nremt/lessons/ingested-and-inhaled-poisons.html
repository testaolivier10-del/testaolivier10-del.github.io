Ingested, inhaled and absorbed poisons

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

On a cold morning a mother, father and two children all have headaches, dizziness and nausea. "We all caught the same flu," the father says. The furnace is running. You clip a pulse oximeter on the mother and it reads 99 percent.

A whole household sick at once, with flu symptoms and no fever, is carbon monoxide until proven otherwise, and that reassuring 99 is false. With poisons, the route in decides the care, and the obvious reading is often the wrong one.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findYou are called for a poisoning or overdose.You doGather what medical direction and poison control will ask for: exactly what the substance was (bring the container if it is safe to), when, how much, what has been done already, the patient's estimated weight, and the effects now. Poison control answers 24 hours a day at 1-800-222-1222.
  2. You findA patient has swallowed a poison and you carry activated charcoal.You doGive it only on the order of medical direction. Never give it to a patient who cannot swallow, has an altered mental status, swallowed an acid or an alkali, or is coughing violently, and never force it on a patient who refuses.
  3. You findA patient took a handful of pills hours ago and now has only mild nausea and no appetite.You doDo not be reassured. Acetaminophen looks mild early while the liver is being overwhelmed, and its antidote works best within 8 to 10 hours. Suspect acetaminophen in any overdose and get the patient to the hospital.
  4. You findSeveral people in one building have headache, dizziness and nausea.You doSuspect carbon monoxide. Get everyone out to fresh air, yourselves included, because the gas that is poisoning them is still in the building. Then give high-concentration oxygen whatever the pulse oximeter says, because CO makes the reading falsely high. Do not wait for cherry-red skin, which is not typically seen.
  5. You findA patient pulled from a fire is coughing, with soot in the nose or mouth, singed nasal hairs or a smoky smell on the breath.You doTreat it as an injured airway and a poisoning together. Give oxygen even if the SpO<sub>2</sub> reads 100 percent.
  6. You findA person is slumped in a car with the windows and doors taped shut and warning signs posted.You doDo not enter. This is the pattern of a chemical suicide (older texts say "detergent suicide"), in which a toxic gas such as hydrogen sulfide fills a small closed space. Stay back, keep bystanders away and call the hazmat team.
  7. You findA chemical is on the patient's skin or in the eyes.You doProtect yourself, then decontaminate before the patient goes in your ambulance. Brush off dry powder before flushing. Flush liquids, and anything in the eyes, with clean water for at least 20 minutes. Never try to neutralize an acid or alkali.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Four routes in: ingested, inhaled, absorbed through the skin, injected.
  • A poison is any substance that can harm the body; a toxin is a poison made by a living organism. Poisoning is most serious in patients who are already ill, the very young and older adults.
  • Activated charcoal works by adsorption: the poison sticks to its surface instead of being absorbed. It is not an antidote and does nothing about poison already in the blood. It is rarely used today.
  • True antidotes are rare. Most poisoning care is stopping further absorption and supporting the patient. Naloxone for opioids is one of the exceptions.
  • Acetaminophen timeline: mild nausea in the first hours, then right upper quadrant pain and jaundice a day or two later, when the best time for treatment has passed.
  • Carbon monoxide has no smell, color or taste. It stops red blood cells from carrying oxygen normally, and a standard pulse oximeter cannot tell it from oxygen.
  • Never neutralize. Putting vinegar or baking soda on an acid or alkali makes heat and damages the tissue further.
  • What can soak into the patient's skin can soak into yours, so decontamination may come before any contact.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The pulse oximeter reads 99 percent, so a patient with suspected carbon monoxide poisoning does not need oxygen.

What is actually true: In carbon monoxide poisoning the pulse oximeter reads falsely high, because it cannot tell hemoglobin carrying carbon monoxide from hemoglobin carrying oxygen. Give oxygen whenever CO is suspected, whatever the number says.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1Four members of a family have headache, dizziness and nausea in a house heated by an old furnace. One has an SpO2 of 99 percent. What do you do?

Several people sick together with these symptoms points to carbon monoxide, and CO makes the pulse oximeter read falsely high. Trusting the 99 percent is the trap; rechecking it gives the same false number.

2Which patient could be given activated charcoal, if medical direction orders it?

Charcoal is only for a patient who is alert and able to swallow, and only when ordered. Altered mental status and violent coughing risk aspiration, and drain cleaner is a caustic, for which charcoal is contraindicated.

3A 20-year-old took "a lot" of acetaminophen six hours ago. She feels a little nauseated but otherwise fine and wants to stay home. Why is transport still important?

Acetaminophen is deceptive: the early symptoms are mild nausea and loss of appetite while the liver is being overwhelmed. The antidote works best within 8 to 10 hours, so feeling fine now is no reason to wait.

4A worker has a liquid chemical splashed across his forearm. What is the correct care?

Liquids are irrigated with clean water for at least 20 minutes. Neutralizing sounds logical, but the reaction produces heat and makes the injury worse.

5You arrive to find a person motionless in a parked car. The windows are taped shut and a handwritten warning sign is on the glass. What do you do?

Taped windows and warning signs mean a toxic gas such as hydrogen sulfide may fill the car. Entering makes you the next patient. The scene belongs to hazmat until it is safe.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

A poison gets in by one of four routes: ingested, inhaled, absorbed or injected. For any poisoning, find out what, when, how much, what has been done, the patient's weight and the effects, and use medical direction and poison control (1-800-222-1222). Activated charcoal binds poison in the gut, is not an antidote, and is given only when ordered to a patient who is alert and can swallow, never after an acid or alkali. Acetaminophen looks mild early and damages the liver later. Carbon monoxide gives flu-like symptoms and a falsely high pulse oximeter reading, so move the patients to fresh air and give oxygen whatever the number. Stay out of hazardous atmospheres, decontaminate before transport, flush chemicals with water for at least 20 minutes, and never neutralize.
