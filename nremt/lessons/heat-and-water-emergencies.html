Heat exposure and water-related emergencies

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A roofer collapsed on a hot afternoon. His skin is hot and still wet with sweat, and he does not know where he is. A coworker says it cannot be heat stroke, because he is still sweating.

The sweat does not rule it out. Hot skin with a brain that has stopped working properly is heat stroke, and every minute he stays hot does more damage.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient in the heat has moist, pale, cool or normal skin, with cramps, weakness, dizziness and a weak pulse, and is thinking clearly.You doTreat for heat exhaustion. Move the patient to a cool place, loosen clothing, give oxygen if needed, and give small sips of water if the patient is alert.
  2. You findA patient in the heat has hot skin, dry or moist, and an altered mental status.You doTreat for heat stroke. Move the patient to a cool place and start cooling at once: cold packs to the neck, armpits and groin at the least, and faster methods such as cold water immersion up to the neck, ice or cold wet sheets and towels, or wetting and fanning where your protocol allows. Cooling starts on scene and continues during transport. Give high-concentration oxygen and transport immediately.
  3. You findA drowning patient is not breathing.You doGet air in as early as possible. Rescue breathing can start in the water if the patient can be reached safely by a trained rescuer, and rescue breathing or CPR starts as soon as the patient is on land. Restrict spinal motion only if there was a trauma mechanism, such as a dive or a strike by a boat. Do not delay ventilation for it otherwise.
  4. You findA person is struggling in the water and you are on shore.You doFollow the rescue order: Reach, Throw and Tow, Row, and only then Go. Entering the water is the last resort and is for a trained rescuer who is a strong swimmer. Untrained rescuers become patients.
  5. You findA scuba diver becomes ill after surfacing.You doSort it by timing. Altered mental status, loss of consciousness, seizures or stroke-like signs within about 15 minutes suggest an air embolism. Joint or muscle pain, headache, confusion, numbness or paralysis starting 1 to 12 hours later (up to 48) suggest decompression sickness. The field care is the same: open airway, highest-concentration oxygen by nonrebreather, keep warm, and consider transport to a hyperbaric facility.
  6. You findA climber at high altitude has a worsening headache with poor coordination or confusion, or is short of breath at rest with a cough.You doRecognize high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) or pulmonary edema (HAPE). Descent is the first priority for both, with high-concentration oxygen, and as little exertion as possible for HAPE. Mild acute mountain sickness (headache, nausea, poor sleep) is treated with rest and fluids; if severe, oxygen and descent.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Heat exhaustion: moist, pale, cool or normal skin from heavy sweating and fluid and salt loss. The patient is thinking clearly.
  • Heat stroke: hot skin plus altered mental status. Temperature control has failed. Sweating may have stopped, or may not.
  • Altered mental status is required for heat stroke. Once it appears with hot skin, the label is heat stroke and the care is cooling and transport.
  • Drowning is a breathing problem, so ventilation comes first, and CPR for a drowning patient includes breaths, not compressions alone. Cold water, below 70 degrees F, can delay death, and resuscitation is attempted even after 30 minutes or more.
  • Spinal precautions in the water are for trauma mechanisms only: diving, a boat or water ski strike. Simple immersion rarely injures the spine.
  • Air embolism comes from holding the breath on ascent and shows within minutes. Decompression sickness comes from ascending too fast and shows hours later.
  • Reach, Throw and Tow, Row, Go.
  • Altitude illness: the treatment that matters most for HACE and HAPE is going down.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If the patient is still sweating, it is heat exhaustion, not heat stroke.

What is actually true: Heat stroke skin is hot, and it can be dry or moist. What separates the two is the brain: hot skin with an altered mental status is heat stroke, even if the patient is drenched in sweat.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A runner collapses at the finish of a summer race. Her skin is hot and sweaty, and she is confused and combative. What is this, and what is the priority?

Hot skin with an altered mental status is heat stroke, whether the skin is dry or moist, and the care is cooling at once and immediate transport. Heat exhaustion is the tempting answer because she is still sweating, but a heat exhaustion patient thinks clearly, and a confused patient should not be given fluids by mouth.

2A teenager is pulled from a lake after slipping off a dock while fishing. He is not breathing. Nobody saw him dive or hit anything. What do you do first?

Drowning is a breathing problem, so ventilation starts as soon as possible. Spinal motion restriction is the tempting choice for any water accident, but simple immersion without a trauma mechanism rarely injures the spine, and ventilation must not be delayed for it.

3A scuba diver surfaces quickly, and within five minutes he is confused, then seizes. Which condition fits best?

Altered mental status, loss of consciousness, seizures or stroke-like signs within about 15 minutes of surfacing point to an air embolism. Decompression sickness is tempting because it is the better known diving illness, but it usually begins 1 to 12 hours later, with joint and muscle pain.

4From a riverbank you see a man struggling in the water about 10 feet out. A pole and a throw rope are beside you. What do you try first?

The order is Reach, Throw and Tow, Row, Go, and he is within reach of the pole. Swimming out is the tempting instinct, but entering the water is the last resort and is only for a trained strong swimmer.

5At a mountain hut, a climber's headache has worsened over a day. He now staggers when he walks and is confused. What is the most important treatment?

A worsening headache with loss of coordination and altered mental status is high-altitude cerebral edema, and immediate descent is the first priority, with high-concentration oxygen. Rest and fluids are tempting because they are the care for mild acute mountain sickness, but this has gone past mild.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Heat exhaustion is moist, pale, cool or normal skin with cramps, weakness and dizziness in a patient who thinks clearly: cool place, loosened clothing, sips of water if alert. Heat stroke is hot skin, dry or moist, with an altered mental status: cool the patient at once, give oxygen and transport immediately. In drowning, ventilation comes first, in the water if a trained rescuer can do it safely and on land without delay; spinal precautions are for trauma mechanisms only, and cold-water victims are resuscitated even after long submersion. Rescue order is Reach, Throw and Tow, Row, Go. After a dive, an air embolism shows within about 15 minutes and decompression sickness hours later; both get oxygen, warmth and consideration of a hyperbaric facility. At altitude, HACE and HAPE are treated first by descent, with oxygen.
