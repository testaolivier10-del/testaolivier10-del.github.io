Reassessment and trending

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

Ten minutes into transport, a patient who fell from a ladder says he feels "about the same." His first pulse was 96 and his blood pressure 128/80. Now the pulse is 118 and the pressure is 104/70. Neither set would alarm you by itself. Together they show a patient who is sliding toward shock.

A patient's condition is a moving target. Reassessment is how you keep watching it, and it is never skipped.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findYou have finished your assessment and started care. The patient is on your stretcher.You doRepeat the primary assessment: mental status, airway, the rate and quality of breathing, the rate and quality of the pulse, and skin color and temperature.
  2. You findSomething in the repeat primary assessment has changed, for better or worse.You doReestablish the patient's priority. A stable patient may no longer be stable, and an unstable patient may have improved.
  3. You findIt is time for another set of vital signs.You doTake them, record them with the time, and compare them with the baseline set and with every set since. That comparison is trending.
  4. You findYou have treated the patient: oxygen, bleeding control, a cervical collar, a splint.You doRepeat the pertinent parts of the history and exam and check every intervention. Is the oxygen still flowing, is the bleeding still controlled, is the collar or splint still doing its job?
  5. You findYou are deciding how often to do all of this.You doEvery 15 minutes for a stable patient, every 5 minutes for an unstable or potentially unstable patient. These are minimums. If you suspect any change, repeat at least the primary assessment right away.
  6. You findYou arrive at the hospital.You doGive the handoff report in order: name, age and gender; the subjective information (chief complaint, present illness, past history, allergies, medications); the objective information (vital signs, exam findings, scene observations); the interventions and their times; then your reassessment findings.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Reassessment has four components: repeat the primary assessment, reestablish priority, reassess and record vital signs, and repeat the pertinent history and exam while checking interventions.
  • Intervals: stable every 15 minutes, unstable or potentially unstable every 5 minutes, and immediately whenever you suspect a change.
  • Trending is tracking change over time. A pulse that rises while the blood pressure falls points to shock in a way neither number does alone, which is why a trend is worth more than any single set of vital signs.
  • FIRR sums up care after the assessment: Field impression (the body system affected and the probable cause), Interventions, Reassessments and Report.
  • Medications beyond oxygen need authorization from medical control, by standing order or direct order per local protocol. Document the time of every intervention and every reassessment.
  • Reassessment is never skipped. If lifesaving care leaves you no free hands, your partner does it.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If the first set of vital signs was normal and the patient says he feels the same, the patient is stable and there is nothing more to check.

What is actually true: One set of vital signs is a snapshot. Only repeated assessments compared against the baseline show which way the patient is heading, and a patient can worsen while still saying he feels the same.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A patient with chest pain is pale, sweaty and has a blood pressure of 92/60. How often do you reassess at a minimum?

An unstable or potentially unstable patient is reassessed at least every 5 minutes. Every 15 minutes is the interval for a stable patient, and this patient is not stable.

2Which pair of readings, taken 10 minutes apart, should worry you most?

A rising pulse with a falling blood pressure is the trend of a patient deteriorating into shock. The pair that looks similar, pulse 110 then 96 with a rising pressure, is a patient who is improving.

3What is the first component of reassessment?

Reassessment starts by repeating the primary assessment, because life threats come first. Vital signs are the third component, after you reestablish the patient's priority.

4You are reassessing a stable patient every 15 minutes. Six minutes after your last check, she suddenly becomes confused. What do you do?

The intervals are a minimum, not a schedule to wait for. Any suspected change means repeating at least the primary assessment immediately. A blood pressure alone skips the airway and breathing check that a change in mental status demands.

5In FIRR, what is the field impression?

The field impression is a working impression that names the affected body system and the probable cause. It guides your interventions and can change. It is not a final diagnosis.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Reassessment turns a snapshot into a trend. Its four components are repeating the primary assessment, reestablishing priority, reassessing and recording vital signs against the baseline, and repeating the pertinent history and exam while checking every intervention. Stable patients are reassessed at least every 15 minutes and unstable or potentially unstable patients at least every 5, and at once when anything changes. Care after the assessment follows FIRR: field impression, interventions, reassessments and a handoff report given in a set order.
