Angina, heart attack and heart failure

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A 60-year-old man felt pressure in his chest while shoveling snow. He sat down, and after a few minutes it eased. He now says he feels fine and wants to walk out to your ambulance.

It may have been angina. It may be the start of a heart attack. In the field the two can look identical, and the difference decides how much heart muscle he keeps.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient has chest discomfort that came on with exertion or stress, whether or not it has eased with rest.You doTreat it as a possible heart attack with the same ACS care. Angina typically subsides with rest within about 5 minutes, but you cannot tell angina from a heart attack in the field.
  2. You findYou suspect an acute myocardial infarction (AMI).You doConfirm the patient has a pulse and is breathing, because some present in cardiac arrest. Request ALS, keep an AED close, give aspirin and assist with nitroglycerin per protocol, and keep the patient calm.
  3. You findThe patient wants to walk to the stretcher or the ambulance.You doDo not let an AMI patient walk. Any exertion raises the heart's demand for oxygen. Bring the stretcher to the patient.
  4. You findA patient with a history of heart failure has severe shortness of breath, crackles and possibly pink, frothy sputum.You doThis is left-sided failure with pulmonary edema. Do the primary assessment first, because breathing can become inadequate. Call ALS early and apply CPAP if protocol allows. Some systems allow EMTs to give nitroglycerin for acute pulmonary edema.
  5. You findA patient has distended neck veins, a swollen abdomen and swollen feet and ankles.You doRecognize right-sided failure: blood is backing up into the body's veins. Do a detailed cardiovascular exam and keep watching the vital signs.
  6. You findA patient has sudden signs of severe internal bleeding or of a stroke, and you suspect a ruptured aneurysm.You doTreat it as life-threatening and transport rapidly. A ruptured aneurysm bleeds fast, and one that ruptures in the brain is a hemorrhagic stroke.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Coronary artery disease (CAD) is plaque narrowing or blocking the coronary arteries. Risk factors you cannot change: heredity and age. Risk factors you can: high blood pressure, obesity, inactivity, high cholesterol and smoking.
  • Angina pectoris: a narrowed artery cannot meet the extra demand of exertion or stress. It eases with rest.
  • AMI: a clot forms on roughened plaque and blocks the artery, or a piece breaks off and lodges downstream, and heart muscle starts to die.
  • Two dangers of an AMI: a dysrhythmia, the most common cause of death, and pump failure, which is rarer and can progress to cardiogenic shock.
  • Time is muscle. The sooner the patient reaches hospital treatment, the less muscle is lost, so transport is the single most important treatment.
  • Heart failure: the heart still pumps, but poorly. Left-sided failure backs up into the lungs (pulmonary edema). Right-sided failure backs up into the body (jugular vein distention, abdominal swelling, pedal edema).
  • Aneurysm: a weakened artery wall that balloons or tears. The most common sites are the aorta and the brain.
  • Children: most heart conditions are congenital and already known. Ask the caregivers what the child's baseline is and why they called today.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The chest pain went away with rest, so it was only angina and the patient can skip the hospital.

What is actually true: Relief with rest is typical of angina, but angina and a heart attack can look identical in the field. Every patient with symptoms is treated as a possible heart attack until the hospital proves otherwise.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1What is the single most important treatment an EMT provides for a suspected heart attack?

Time is muscle: the faster the patient reaches hospital intervention, the less heart muscle is permanently lost. Oxygen is given only when needed, and a long on-scene exam costs time.

2A patient has swollen ankles, a swollen abdomen and bulging neck veins, but clear lungs. Which problem fits best?

When the right side fails, pressure backs up into the vena cava and the body's veins, producing jugular vein distention, abdominal swelling and pedal edema. Left-sided failure backs up into the lungs and causes crackles and breathing trouble.

3What most commonly causes death from a heart attack?

A dysrhythmia is the most common cause of death from an MI, which is why an AED stays close. Mechanical pump failure leading to cardiogenic shock is the other danger but is rarer.

4A man with a suspected heart attack feels better after nitroglycerin and offers to walk down one flight of stairs to the ambulance. What do you do?

An AMI patient does not walk. Exertion raises the heart's demand for oxygen at the moment its supply is cut. Feeling better and a normal blood pressure do not change that.

5A patient has crackles in both lungs, pink frothy sputum and severe shortness of breath. Which statement explains the cause?

In left-sided failure, pressure backs up into the pulmonary veins and the capillaries around the alveoli leak fluid: pulmonary edema. Right-sided failure causes swelling in the body, not fluid in the lungs.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Coronary artery disease narrows the heart's arteries. Angina is discomfort on exertion that eases with rest; an AMI is a clot blocking the artery, with dysrhythmia as its most common killer. The two can look the same, so every symptomatic patient gets ACS care: confirm pulse and breathing, call ALS, keep an AED close, aspirin and nitroglycerin per protocol, no walking and prompt transport, because time is muscle. Left-sided heart failure floods the lungs and may respond to CPAP; right-sided failure swells the neck veins, abdomen and feet. A ruptured aneurysm, in the aorta or the brain, bleeds fast.
