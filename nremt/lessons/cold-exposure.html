Cold exposure: hypothermia and frostbite

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

An older man was found on his garage floor on a cold morning. He has been there all night. He is drowsy, his answers make no sense, and he is not shivering.

It is easy to read "not shivering" as "not that cold". It means the opposite. Shivering stops when the body is too cold to keep it up, and a heart that cold can be tipped into a fatal rhythm by rough handling.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient has been exposed to cold.You doClose off the routes of heat loss. Get the patient off the cold ground (conduction), out of the wind (convection), covered, including the head (radiation), and out of wet clothing (evaporation). Handle the patient gently from the start.
  2. You findYou need to judge how severe the hypothermia is.You doCheck the mental status. An alert patient who responds appropriately has mild hypothermia. A patient who is unresponsive or not responding appropriately has severe hypothermia. No shivering in a cold patient means assume severe: shivering stops below about 90 degrees F.
  3. You findThe patient is alert, responding appropriately and shivering.You doRemove wet clothing, insulate, and rewarm actively during transport with heat packs or warm air, slowly, with a layer between any heat source and the skin. Put the heat on the trunk first (the sides of the chest, the neck, the armpits and the groin), never on the limbs first. Do not let the patient walk, and do not rub or massage the arms and legs.
  4. You findThe patient is unresponsive or not responding appropriately.You doOpen the airway and give high-concentration oxygen, warmed and humidified if available. Remove wet clothing, wrap the patient in blankets to stop further heat loss, handle gently because rough handling can trigger ventricular fibrillation, give nothing by mouth, and transport immediately. Any active rewarming in the field is per local protocol, and goes to the trunk only.
  5. You findA profoundly cold patient seems to have no pulse.You doCheck carefully for breathing and a carotid pulse, for as long as your local protocol allows (many EMT texts say 30 to 60 seconds; the AHA guidelines set no fixed time), because a cold heart can beat very slowly and weakly. If there is no pulse, start CPR. Do not withhold resuscitation because the body is cold: the patient is not dead until warm and dead.
  6. You findA hand, foot, ear or nose has a local cold injury.You doFrostnip (red skin that turns white, minor damage): warm it with body heat, such as cupped hands, an armpit or warm breath. Frostbite (white, waxy skin that turns mottled and grayish blue, with swelling or blisters): cover and protect it and transport. Do not thaw it if it could refreeze. If transport will be long and there is no risk of refreezing, rewarming in clean warm water (99 to 104 degrees F) is per local protocol. Never rub it, rub snow on it, break blisters or let the patient walk on it.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Five routes of heat loss: conduction (contact; water carries heat away about 25 times faster than air), convection (moving air or water), radiation (from uncovered skin), evaporation (sweat, wet skin or clothing) and respiration (warm air breathed out).
  • Mental status is the decision point. Alert and appropriate is mild. Altered or unresponsive is severe.
  • No shivering means worse, not better.
  • Warm the trunk, not the limbs. Warming the limbs first can send cold blood back to the core and cause shock.
  • Gentle handling. A cold heart is irritable, and rough movement can cause ventricular fibrillation.
  • Who gets cold faster: patients in shock, with burns, with alcohol or drugs on board, lying on cold ground, and the very old or very young.
  • Frostbite: protect it, do not rub it, and do not thaw it if it might freeze again.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The patient has stopped shivering, so the hypothermia must be getting better.

What is actually true: Shivering is the body making heat, and it stops when the body is too cold to keep going, below about 90 degrees F. A cold patient who is not shivering is assumed to have severe hypothermia.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A hiker found in the snow is confused, slow to answer and not shivering. How do you classify her hypothermia?

The decision point is mental status: a patient who is not responding appropriately has severe hypothermia, and absent shivering points the same way. Calling it mild because the shivering stopped is the tempting mistake, since shivering stops when the body gets colder, not warmer.

2An alert, shivering patient with mild hypothermia is in the ambulance with wet clothes removed. Where do you place the heat packs?

Heat goes to the trunk first: the lateral chest, neck, armpits and groin. Warming the hands and feet is tempting because they feel coldest, but warming the limbs first can send cold blood back to the core and cause shock.

3You are moving an unresponsive patient with severe hypothermia to the stretcher. Why must the move be gentle?

A severely cold heart is irritable, and rough handling can trigger ventricular fibrillation. The other options sound plausible for a fragile patient, but the rhythm is the reason the notes and protocols stress gentle handling.

4A man's toes are white, waxy and hard after hours in wet boots. You are 15 minutes from the hospital, outdoors, and it is below freezing. What do you do for the toes?

Frostbite is covered and protected, and the patient is transported. It is not thawed where it could refreeze. Rubbing is the tempting old remedy, but rubbing or massaging frozen tissue, with hands or with snow, damages it further, and so does walking on it.

5A profoundly cold, unresponsive patient is pulled from a snowbank. A quick check finds no obvious pulse. What do you do?

A cold heart can beat slowly and weakly, so the pulse is checked carefully, for as long as local protocol allows (many texts say 30 to 60 seconds), and CPR is started if there is none. Withholding resuscitation is the tempting error: a patient is not dead until warm and dead.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

The body loses heat by conduction, convection, radiation, evaporation and respiration, and care for a cold patient starts by closing those routes: off the ground, out of the wind, dry and covered. Mental status sorts hypothermia. The alert, appropriate patient has mild hypothermia and can be rewarmed actively and slowly, heat to the trunk first and never the limbs. The patient who is altered, unresponsive or no longer shivering has severe hypothermia: airway, oxygen, insulation, gentle handling to avoid ventricular fibrillation, nothing by mouth and immediate transport, with field rewarming per local protocol. Check the pulse carefully in a profoundly cold patient, for as long as your protocol allows, and do not withhold resuscitation because of cold alone. Frostnip is warmed with body heat; frostbite is covered and protected, never rubbed, and not thawed if it could refreeze.
