Seizures, stroke and syncope

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A 66-year-old man was fine at breakfast. At 9:40 his wife noticed the right side of his face drooping and his words coming out wrong. When she calls 911 she is not sure the time matters.

It matters more than almost anything else you will write down today. The treatments that can reverse a stroke are given by the clock, and the clock starts at the last moment he was known to be well.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient is in the middle of a tonic-clonic seizure.You doPrevent injury; you are not trying to stop the seizure. Get the patient onto the floor, move objects away, loosen tight clothing, and turn the patient on the side if no spinal injury is suspected. Do not hold the patient still and never put anything in the mouth.
  2. You findThe convulsions stop. The patient is drowsy, confused and drooling.You doThis is the postictal phase. Protect the airway, suction if needed, use the recovery position if there is no spinal injury, give oxygen guided by pulse oximetry, and check for injuries from the seizure.
  3. You findThe seizure has lasted longer than 5 minutes, or a second seizure begins before the patient has fully woken up.You doThis is status epilepticus, a life-threatening emergency. Transport immediately and request an ALS intercept.
  4. You findA patient has sudden weakness on one side, a drooping face or trouble speaking.You doDo the Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale: facial smile, arm drift (eyes closed, arms out for 10 seconds) and speech. An abnormal result on any one of the three means a likely stroke.
  5. You findThe stroke scale is abnormal.You doFind out and record the exact time of onset, or when the patient was last known well. Check the blood glucose if you are allowed, because hypoglycemia mimics stroke. Reassure the patient, give oxygen only if the SpO<sub>2</sub> is below 94% or there is distress, and transport to a stroke-capable hospital.
  6. You findA patient fainted and woke up quickly once lying down.You doThis is syncope. Lay the patient flat, loosen tight clothing, give oxygen according to saturation and distress, treat injuries from the fall, and request ALS evaluation, because a cardiac cause must always be considered.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • A seizure is a sign, not a disease. It is a sudden change in sensation, behavior or movement from irregular electrical activity in the brain.
  • Three phases: tonic (rigid, up to about 30 seconds, breathing may stop), clonic (violent jerking for 1 to 2 minutes, possible cyanosis) and postictal (drowsy and confused, gradually clearing, sometimes with brief one-sided weakness).
  • Causes: in an adult with a known seizure disorder, a missed dose of medication is the most common. Others include fever in young children, hypoxia, stroke, head injury, toxins, hypoglycemia, tumor and infection.
  • Ischemic stroke (a clot or embolus blocks a brain artery) is the most common type. Hemorrhagic stroke is a ruptured vessel, often from long-standing high blood pressure or an aneurysm. Damage on one side of the brain causes weakness on the opposite side of the body.
  • A TIA causes the same signs but they clear completely within 24 hours. You cannot tell a TIA from a stroke in the field, so treat it as a stroke.
  • Stroke mimics: hypoglycemia, postictal weakness after a seizure, and infection or sepsis in older patients.
  • Time decides treatment. Clot-dissolving medication is typically limited to about 3 to 4.5 hours from onset, so the onset or last-known-well time is among the most valuable things you report.
  • Syncope has four groups of causes: cardiovascular (dysrhythmia, vasovagal), hypovolemic (dehydration, internal bleeding), structural or metabolic (stroke, seizure, hypoglycemia) and environmental or toxic (alcohol, carbon monoxide). If the patient does not recover quickly, it is unresponsiveness, not syncope.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: During a seizure you should hold the patient down and put something between the teeth so the tongue is not bitten or swallowed.

What is actually true: Never restrain a seizing patient and never put anything in the mouth. Both cause injuries and can block the airway. Protect the patient from the surroundings, turn the patient on the side, and manage the airway once the convulsions stop.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A patient has been convulsing continuously for 7 minutes. What is this, and what does it call for?

A single seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes, or two or more without full recovery between them, is status epilepticus. It is life-threatening and needs immediate transport and an ALS intercept. A typical seizure ends within a couple of minutes.

2On the Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale, a patient smiles evenly and speaks clearly, but her left arm drifts down when she holds both arms out with her eyes closed. How do you read this?

An abnormal result on any one of the three tests indicates a high chance of acute stroke. Waiting for a second finding is tempting but costs time the patient does not have.

3Which piece of information is most valuable to the hospital for a patient with a suspected stroke?

Clot-dissolving treatment depends on how long ago the symptoms began, so the onset time or the last-known-well time drives the hospital's decisions. The last meal is part of SAMPLE but does not decide stroke treatment.

4A woman's facial droop and slurred speech cleared completely before you arrived, and she now feels normal and wants to stay home. What is the right approach?

A TIA produces stroke signs that resolve, but you cannot tell a TIA from a stroke in the field, so the patient is always treated as a stroke. Resolved symptoms are tempting to dismiss.

5A man who has just had a seizure is sleepy and confused, with gurgling respirations and saliva pooling in his mouth. What do you do first?

After the convulsions end, the priority is the airway: suction secretions and use the recovery position if there is no spinal injury. Oral glucose is unsafe in a patient who cannot swallow reliably, and nothing is placed between the teeth.

6A 70-year-old faints while standing in church and wakes within a minute of being laid down. She feels fine and is embarrassed. Why should she still be evaluated by ALS?

Syncope recovers quickly, but one of its cause groups is cardiovascular, including dysrhythmias, so a cardiac cause always warrants consideration. It does not always mean stroke, and glucose is given only when indicated.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

A seizing patient is protected, not restrained, with nothing in the mouth; afterward the airway comes first. A seizure longer than 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery, is status epilepticus and needs immediate transport and ALS. For a suspected stroke, one abnormal finding on the Cincinnati scale (face, arm drift, speech) is enough: record the onset or last-known-well time, check the glucose if allowed, give oxygen only below 94% or in distress, and go to a stroke-capable hospital. A TIA is treated as a stroke. Syncope recovers quickly once the patient is flat but still deserves ALS evaluation for a cardiac cause.
