Acute coronary syndrome

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A 62-year-old woman with diabetes called because she feels exhausted, sick to her stomach and suddenly sweaty. She has no chest pain at all and thinks she is coming down with the flu.

She may be having a heart attack. Heart muscle has almost no oxygen reserve, so a blocked coronary artery starts doing damage within minutes, and the patient does not always read the textbook.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient has chest discomfort (pain, pressure, squeezing or aching), or no chest discomfort but shortness of breath, nausea, sudden sweating, fainting or unexplained fatigue.You doTreat the picture as cardiac. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) can present without chest pain, especially in older adults, women and patients with diabetes. Do not talk yourself out of it.
  2. You findYou start your assessment.You doDo the primary assessment, then OPQRST and SAMPLE, then baseline vital signs. A fist clutched to the chest (Levine's sign), sweating, pale or gray skin and a sense of impending doom all point toward ACS.
  3. You findYou are deciding on position and oxygen.You doLet the patient take a position of comfort: usually sitting up if short of breath, lying down if hypotensive. Give oxygen to reach an SpO<sub>2</sub> of 94% or higher and no higher than needed, per protocol. An alert patient in no distress with a normal saturation gets no oxygen.
  4. You findYou are trained, equipped and authorized to acquire a 12-lead ECG.You doAcquire it and transmit it. Interpreting it is outside EMT scope, but an early tracing speeds the hospital's diagnosis.
  5. You findThe patient can swallow safely and has no aspirin allergy and no recent GI bleeding.You doGive aspirin, 162 to 325 mg, chewed, per protocol. Follow your protocol for a patient who already took aspirin today or who takes a prescribed blood thinner.
  6. You findThe patient has chest discomfort, a cardiac history and his own prescribed nitroglycerin. The systolic pressure is above your protocol's threshold, he has not recently taken an erectile dysfunction medication, and medical direction authorizes it.You doAssist with one dose of nitroglycerin. It may be repeated after 5 minutes if the blood pressure remains adequate, to a maximum of 3 doses. Recheck the blood pressure before each dose.
  7. You findYou are ready to move.You doTransport promptly and drive smoothly. Sudden starts, stops and needless sirens add stress to a heart that is already struggling.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • ACS (cardiac compromise) is any situation where the blood supply to heart muscle is blocked or reduced. The oxygen-starved state is ischemia, and much of its damage can be reversed if blood flow is restored in time.
  • Chest discomfort is the most common finding, and it may spread to the jaw, the neck, either arm or the upper abdomen. It can also be absent.
  • Oxygen is not automatic. It is titrated to an SpO2 of 94% or higher. High-concentration oxygen is for respiratory failure, a low saturation or a critical appearance.
  • Aspirin is chewed, 162 to 325 mg. Not for a patient with an aspirin allergy, recent GI bleeding or a risk of aspiration.
  • Nitroglycerin is the patient's own prescription, and every condition must be met. Erectile dysfunction medications taken within the window in your protocol (often 24 to 48 hours, up to 72 in some protocols) rule it out, because the combination can cause a severe drop in blood pressure.
  • Transport without waiting on scene when there is no nitroglycerin to assist with or the blood pressure is below the protocol threshold.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: No chest pain means it is not the heart.

What is actually true: Chest discomfort can be completely absent in ACS, especially in older adults, women and patients with diabetes. Shortness of breath, nausea, sudden sweating, fainting or vague fatigue may be the only signs.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A 55-year-old man has crushing chest pain and his own nitroglycerin. His blood pressure is 138/84. He tells you he took sildenafil (Viagra) last night. What do you do about the nitroglycerin?

Nitroglycerin on top of a recent erectile dysfunction medication can cause a severe drop in blood pressure, so it is withheld. A good blood pressure right now is tempting but does not make the combination safe. Aspirin, oxygen if indicated and transport continue.

2A patient with chest pressure is alert, in no respiratory distress, with an SpO2 of 98% on room air. What is the right oxygen decision?

The target is an SpO2 of 94% or higher and no higher than necessary. An alert patient without distress and with a normal saturation does not need oxygen. High-concentration oxygen for every chest pain is the older teaching.

3Which patient should not be given aspirin for chest pain?

Recent GI bleeding is a reason to withhold aspirin, along with an allergy and a risk of aspiration. Hypertension, arm pain and recent nitroglycerin do not rule aspirin out.

4How is aspirin given to a patient with suspected ACS?

Aspirin for ACS is chewed, 162 to 325 mg, so it is absorbed quickly. Under the tongue is how nitroglycerin is given, not aspirin, and a single 81 mg tablet swallowed whole is the daily prevention dose, too small and too slow here.

5A 78-year-old woman with diabetes suddenly feels weak, nauseated and sweaty, and is mildly short of breath. She denies chest pain. How should you treat this?

Older adults, women and patients with diabetes often have ACS without chest pain. Sudden sweating, nausea, weakness and shortness of breath match the pattern, so she is treated as cardiac. Checking the glucose is reasonable, but stopping there would miss the heart.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

ACS is any reduction in blood flow to heart muscle. Chest discomfort is its most common sign but can be absent, above all in older adults, women and patients with diabetes, so a matching picture is treated as cardiac. Care is a primary assessment, a position of comfort, oxygen only as needed to reach 94%, a 12-lead if authorized, chewed aspirin, 162 to 325 mg, unless ruled out, and assisted nitroglycerin only when every condition is met, including no recent erectile dysfunction medication and an adequate blood pressure. Then prompt, smooth transport.
