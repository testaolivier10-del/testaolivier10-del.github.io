Interactive lesson · Chapter 37.3

Abuse, neglect and human trafficking

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A father says his 3-year-old pulled a pot of hot water off the stove. But the burns cover both feet evenly, like a pair of socks, with a sharp line at the ankles and no splash marks anywhere. The story and the injury do not match.

You will be one of the few outsiders ever let into this home. What you do in the next ten minutes, and what you do not say, can decide whether this child is protected.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findThe story does not fit the injury, there are injuries at different stages of healing, there is a pattern of repeat injuries, or the caregiver seems indifferent.You doSuspect abuse, at any age. Treat the patient. Do not accuse anyone at the scene: suspicion is reported through the channels your jurisdiction sets out, not argued on the doorstep.
  2. You findA child has marks shaped like an object, fractures of different ages, burns with a sharp edge (a cigarette, a glove or a stocking pattern), or injuries on both the front and the back of the body.You doRecognize these as typical of inflicted injury. An infant with a bulging fontanelle who is unconscious may have been shaken, and head injury is among the deadliest abuse injuries.
  3. You findThe caregiver will not leave you alone with the child, the story changes between tellers or retellings, or the child is afraid to say what happened.You doWhere the scene allows, speak with the caregiver and the patient separately, without judgment. Keep your face and voice neutral so nothing shows suspicion.
  4. You findAn older or dependent adult has injuries blamed on a fall, is malnourished, or has a caregiver who is unaccountably absent or gets in the way of care.You doDo not accept "a simple fall" just because a fall would explain it. Falls are common in frail older people, and that makes them an easy cover story.
  5. You findYou suspect intimate partner violence.You doAssess the patient away from the partner, and away from the children, when the scene allows. Offer advocacy and hotline information every time, even on a repeat call. A patient who refuses help is not being foolish: leaving is the most dangerous moment.
  6. You findA companion controls the encounter, answers for the patient and holds the patient's ID, and the patient seems fearful or submissive.You doConsider human trafficking. Treat the medical problem first. Do not confront the companion or voice your suspicion at the scene, and report as your protocols direct (law enforcement, the receiving hospital staff or the national hotline).
  7. You findYou are writing the report.You doRecord what you saw and what was said, in the speakers' words. Describe abuse as suspected or possible and leave the conclusions to the agencies that investigate.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • More vulnerable: patients with special needs depend on others for daily care, which raises their risk of abuse at any age.
  • Your role is limited. You are not the police, an investigator, a social worker or a judge.
  • Mandated reporting: in many states the EMT must report suspected abuse. Know your own state's rule.
  • Elder abuse is grouped as physical, psychological and financial, with neglect alongside them.
  • Trafficking is exploitation by force, fraud or coercion, and it happens in every U.S. state. National Human Trafficking Hotline: 1 (888) 373-7888.
  • Intimate partner violence: the highest risk to the victim is at the moment of leaving.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If I am sure a caregiver hurt the patient, I should confront them so they know they have been caught.

What is actually true: Confronting or accusing puts the patient, and you, at more risk and can end your access to the patient. Stay neutral, treat, then report and document your suspicion through the proper channels.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A toddler has burns on both feet with a sharp line at the ankles. The father says she spilled hot water. How do you write this up?

You record the facts you saw and the statements you heard, and report the abuse as suspected. Writing a verdict is not your role, and writing it up as an accident or leaving out the story hides the mismatch that investigators need to see.

2You suspect a caregiver has been hitting an elderly patient. What is the right way to act on scene?

The EMT makes no accusations and shows no suspicion, and speaks with patient and caregiver apart where the scene allows. Threatening the caregiver or questioning the patient in front of them raises the danger to the patient.

3A young woman has a wrist fracture. The man with her answers every question, holds her passport and will not let her speak alone. What do you do?

These are warning signs of trafficking. The medical problem is treated first. The companion is not confronted and the suspicion is not announced at the scene, because that can put her in more danger. The report goes through the channel your protocol sets, usually law enforcement or the receiving hospital.

4This is your third call to the same home for a woman injured by her partner. She again refuses transport. What should you do?

Hotline and advocacy information is offered every time, including on repeat calls. The time of leaving is the most dangerous for a victim, which is a large part of why people stay, so pressure and judgment do not help.

5An infant is unresponsive and has a bulging fontanelle. The caregiver says the baby "just went limp." There are no marks on the skin. What should you suspect?

A bulging fontanelle with unconsciousness is how shaken baby syndrome (abusive head trauma) shows itself, often with no outside marks. Dehydration makes the fontanelle sunken, not bulging.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Suspect abuse when the story does not match the injury, when injuries are of different ages or repeated, and when the caregiver is indifferent or controlling. In children look for patterned marks, sharply edged burns and fractures at different stages of healing; in older and dependent adults, do not let "a fall" end your thinking. Your role is to treat, stay neutral, interview separately when you can, and report and document the abuse as suspected, with facts and quotes. In partner violence, assess away from the partner and offer resources every time. In suspected trafficking, treat first, do not voice your suspicion on scene, and report as your protocols direct.
