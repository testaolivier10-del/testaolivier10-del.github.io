How EMS began

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

It is 1950 in a small town. A farmer is thrown from a tractor, and the vehicle that comes for him is the funeral home's hearse. The driver loads him in the back and drives fast. Nobody rides with him, nobody checks his breathing, and nobody controls his bleeding on the way.

For most of its history that was the whole job: a ride. This lesson follows how EMS went from a vehicle that carries the injured to care that starts where the patient is found, because that one idea is what every other chapter of the course builds on.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • The first EMS was about transport. The service documented in France in the 1790s carried the wounded off the battlefield to where surgeons could treat them.
  • War drove the progress. Clara Barton and the Civil War, volunteer ambulance corps by the First World War, then the major advances in field care in Korea and Vietnam.
  • Military lessons became civilian care. The advances in field care from Korea and Vietnam carried over into civilian trauma centers.
  • Civilian EMS developed more slowly. City ambulances in the early 1900s were transport only, and small communities had no service until the late 1940s, often a hearse or the fire department.
  • The defining shift is from "just transport" to care of hospital quality that begins at the scene and continues until the hospital.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: An ambulance has always been a place where treatment happens. The job of EMS is basically a fast ride with some care added on.

What is actually true: It is the other way around. For most of its history the ambulance was only a vehicle. Modern EMS is defined by care that starts at the scene and does not stop until the patient is handed over at the hospital. The ride is one part of that care.

Check yourself

1What was the main job of the earliest documented emergency medical service, in France in the 1790s?

The first documented service existed to carry the wounded off the battlefield to where they could be treated. Training soldiers in first aid is tempting to pick because it sounds like modern EMS, but care that starts at the scene as the standard came much later.

2Which wars brought the major advances in field care that carried over into civilian trauma centers?

The notes credit Korea and Vietnam with the major advances in field care, which then shaped civilian trauma centers. The First World War made volunteer ambulance corps a normal part of military medicine, but that was still mostly transport.

3In the late 1940s a small American community finally gets ambulance service. Who is most likely to be providing it?

Smaller communities had no service until after the Second World War, and it was often a funeral home's hearse or the fire department. Paramedics and trauma centers did not exist yet.

4Which statement best describes the defining shift in the history of EMS?

The shift that defines modern EMS is from "just transport" to care of hospital quality that begins at the scene and continues to the hospital. The other changes happened, but none of them is the idea the rest of the course rests on.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

EMS began as transport. The earliest documented service, in France in the 1790s, was built to carry the wounded off the battlefield. Clara Barton organized care in the Civil War and founded the American Red Cross, volunteer ambulance corps were established by the First World War, and Korea and Vietnam brought the major advances in field care that fed civilian trauma centers. Civilian ambulances stayed transport only for decades, often a hearse or the fire department in small towns. The defining shift is to care of hospital quality that begins at the scene and continues without interruption to the hospital.
