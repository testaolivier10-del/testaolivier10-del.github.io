EMS in public health and evidence-based practice

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

For years, nearly every patient with chest pain got high-flow oxygen. It felt like the safe thing to do, and everyone had always done it. Then researchers followed what actually happened to those patients, and found that oxygen a patient does not need does not help, and too much of it may make a heart attack or a stroke turn out worse.

That is evidence-based practice: care changes when the evidence says it should. This lesson covers how EMS decides what works, what EMS does for the health of a whole community, and what professional behavior looks like on a call.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA treatment has "always been done this way," and someone asks whether it actually helps.You doTreat it as a question for evidence. Evidence-based techniques are those supported by scientific evidence, not by tradition alone.
  2. You findA new practice is being considered for your service.You doFollow the process: form a hypothesis, review the literature, evaluate the evidence together with the Medical Director, and adopt the practice only if the evidence supports it.
  3. You findYou read about a new technique in an EMS magazine.You doWeigh the source. Peer-reviewed research has been vetted by other experts before it is published in a journal, which makes it more reliable. EMS magazines are informative but do not publish original, peer-reviewed research.
  4. You findOn a call you notice loose rugs and poor lighting in an older patient's home.You doThat is public health work. Identifying fall hazards is part of injury prevention, and EMS is well placed for it because it works inside patients' homes and communities.
  5. You findYou meet a patient for the first time.You doBe professional: neat uniform, introduce yourself and your crew, reassure the patient. If a partner needs correcting, do it privately and constructively, never in front of the patient.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Public health is the system that looks after the health of a whole population: clean water, vaccination, prenatal care and the like.
  • EMS contributes through: injury prevention for older adults (fall hazards, blood pressure clinics, the File of Life) and for the young (car-seat clinics, bicycle helmet programs), public vaccination programs, and disease surveillance.
  • Disease surveillance: trends in call volume are an early warning of influenza, opioid overdoses and mass-casualty events.
  • Mobile Integrated Health Care, also called community paramedicine, is the name for this expanded role.
  • Patient outcomes are the measure that matters: long-term results, not simply survival to the hospital.
  • Oxygen is the classic example. It is not a "wonder drug," and excess oxygen can worsen the outcome of a heart attack or a stroke.
  • EMS research is hard: the environment is unstable, patient encounters are brief, data collection is disjointed, and consent from critically ill patients raises ethical issues.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A treatment worked if the patient was still alive when we reached the hospital.

What is actually true: Survival to the hospital is a short-term result. Evidence-based practice judges care by patient outcomes, meaning long-term results. A treatment can look fine at the hospital door and still leave patients worse off later, which is what was found with oxygen given to patients who did not need it.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1In evidence-based practice, what is the measure that matters most when judging a treatment?

Patient outcomes mean long-term results, not simply survival to the hospital. Survival to the hospital is the tempting answer, but a patient can arrive alive and still do worse in the long run.

2Which source is the most reliable place to find evidence for a new EMS practice?

Peer-reviewed research has been vetted by other experts before publication. EMS magazines are informative, but they do not publish original, peer-reviewed research, and a partner's story is tradition, not evidence.

3An EMS agency notices a sharp rise in overdose calls in one neighborhood over a week and reports it. Which public health role is this?

Trends in call volume are an early warning of influenza, opioid overdoses and mass-casualty events, which is disease surveillance. Injury prevention is the tempting choice, but it means programs such as fall-hazard checks and car-seat clinics.

4Your partner makes a small mistake on a call that does not put the patient at risk. What is the professional way to correct it?

Correction is given privately and constructively, never in front of the patient. Criticizing a partner in front of the patient is the unprofessional behavior in the chapter's example, and saying nothing means the mistake is never addressed.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

EMS is part of public health, the system that looks after the health of a whole population. Agencies run injury prevention for older adults and young people, take part in vaccination programs, and act as an early warning through disease surveillance; the expanded role is called Mobile Integrated Health Care or community paramedicine. Evidence-based practice means using what scientific evidence supports, judged by long-term patient outcomes, adopted with the Medical Director only when the evidence supports it, and drawn from peer-reviewed research. Oxygen is the classic example: it is not a wonder drug, and too much can worsen a heart attack or stroke. Professionalism means a neat appearance, introducing yourself, reassuring the patient and correcting colleagues in private.
