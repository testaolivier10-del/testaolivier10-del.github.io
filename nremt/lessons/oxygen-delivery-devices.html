Delivery devices, flow rates and targets

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A man with a long history of COPD is short of breath. His saturation reads 84%. One crew member wants a non-rebreather at 15 L/min until the reading is 100%. Another says COPD patients should not get oxygen at all.

Both are wrong. Oxygen is a drug: it has a dose, a device that delivers it and a target. This lesson covers the four devices, the flow each one is built for, and the saturation you are aiming at.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findThe patient has mild hypoxia, or will not tolerate a mask.You doUse a nasal cannula at 1 to 6 L/min, which gives roughly 24 to 44% oxygen.
  2. You findThe patient has significant hypoxia and is breathing adequately.You doUse a non-rebreather mask at 10 to 15 L/min, roughly 80 to 90% oxygen. Inflate the reservoir bag before you put the mask on the patient.
  3. You findThe reservoir bag of the non-rebreather collapses when the patient breathes in.You doTurn the flow up. A collapsing bag means the flow is too low and the mask is not delivering what it should.
  4. You findThe patient's own breathing is inadequate or absent.You doUse a bag-valve mask with a reservoir at 15 L/min, close to 100% oxygen. Here you are moving the air, not just enriching it.
  5. You findThe patient is on oxygen and you have a pulse oximeter reading.You doTitrate to a target. For most patients that is about 94% or above. For a patient with known COPD the commonly taught target is about 88 to 92%. Local protocol sets the actual numbers.
  6. You findA patient with known COPD is hypoxic.You doGive oxygen and titrate it toward the lower target. A lower target is a reason to titrate, never a reason to withhold.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • The flow rate is part of the device. Outside its range a device either does not work or does harm.
  • Nasal cannula: below 1 L/min delivers essentially nothing; above 6 L/min dries and irritates the nose without adding much.
  • Simple face mask: 6 to 10 L/min, roughly 35 to 60%. It is for a moderate need and is less often carried.
  • Non-rebreather: below about 10 L/min it cannot keep its reservoir inflated.
  • Percentages are approximate. They depend on how much room air the patient pulls in around the device.
  • Treat hypoxia, not appearances. Reaching the target on a nasal cannula is better than overshooting it on a non-rebreather.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Every patient who looks unwell should get high-flow oxygen by non-rebreather.

What is actually true: Current practice is to treat hypoxia and titrate to a saturation target, about 94% or above for most patients and about 88 to 92% with known COPD, per local protocol. More oxygen than the patient needs is not better care.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1What is the flow range for a nasal cannula?

A nasal cannula runs at 1 to 6 L/min. Above 6 it dries the nose without adding much oxygen. The 6 to 10 range belongs to the simple face mask and 10 to 15 to the non-rebreather.

2A patient on a non-rebreather at 8 L/min has a reservoir bag that goes flat with every breath in. What do you do?

A non-rebreather needs about 10 to 15 L/min to keep its reservoir inflated. A bag that collapses on inspiration means the flow is too low. Tightening the strap does not fix a flow problem.

3A patient with known COPD is short of breath with a saturation of 84%. What is the right approach?

A hypoxic COPD patient gets oxygen, titrated to the commonly taught target of about 88 to 92% per local protocol. Withholding is the old teaching and leaves the patient hypoxic; aiming for 100% overshoots the target.

4Which device is right for a patient who is not breathing?

Only the bag-valve mask moves air for the patient. A non-rebreather at 15 L/min gives a high concentration, but only to a patient who is breathing adequately on their own.

5Why are the oxygen percentages listed for each device only approximate?

The concentration that reaches the patient depends on how much room air is pulled in around the device with each breath, so the figures are approximate.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Each device has its own flow range: nasal cannula 1 to 6 L/min (about 24 to 44%), simple face mask 6 to 10 L/min (about 35 to 60%), non-rebreather 10 to 15 L/min (about 80 to 90%) with the reservoir inflated first, and bag-valve mask with reservoir at 15 L/min (close to 100%) for inadequate breathing. Titrate to a target: about 94% or above for most patients and about 88 to 92% for known COPD, per local protocol. A lower target means titrate, never withhold.
