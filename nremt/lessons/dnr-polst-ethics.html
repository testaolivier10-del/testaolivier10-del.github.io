DNR orders, POLST and ethical dilemmas

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

An 82-year-old woman is on her living room floor, not breathing and without a pulse. Her daughter is standing over her in tears. "Please do not do anything," she says. "She has a DNR. She never wanted this. The paper is at the lawyer's office."

You have seconds to decide, and no document in front of you. This lesson covers what a DNR and a POLST are, the rule for when you have no paper, what to do when the family wants a valid DNR ignored, and how to think through ethical problems that no protocol answers.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • An advance directive is written and signed in advance of the event it governs. A DNR order is one form of it.
  • DNR orders vary. One might direct resuscitation only if the arrest is witnessed, and not if the patient is found already in arrest.
  • POLST (Physician's Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment; the national program now calls it "portable medical orders," and the name differs by state) is broader than a DNR. In some states physician assistants and advanced practice nurses can sign it, so learn the rules where you work.
  • A living will is a signed statement of preferences about long-term life support and comfort measures. It typically applies in the hospital, not to prehospital care.
  • A health care proxy is a person the patient has named to make decisions if the patient becomes unable to.
  • The decision rule: without an actual legal document in hand, provide care. It is better to be criticized for trying to save a life than for letting a patient die.
  • Ethics: patient safety, and honesty about the care actually given, come before loyalty to a colleague.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If the family tells me the patient had a DNR and did not want CPR, I should respect their wishes and stop.

What is actually true: A family member's word alone, without the legal document present, is not enough to withhold resuscitation. Start care. If a valid document is produced, it can be honored then; if you withhold care and no valid order exists, the patient has lost the only chance they had.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A man is in cardiac arrest. His son says, "Dad has a DNR, but it is in a safe at the bank." No document is at the scene. What should you do?

An oral request alone, without the legal DNR document present, is not enough to withhold resuscitation, so you provide care. Taking the son's word is tempting because he is sincere, but only the document itself counts, and waiting costs the patient his chance.

2You are shown a valid DNR order for a patient in cardiac arrest, but her husband shouts at you to "do something" and start CPR. What is the best step?

When the family wants a valid DNR ignored, contact medical direction; the physician may need to talk with the family directly. Starting CPR to calm the husband is tempting, but it overrides the patient's own legal wishes.

3How does a POLST form differ from a DNR order?

A POLST is broader than a DNR: it covers resuscitation together with other preferences such as artificial feeding and antibiotics. Naming a decision maker is the tempting mix-up, but that is a health care proxy.

4Which document typically applies to in-hospital situations and not to prehospital care?

A living will states preferences about long-term life support and comfort measures and typically applies in the hospital. A DNR order and a POLST are the documents an EMT acts on in the field.

5After a call, you realize your partner gave a patient the wrong medication and has left it out of the report. What does the ethical framework point to?

Patient safety and honesty about the care actually given come before loyalty to a colleague, so a serious medication error is reported. Staying quiet out of loyalty is the pressure the framework tells you to weigh and set aside.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

A DNR order is a legal document, a form of advance directive, saying the patient does not want resuscitative efforts; some DNR orders set conditions. A POLST is broader and covers other end-of-life treatment choices as well. A living will usually applies in the hospital, and a health care proxy is a person named to decide for the patient. The rule in the field: a family's oral request without the legal document present is not enough, so provide care. If the family wants a valid DNR ignored, contact medical direction. For ethical dilemmas with no protocol, identify the options, weigh patient welfare, safety and legal duty, and choose what best protects the patient, as when you report a colleague's serious medication error.
