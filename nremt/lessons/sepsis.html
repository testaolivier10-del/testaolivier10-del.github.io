Recognizing sepsis

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A nursing home calls for an 81-year-old who is "not herself". She has a urinary catheter. She is drowsy and slow to answer, her skin is warm, her heart rate is 112, she is breathing 26 times a minute and her temperature is 101.8°F.

None of that is dramatic, and that is the danger. Sepsis is the body's own response to an infection turning against it, and it often arrives looking like a tired old woman with a fever. The EMT who names it early, and tells the hospital, changes what happens next.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient has a known or likely infection: pneumonia, a urinary catheter, a recent operation, a pressure sore, an infected wound, or signs of meningitis.You doThink sepsis. Any infection can lead to it. The common sources are the lungs, the gastrointestinal tract, the urinary tract, the skin and soft tissue, and the central nervous system, and often no source is ever found.
  2. You findYou take vital signs.You doCheck them against the SIRS criteria you can measure in the field: a temperature above 100.4&deg;F (38&deg;C) or below 96.8&deg;F (36&deg;C), a heart rate above 90, a breathing rate above 20. Two or more, with a suspected infection, raise the likelihood of sepsis.
  3. You findThe systolic blood pressure is below 90, or the patient is newly confused or more confused than usual.You doTreat these as signs that sepsis is already affecting perfusion or the brain. They are not SIRS criteria, but many EMS sepsis screens count them. Follow your protocol.
  4. You findYou suspect sepsis.You doGive supportive care: oxygen as needed to keep the saturation at 94 percent or above, keep the body temperature normal, and call for advanced life support as appropriate.
  5. You findThe patient meets your system's sepsis criteria.You doNotify the emergency department with a sepsis alert. Earlier diagnosis means earlier treatment and better survival.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Sepsis is the response, not just the infection. The damage to tissues and organs is done by the body's own abnormal reaction to the infection.
  • Two things lower perfusion: leaky capillaries let fluid escape from the vessels, and dilated vessels keep blood from returning to the heart.
  • Septic shock is sepsis with low blood pressure that does not respond to IV fluids.
  • The fourth SIRS criterion is an abnormal white blood cell count, a lab value from the hospital. In the field you work with temperature, heart rate and breathing rate.
  • SIRS is a prompt, not a verdict. Trauma patients, for example, often meet two criteria without being septic.
  • Cold counts too. Some septic patients have a low temperature instead of a fever, so a normal or low temperature does not rule sepsis out.
  • qSOFA (altered mental status, a breathing rate of 22 or more, a systolic pressure of 100 or less) does not find sepsis; it points to which septic patients are likely to do worse.
  • No field tool is perfect, so follow your local protocol.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: No fever means no sepsis.

What is actually true: Some septic patients are hypothermic rather than febrile. A temperature below 96.8&deg;F (36&deg;C) is itself a SIRS criterion, and sepsis is never ruled out on temperature alone.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A 77-year-old with pneumonia has a temperature of 101.2&deg;F, a heart rate of 108 and a breathing rate of 24. How many of the field SIRS criteria does she meet?

Temperature above 100.4&deg;F, heart rate above 90 and breathing rate above 20 are all met. With a known infection, two or more raise the likelihood of sepsis, and she has three.

2What actually damages the organs in sepsis?

Sepsis is defined by the body's abnormal, counterproductive response to an infection, and that response does the damage. Fever is a sign of the illness, and some septic patients do not have one at all.

3A patient from a nursing home has a suspected urinary infection, a heart rate of 104, a breathing rate of 22 and a temperature of 95.9&deg;F. What should you conclude?

A temperature below 96.8&deg;F is a SIRS criterion, so this patient meets all three field criteria with a suspected infection. Ruling sepsis out for lack of fever is the classic mistake.

4For a stable patient who meets your sepsis criteria, which action is likely to help the most?

Early notification leads to earlier diagnosis and treatment and to better survival. Oxygen is given as needed to keep the saturation at 94 percent or above, not automatically at high flow.

5What is qSOFA used for?

The three qSOFA findings (altered mental status, breathing rate of 22 or more, systolic pressure of 100 or less) predict which septic patients face a worse outcome. They do not diagnose sepsis or identify the organism.

6A healthy 30-year-old with a broken femur from a fall has a heart rate of 118 and a breathing rate of 24. He has no sign of infection. What does this show about SIRS?

SIRS is a prompt, not a verdict. Trauma patients often meet two criteria from pain and blood loss, and the screen is meant for patients with a documented or suspected infection.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Sepsis is a life-threatening, abnormal response of the body to an infection, and that response damages the organs. Leaky capillaries and dilated vessels lower perfusion; when the blood pressure stays low despite fluids it is septic shock. In a patient with a suspected infection, two or more field SIRS criteria (temperature above 100.4°F or below 96.8°F, heart rate above 90, breathing rate above 20) raise the likelihood, and a systolic pressure below 90 or new confusion means it is already serious. Care is supportive: oxygen to a saturation of 94 percent or above, a normal body temperature, ALS as appropriate, and above all an early sepsis alert to the hospital.
