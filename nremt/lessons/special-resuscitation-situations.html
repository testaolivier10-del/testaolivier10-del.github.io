Special resuscitation situations

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

A man is pulled from an icy pond after 20 minutes under water. He is cold, blue, stiff with cold and has no pulse. A bystander says quietly, "He's gone."

Maybe not. Cold stretches the window of survival, and "not dead until warm and dead" is a working rule. Some arrests bend the standard approach, and you need to know which parts bend and which never do.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findA patient in cardiac arrest is severely cold.You doStart full resuscitation at once and keep it going while the patient is rewarmed: CPR, and the AED as soon as it is at hand. Whether shocks are repeated after a first unsuccessful one in a severely cold patient is set by your local protocol. Handle the patient gently, remove wet clothing, prevent further heat loss, and transport to a hospital that can rewarm actively. Do not terminate resuscitation in the field.
  2. You findA patient in arrest has been pulled from the water.You doMost drowning arrests are asphyxial, so be aggressive with the airway and ventilations along with compressions. Start CPR with breaths before the AED goes on. Then pull the patient clear of the water and quickly wipe the chest dry before attaching the pads.
  3. You findA patient goes into cardiac arrest in the back of the moving ambulance.You doStop the vehicle, then do CPR and use the AED. Motion lowers compression quality, and some AEDs cannot analyze while the vehicle is moving.
  4. You findYou see a lump under the skin of the upper chest: a pacemaker or an implanted defibrillator.You doResuscitate as usual. Place the pads a few inches away from the device, not on top of it, while staying in the normal pad area.
  5. You findAn unresponsive patient has a ventricular assist device (VAD or LVAD). You cannot feel a pulse.You doNo pulse can be normal with a VAD, so judge by level of consciousness and breathing. If the patient is unconscious with signs of poor perfusion, assume the device has failed and begin compressions per local protocol.
  6. You findParents find their infant not breathing in the crib.You doStart resuscitation unless rigor mortis or dependent lividity is present. Support the family, and never speak to them in a suspicious tone.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Hypothermic arrest: full resuscitation starts immediately and continues during rewarming, and the AED is used. Guidance on repeat shocks has changed more than once: the 2025 AHA guidelines say that if the first shock does not work, it may be reasonable to hold further shocks until the core temperature reaches 86°F (30°C), where the 2020 guidelines kept to the standard sequence. Follow your local protocol.
  • The cold patient has a longer window of survival, which is the meaning of "not dead until warm and dead." Stiffness and fixed, dilated pupils are not reliable signs of death in severe hypothermia.
  • Water and the AED: the AED is used after drowning, but not on a patient lying in water or on a wet chest. Wiping the chest is enough; you do not wait for the patient to dry.
  • Drowning is an oxygen problem first. Breaths matter here more than in a sudden collapse.
  • An implanted defibrillator that is firing means the device is detecting a dangerous rhythm: transport immediately. A patient with past bypass surgery gets the same care as anyone else.
  • SUID (sudden unexpected infant death) is the death of an infant under 1 year with no immediately obvious cause. It is an umbrella term covering SIDS, accidental suffocation or strangulation in bed, and deaths that remain undetermined.
  • SIDS is a diagnosis of exclusion made only after a full investigation, autopsy and scene review. It cannot be determined in the field.
  • It happens to well-cared-for babies. It is better to resuscitate when it was not necessary than to fail to resuscitate when it was.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: He is cold, stiff and has no pulse after that long in icy water, so he is dead and CPR is pointless.

What is actually true: Severe cold can look like death, and it also protects the brain. Unless there is an obviously lethal injury, a hypothermic patient in cardiac arrest gets full resuscitation, continued during rewarming and transport. That is what "not dead until warm and dead" means.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A patient in arrest was just pulled from a lake. He is lying at the shoreline with his legs still in the water and his chest soaking wet, and your partner has started CPR with breaths. What do you do before attaching the AED?

The AED is used for a drowning patient once CPR with breaths is under way, but not while he is lying in water or on a soaking-wet chest. Pull him clear and quickly wipe the chest so the pads stick and the current goes through the heart instead of across wet skin. Waiting for him to dry is the tempting overcorrection: wiping the chest is enough.

2A patient with an LVAD is awake, talking and comfortable. You cannot feel a pulse or get a blood pressure. What does this mean?

A VAD can move blood in a continuous flow, so a living patient may have no palpable pulse or measurable blood pressure. Level of consciousness and breathing guide you. Compressions are for the VAD patient who is unconscious with poor perfusion.

3Your patient goes into cardiac arrest while the ambulance is on the highway. What is the best action?

Motion lowers compression quality, and some AEDs cannot analyze in a moving vehicle. Stopping feels like losing time, but effective compressions and an accurate analysis matter more.

4You are placing AED pads and notice a hard lump under the skin below the right collarbone. Where does the pad go?

An implanted pacemaker or defibrillator does not stop you from defibrillating. The pad is offset a few inches from the device and kept in the general target area. A pad directly over the device can block the current and damage it.

5A 3-month-old is found not breathing in her crib. She is still warm, with no rigor mortis and no lividity. A neighbor whispers that the parents must have done something. What do you do?

Without rigor mortis or dependent lividity, you resuscitate. SUID happens to well-cared-for babies, so the family gets support, not suspicion. SIDS is a diagnosis of exclusion made after investigation and cannot be named in the field.

6A hypothermic patient in cardiac arrest has not responded after 20 minutes of CPR at the scene. Which is correct?

Resuscitation is not terminated in the field for a hypothermic patient, because the window of survival is longer in the cold. CPR continues during transport to a hospital that can rewarm actively, with the AED used as your protocol directs. Twenty minutes is tempting as a stopping point, but the usual time limits do not apply to a cold patient.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Special situations adjust the standard resuscitation without replacing it. A hypothermic patient gets full CPR and the AED per protocol, gentle handling and transport for rewarming, with no field termination. A drowning patient needs strong airway and ventilation care first, then a chest wiped dry before the AED. Arrest in a moving ambulance means stopping the vehicle. Pads go a few inches from a pacemaker or implanted defibrillator. A VAD patient may have no pulse while alive, so mental status and breathing guide you. In sudden unexpected infant death, resuscitate unless rigor mortis or lividity is present, and treat the family with compassion.
