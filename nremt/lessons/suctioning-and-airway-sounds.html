Suctioning and the sounds of an obstructed airway

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

An unresponsive patient has vomited. Each breath makes a wet, bubbling sound. Your partner tilts the head back further, and the sound does not change at all.

An airway in trouble usually tells you what is wrong by the sound it makes. Snoring, gurgling, stridor and wheezing each point to a different problem with a different answer. This lesson covers those four sounds and how to suction without doing harm.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findYou hear gurgling: fluid such as blood, vomit or secretions in the airway.You doSuction. Repositioning does nothing for fluid. Pre-oxygenate first when you can, because suction removes air along with the fluid.
  2. You findYou are getting the catheter ready.You doMeasure it from the corner of the mouth to the earlobe or the angle of the jaw. Insert it with the suction off, only to that depth and never past where you can see, then apply suction as you withdraw in a sweeping motion.
  3. You findThe suction catheter is in the patient's mouth.You doKeep each attempt short: about 15 seconds at most in an adult, and about 10 seconds in a child and about 5 seconds in an infant, with a lower suction pressure. Some texts teach 10 seconds for adults, so follow local protocol. Then ventilate, and suction again if needed.
  4. You findThe vomit is thick enough to clog even a rigid catheter.You doLog-roll the patient onto their side and let gravity clear the mouth.
  5. You findA child's heart rate drops while you are suctioning.You doStop and ventilate. Touching the back of the throat can trigger a vagal response that slows the heart, and this matters most in infants and children.
  6. You findYou hear snoring, stridor or wheezing instead of gurgling.You doSnoring is the tongue: use a manual maneuver, then an adjunct. Stridor is narrowing at or above the larynx: treat it as an emergency, transport rapidly, and treat the cause when you can (epinephrine per protocol for anaphylaxis). Wheezing is narrowed lower airways: it is treated with medication, not with an adjunct or suction.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Suction is time-limited because the patient is not breathing or being ventilated while the catheter is in. The pattern is suction, ventilate, suction again.
  • Rigid (Yankauer) catheter: thick secretions and vomit in an adult's mouth. Soft (French) catheter: the nose, a stoma, or through an adjunct.
  • Snoring is the tongue against the back of the throat. Gurgling is fluid.
  • Stridor is high-pitched and heard on breathing in. It comes from swelling (anaphylaxis, burns, infection) or a foreign body at or above the larynx.
  • Wheezing is a whistling sound, classically on breathing out, from narrowed lower airways: asthma, COPD, and the lower-airway part of anaphylaxis.
  • Effort with no air movement is a complete obstruction. Silence with effort is worse than any of the four sounds.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Keep suctioning until the airway is perfectly clear, however long that takes.

What is actually true: Every second of suction is a second without air. Suction for a short, defined time, ventilate, and go back in if you need to. A hypoxic patient gets more hypoxic during suction before getting better.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1An unresponsive patient has a wet, bubbling sound with each breath. What does the airway need?

Gurgling is fluid in the airway, and the answer is suction. A head-tilt/chin-lift is the answer to snoring, which is the tongue; it does nothing for fluid.

2When is suction applied during the procedure?

Insert with the suction off, then apply it on the way out with a sweeping motion. Suction on the way in pulls soft tissue into the tip and removes oxygen for longer.

3A patient with facial swelling after a bee sting has a high-pitched sound each time she breathes in. What does this sound tell you?

A high-pitched sound on breathing in is stridor: narrowing at or above the larynx, here from swelling. After a sting that points to anaphylaxis, which calls for epinephrine per protocol and rapid transport. Wheezing is the lower-airway sound and is classically heard on breathing out.

4You are suctioning an infant and the heart rate falls. What do you do?

Stimulating the back of the throat can cause a vagal response and slow the heart, especially in infants and children. Stop and ventilate. Finishing the attempt prolongs both the stimulation and the time without air.

5A man at a restaurant is making obvious efforts to breathe. His chest heaves, but you hear no sound and feel no air moving. What is this?

Respiratory effort with no air movement is a complete obstruction, the worst finding on the list. A partial obstruction makes noise because some air is still moving.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Suction clears blood, vomit and secretions, but it also removes air, so it is short: about 15 seconds in an adult (10 in some texts, per local protocol), about 10 seconds in a child and about 5 in an infant. Measure the catheter, insert it without suction to a depth you can see, and suction on the way out; pre-oxygenate before and ventilate after. Snoring is the tongue and needs positioning and an adjunct; gurgling is fluid and needs suction; stridor is upper-airway narrowing and needs rapid transport; wheezing is lower-airway narrowing and needs medication. Effort with no air movement is a complete obstruction.
