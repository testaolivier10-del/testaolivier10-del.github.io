Choking: foreign body airway obstruction

Part 1 of 7

Why this matters

At a restaurant, a man stands up from his table with both hands at his throat. His friend is slapping his back and shouting for help. As you reach him, he tries to cough and almost nothing comes out.

With a choking patient there is only one question: is enough air moving? The answer decides whether you stand back and coach or step in at once. The 2025 American Heart Association guidelines also changed what stepping in looks like.

Part 2 of 7

What you find, and what you do

  1. You findThe patient is coughing forcefully and can speak. You can hear air moving.You doThis is a mild obstruction. Do not intervene. Stay with the patient, encourage the coughing and watch closely. A forceful cough moves more air than any thrust you can deliver.
  2. You findThe cough is weak or absent, the patient cannot speak, and there is high-pitched stridor or no sound at all. The patient may clutch the throat or turn blue.You doThis is a severe obstruction. Act now. A weak, ineffective cough still counts as severe.
  3. You findThe patient with a severe obstruction is a responsive adult or child.You doGive 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, then 5 abdominal thrusts: a fist thumb-side in, just above the navel and below the xiphoid, each thrust inward and upward. Repeat until the object comes out or the patient becomes unresponsive.
  4. You findThe patient is in late pregnancy, or you cannot get your arms around the abdomen, as with significant obesity.You doGive 5 chest thrusts in place of the abdominal thrusts, still after 5 back blows.
  5. You findThe patient is a responsive infant under 1 year old.You doSupport the head and jaw, lay the infant face down along your forearm with the head lower than the chest, and give 5 back blows. Turn the infant over as a unit and give 5 chest thrusts on the lower half of the breastbone with the heel of one hand. Never give abdominal thrusts to an infant.
  6. You findThe patient becomes unresponsive.You doLower the patient to the ground and start CPR, beginning with chest compressions. Before each set of breaths, look in the mouth and remove an object only if you can see it. Do not do blind finger sweeps.

Part 3 of 7

Key ideas

  • Mild or severe depends on air movement, not on how distressed the patient looks.
  • One sequence for every age (2025 AHA): 5 back blows, then 5 thrusts, repeated. Adults and children get abdominal thrusts; infants get chest thrusts.
  • Back blows are new for adults and children. The older teaching was abdominal thrusts alone.
  • Infant chest thrusts use the heel of one hand, not two fingers. Abdominal thrusts are not used under 1 year because the infant liver is large, sits low and is poorly protected.
  • After the object is out, the patient still goes to the hospital: thrusts can injure organs, a fragment may remain, and aspiration can show up later as pneumonia.
  • Thrusts do nothing for a swollen airway (anaphylaxis, an inhalation burn, epiglottitis). That is oxygen, minimal agitation and rapid transport, plus epinephrine per protocol when the cause is anaphylaxis.

Part 4 of 7

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If a choking patient is still coughing at all, the obstruction is mild and you should wait.

What is actually true: Only a forceful, effective cough with speech means mild. A weak cough that moves little air is a severe obstruction, and it gets back blows and thrusts right away.

Part 5 of 7

Check yourself

1A woman is choking on a piece of steak. She is coughing hard and gasps out, "I'm okay." What do you do?

A forceful cough and the ability to speak mean a mild obstruction, and her own cough is the most effective tool available. Back blows and thrusts are for a severe obstruction; using them now could drive the object deeper.

2A 40-year-old man cannot speak or cough and is turning blue. Under the 2025 AHA guidelines, what comes first?

The 2025 sequence starts with 5 back blows at every age, followed by 5 thrusts. Abdominal thrusts alone was the older adult teaching, which is why it is the tempting answer.

3A 7-month-old has a severe obstruction. After 5 back blows, the object is still there. What do you give next?

Infants get chest thrusts, delivered with the heel of one hand under the 2025 guidelines. Two fingers is the older technique and did not reach a useful depth. Abdominal thrusts are never used on an infant because of the large, poorly protected liver.

4A choking adult goes limp and unresponsive in your arms. What do you do?

An unresponsive choking patient gets CPR, starting with compressions, which generate high airway pressures. Look in the mouth before each set of breaths and remove only what you can see. Blind finger sweeps can push the object deeper.

5You relieve a child's obstruction with back blows and abdominal thrusts. She is now breathing normally and crying. What is next?

Every patient whose obstruction is relieved is still transported. Abdominal thrusts can injure the liver, spleen or stomach, a fragment may remain, and aspiration can cause pneumonia later.

Part 6 of 7

Summary

Choking is mild if the patient can cough forcefully and speak, and severe if the cough is weak or absent and air is not moving. Mild: encourage coughing and watch. Severe, in a responsive patient of any age: 5 back blows, then 5 thrusts, repeated. The thrusts are abdominal for adults and children, chest thrusts with the heel of one hand for infants, and chest thrusts for a patient in late pregnancy or one whose abdomen you cannot reach around. If the patient becomes unresponsive, start CPR with compressions and look in the mouth before breaths, with no blind finger sweeps. Transport every patient afterward, and remember that thrusts do not help a swollen airway.
