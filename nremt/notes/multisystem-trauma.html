A patient with injuries to more than one body system is the trauma patient most likely to die, and the decisions are about time. This chapter separates multiple trauma from multisystem trauma, explains the tension between treating on scene and transporting, presents the field triage criteria used to judge severity and choose a destination, and considers special populations and the pathophysiology of internal injury.

34.1Treat vs. Transport & Field Triage

Multiple trauma vs. multisystem trauma

Multiple trauma means more than one serious injury, regardless of body system; a fractured arm and a fractured leg together are multiple trauma within a single system. Multisystem trauma means injury that affects more than one body system, and it is more dangerous because the physiologic reserve of several organ systems is compromised at the same time. The more systems involved, the less reserve the patient has to compensate for shock, and the more likely it becomes that field stabilization is impossible. For these patients, definitive care in the form of surgery is the actual treatment.

The core tension: treat vs. transport

Every decision in multisystem trauma balances the time spent treating against the time lost by delaying transport. An isolated musculoskeletal injury that cannot kill the patient in the next 10 minutes never takes precedence over a threat to the airway or breathing. When time is limited, interventions that address immediate life threats always come before interventions that only improve comfort.

The phrase "the well-packaged corpse" captures the failure to avoid. The goal is never to arrive at the hospital with a perfectly splinted, perfectly immobilized patient who died en route because too much scene time was spent achieving that perfection. For the truly critical patient, definitive care beats field perfection.

Field triage: the 2021 red and yellow criteria

The trauma destination decision follows the 2021 National Guideline for the Field Triage of Injured Patients (American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma, with NAEMSP and federal partners). It replaced the 2011 scheme, which checked physiologic, then anatomic, then mechanism criteria in four numbered steps. The 2021 guideline has two tiers. Red criteria mean a high risk of serious injury: take the patient to the highest-level trauma center available within the regional trauma system. Yellow criteria mean a moderate risk: if no red criterion is met, take the patient to a trauma center, which need not be the highest level.

Red: injury patterns. A penetrating injury to the head, neck, torso or an extremity proximal to the elbow or knee; a skull deformity or suspected skull fracture; a suspected spinal injury with new motor or sensory loss; chest wall instability, deformity or a suspected flail chest; a suspected pelvic fracture; suspected fractures of two or more proximal long bones; a crushed, degloved, mangled or pulseless extremity; an amputation proximal to the wrist or ankle; and active bleeding that needs a tourniquet or wound packing with continuous pressure.

A fall from more than 10 ft (3 m), at any age.

Yellow: EMS judgment. Consider the risk factors that make the same forces more dangerous: a low-level fall with significant head impact in a child aged 5 or under or an adult aged 65 or over; anticoagulant use; suspected child abuse; special, high-resource health care needs; pregnancy beyond 20 weeks; and burns together with trauma. Injured children should go preferentially to pediatric-capable trauma centers. A yellow criterion alone does not guarantee injury; it justifies a more cautious destination when harder evidence is absent.

Your regional protocol decides the actual destinations and how far you may travel to reach one. On the exam, red means highest-level trauma center, yellow means a trauma center, and the vital-sign thresholds above are the ones to know.

Special populations & pathophysiology of internal injuries

Age changes the thresholds. For age 65 and over, the field triage guideline uses a systolic blood pressure (BP) below 110, rather than below 90, as a red criterion, because a "normal-looking" reading of 100 mmHg may be significant relative hypotension in an older adult; a heart rate greater than the systolic pressure is a red criterion at any age from 10 up. Children present the opposite problem. Their bodies compensate more effectively and for longer, then decompensate suddenly and severely, so a "stable-looking" pediatric trauma patient can crash with little warning.

The internal injuries of multisystem trauma share a common physiology. In a tension pneumothorax, the compressed vena cava reduces venous return, which in turn reduces cardiac output, and shock follows. Cardiac tamponade produces similar findings, namely jugular venous distention (JVD), low BP and a narrowing pulse pressure, but it does so by external compression of the heart rather than collapse of a lung; breath sounds therefore remain bilateral, unlike the one-sided absence of a tension pneumothorax. A solid organ injury can be masked by the organ's capsule, which delays diagnosis, while a hollow organ injury causes diffuse pain as the spilled contents irritate the peritoneum.

Key points · 34.1
  • Multisystem trauma (injury spanning multiple body systems) is more dangerous than multiple trauma to one system.
  • The 2021 field triage guideline has two tiers: any red criterion means the highest-level trauma center; a yellow criterion without a red one means a trauma center.
  • Red vital signs: unable to follow commands (motor GCS <6), RR <10 or >29, SpO2 <90% on room air, SBP <90 (age 10–64) or <110 (65+), or heart rate greater than SBP (age 10 and over).
  • Yellow: high-risk mechanism (ejection, major intrusion, death in the compartment, fall >10 ft at any age) or EMS judgment (anticoagulants, pregnancy >20 weeks, low fall with head strike at ≤5 or ≥65).
  • Children compensate well then crash suddenly — a stable-looking pediatric trauma patient can deteriorate with little warning.
  • "The well-packaged corpse" — don't perfect a splint at the cost of transport time for a critical patient.

Key terms in this chapter

Bilateral
Affecting or involving both sides of the body.
Distention
Swelling or stretching outward, such as abdominal distention.
Extrication
The process of freeing and removing a trapped patient, such as from a wrecked vehicle.
Pediatric
Relating to infants, children, and adolescents — generally patients under 18 years of age.
Pneumothorax
A collection of air in the pleural space, causing partial or complete lung collapse.
Proximal
Closer to the point of attachment or the trunk of the body.
Sign
An objective finding that can be observed or measured by another person, such as a rash or an abnormal pulse — contrast with a symptom.
