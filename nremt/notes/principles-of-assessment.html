History taking and the physical examination are skills with a structure, and the structure is what keeps you from missing things. This chapter presents the OPQRST and SAMPLE frameworks, the three examination techniques of observing, auscultating and palpating, how to choose a body-system exam from the chief complaint, and the mental shortcuts that speed clinical decisions and the ways they mislead.

14.1History Taking & the Physical Exam

History taking

The patient history has 2 components: the history of present illness (HPI) and the past medical history (PMH). If the patient cannot communicate, gather the history from family members, bystanders, and the medications and medical devices found at the scene. Building rapport makes the history better: get down to the patient's level, show empathy, and listen carefully so that you do not re-ask questions the patient has already answered.

Open-ended questions, which cannot be answered with a simple yes or no, are the default because they draw out more detailed information. Closed-ended questions, which can be answered yes or no, are reserved for serious or urgent situations that need fast answers. Diagnosis is not always definitive; sometimes the goal is simply to establish what this patient needs rather than exactly what is wrong.

OPQRST & SAMPLE

Two mnemonics organize the history. OPQRST guides your questions about the present complaint; the table below gives the meaning of each letter with a sample question.

Table 14.1OPQRST & SAMPLE

TTimeWhen did it start? Sudden or gradual? Changed since onset?

SAMPLE covers the rest of the patient's history, as the second table shows.

Table 14.2OPQRST & SAMPLE

The medications a patient takes can reveal conditions the patient has not disclosed; a patient who denies diabetes but takes insulin is one example. A good history question tends to yield 2 more good questions.

When taking a history from a child, use simple language, include the parents or caregivers, and remember that children may feel that illness is a "punishment."

Physical examination — 3 techniques

The physical examination uses 3 techniques, which the table below describes.

Table 14.3Physical examination — 3 techniques

TechniqueDescription
ObserveLook for overall condition; compare injured vs. uninjured side
AuscultateListen (usually via stethoscope) for abnormal sounds
PalpateFeel for deformities/abnormalities

When examining an alert infant or small child, reverse the usual order and work from toe to head. Explain each step, examine the child on the parent's lap if possible, never lie about whether something will hurt, and protect the child's modesty.

Body system exams (choose by chief complaint)

Which body system you examine in detail is chosen by the chief complaint.

For a respiratory complaint, always assess first whether breathing is adequate. Then observe the motion of the chest wall, auscultate the lung sounds (wheezes indicate narrowing of the airways; rhonchi and rales indicate fluid), obtain a pulse oximetry reading, and look for edema.

The cardiovascular exam covers the skin color, temperature, and condition, the mental status, the pulse, and the blood pressure (BP), which is taken in both arms because a difference of more than 20 mmHg may indicate an aortic aneurysm. Look for jugular vein distention (JVD) and palpate the chest for tenderness.

The nervous system exam includes a stroke scale, peripheral sensation and movement (unequal findings suggest a possible spinal injury), palpation of the spine, grip and foot strength, the pupils, and the gait.

The endocrine exam looks at the mental status and the skin, since cool, moist skin suggests possible hypoglycemia. Measure the blood glucose, check for an insulin pump, and look for medical identification (ID) jewelry.

In the gastrointestinal exam, observe the patient's position, such as a fetal position or guarding. Inspect the abdomen, auscultate for bowel sounds before you palpate, and then palpate the quadrants, leaving the painful area for last.

For a suspected allergic reaction, the immune system exam inspects the point of contact, the skin for rash or hives, and the face, lips, and mouth for swelling. Listen to the patient's voice and auscultate the lungs for wheezing.

The musculoskeletal exam is mostly trauma-related. Inspect for deformity, swelling, and bruising; palpate gently for crepitation; compare the two sides for symmetry; and palpate from head to toe for multiple injuries.

Critical thinking & decision making

EMTs do diagnose; cardiac arrest is a diagnosis. The diagnoses are often general, however, such as "abdominal pain of unknown cause." A field diagnosis, also called a presumptive or working diagnosis, is based on the history, the physical exam, and the vital signs, and the differential diagnosis is the list of possible conditions that could explain the findings. Compared with traditional medicine or emergency medicine, diagnosis in EMS is urgent, focused, and limited in scope: the goal is to rule out life threats, treat according to protocol, and transport. Red flags are signs or symptoms that suggest a serious underlying condition; tearing abdominal pain that radiates to the back, for example, suggests an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA).

Diagnostic heuristics & their pitfalls

Clinicians rely on mental shortcuts, or heuristics, to reach a diagnosis quickly, and each one carries a characteristic pitfall. The table below defines the common heuristics and the error each can produce.

Table 14.4Diagnostic heuristics & their pitfalls

HeuristicDefinitionPitfall
RepresentativenessMatch signs/symptoms to a known patternAtypical presentations get missed
AvailabilityRecency bias — a recently-seen condition seems more likelyOverestimating rare-condition frequency
OverconfidenceExcess trust in own knowledgeMissed errors/limitations
Confirmation biasSeeking only supporting evidenceIgnoring contradicting data
Illusory correlationAssuming causation from coincidental associationFalse cause-effect
Anchoring & adjustmentSticking to the initial hypothesis despite new infoMissing an evolving diagnosis
Search satisfyingStopping the search once "an answer" is foundMissing secondary diagnoses

Thinking like an expert means accepting ambiguity as inherent to EMS, knowing the limits of both people and technology, building strong foundational knowledge, learning the conditions that mimic one another, not letting the search for a diagnosis delay needed treatment, and knowing the limits of your own competency.

Key points · 14.1
  • Chief complaint – symptom/concern described by the patient.
  • Closed-ended question – answerable with yes/no; open-ended question – requires more than yes/no.
  • Crepitation – grating of broken bone ends.
  • Diagnosis – a label for the patient's condition guiding care; differential diagnosis – list of potential diagnoses.
  • HPI – history of present illness; PMH – past medical history.
  • JVD – jugular vein distention.
  • OPQRST – onset, provocation, quality, region/radiation, severity, time.
  • SAMPLE – signs/symptoms, allergies, medications, pertinent history, last oral intake, events.

Key terms in this chapter

Distention
Swelling or stretching outward, such as abdominal distention.
Edema
Swelling caused by excess fluid trapped in body tissues.
Gait
A person's manner or pattern of walking.
Guarding
Involuntary muscle tensing over a painful area, often the abdomen, to protect it from further pain.
Palpation
Examining by touch, feeling for abnormalities like tenderness, masses, or crepitus.
Peripheral
Relating to the outer parts of the body, away from the center, such as the peripheral pulses in the wrists and feet.
Rhonchi
Coarse, low-pitched, rattling lung sounds, often caused by secretions in the larger airways.
Sign
An objective finding that can be observed or measured by another person, such as a rash or an abnormal pulse — contrast with a symptom.
Symptom
A subjective sensation reported by the patient, such as pain or nausea, that can't be directly observed by someone else — contrast with a sign.
Wheezing
A high-pitched, whistling lung sound produced by air moving through narrowed airways, typical of asthma or COPD.
