Oxygen is a drug, delivered by equipment that has to match the patient's need and be handled with respect for the pressure it holds. This chapter covers the decision that comes first — whether this patient needs oxygen or needs someone to breathe for them — then fits the delivery device to the patient, uses the Venturi mask and the COPD patient as the case that proves more oxygen is not always better, and finishes with the cylinders, regulators, flowmeters, pin-index system and fire hazards that make oxygen equipment safe or dangerous.

10.1Oxygen or Ventilation? Making the Call

Airway, Respiration & Ventilation domain

Oxygen and ventilation solve different problems, and giving the wrong one is a recognizable exam trap

Supplemental oxygen enriches the air a patient is moving. It assumes the patient is moving air. Ventilation — a bag-valve mask — moves air for a patient who is not moving enough of it themselves. A patient breathing 6 times a minute with shallow chest movement does not have an oxygen-concentration problem; they have a volume problem, and a non-rebreather at 15 L/min enriches a tidal volume that is too small to matter. The reverse error is rarer but real: bagging a patient who is breathing adequately and merely hypoxic adds nothing and risks gastric distention.

So the question is not "how sick does this patient look" but "is air actually moving in adequate volume at an adequate rate?" If yes, and the patient is hypoxic, give oxygen. If no, ventilate — and give oxygen through the bag while you do.

The findings that say ventilation, not a mask

Inadequate breathing has a consistent picture, and any of it should push you toward the bag: a rate that is too slow or too fast to move useful volume, chest rise that is shallow or absent, breath sounds that are diminished or absent, cyanosis, altered mental status, an inability to speak more than a word or two at a time, and in a child, the specific signs that the work of breathing has become unsustainable — nasal flaring, retractions between and below the ribs, grunting, and head bobbing.

One pattern deserves naming because it is regularly misread. A patient in severe respiratory distress who suddenly becomes quiet and sleepy is usually not improving. Falling respiratory effort and a falling level of consciousness in a patient who was working hard a minute ago are signs of exhaustion and rising carbon dioxide, and that patient is about to need ventilation rather than reassurance.

When you assist ventilations in a patient who still has some respiratory drive of their own, time your squeezes to the beginning of their own inspiratory effort. Bagging against a patient who is trying to exhale forces air into the stomach and fights the patient rather than helping them.

Key points · 10.1
  • Supplemental oxygen enriches the air a patient moves; ventilation moves air for a patient who cannot move enough of it.
  • A slow, shallow breather has a volume problem — a non-rebreather enriches a tidal volume too small to help. Ventilate.
  • Signs pointing to ventilation: rate too slow or too fast, shallow or absent chest rise, diminished breath sounds, cyanosis, altered mental status, one- or two-word sentences.
  • Pediatric signs of unsustainable work of breathing: nasal flaring, retractions, grunting, head bobbing.
  • A patient in severe distress who goes quiet and sleepy is tiring, not improving. Rising CO2 and falling effort mean ventilation is next.
  • When assisting a patient with some drive of their own, time squeezes to the start of their own inspiration — bagging against exhalation inflates the stomach.

10.2Delivery Devices, Flow Rates & Targets

Airway, Respiration & Ventilation domain

Four devices, and the flow rate is part of the device

Each delivery device has a flow range it is designed for, and running it outside that range either fails to work or actively harms. The numbers below are the commonly taught EMT figures; the delivered concentration is always approximate, because it depends on how much room air the patient entrains around the device with each breath.

Table 10.1Four devices, and the flow rate is part of the device

DeviceFlowApprox. O₂Use it for
Nasal cannula1–6 L/min~24–44%Mild hypoxia; a patient who will not tolerate a mask
Simple face mask6–10 L/min~35–60%Moderate need; less often carried
Non-rebreather mask10–15 L/min~80–90%Significant hypoxia in a patient breathing adequately
Bag-valve mask with reservoir15 L/min~100%Inadequate breathing or apnea — you are moving the air

Two floor values are worth holding onto. A nasal cannula below 1 L/min delivers essentially nothing, and above 6 L/min it dries and irritates the nasal mucosa without meaningfully raising the delivered concentration. A non-rebreather below about 10 L/min cannot keep its reservoir bag inflated, and a non-rebreather whose bag collapses on inspiration is not delivering what the label claims — inflate the reservoir before you put the mask on the patient, and turn the flow up if the bag deflates as they breathe.

Titrate to a target, and know the one population with a lower target

The modern practice is to treat hypoxia rather than to reflexively apply high-flow oxygen to everyone who looks unwell. For most patients the target saturation is roughly 94% or above, and reaching it on a nasal cannula is a better outcome than overshooting it on a non-rebreather. For a patient with known COPD, the commonly taught target is lower, around 88–92%, which is closer to their own chronic baseline.

The important qualifier, repeated because it is the thing candidates get wrong in both directions: a lower target is a reason to titrate, never a reason to withhold. A hypoxic COPD patient gets oxygen. Local protocol sets the actual numbers.

Key points · 10.2
  • Nasal cannula: 1–6 L/min, ~24–44%. Below 1 L/min does nothing; above 6 L/min dries the mucosa without adding much.
  • Simple face mask: 6–10 L/min, ~35–60%.
  • Non-rebreather: 10–15 L/min, ~80–90%. Inflate the reservoir before applying; a bag that collapses on inspiration means the flow is too low.
  • BVM with reservoir at 15 L/min: ~100% — and it is for patients whose own breathing is inadequate.
  • Delivered concentration is always approximate; it depends on room air entrained around the device.
  • General target: SpO₂ about 94% or above. Known COPD: about 88–92%. Local protocol governs.
  • A lower target means titrate, never withhold. A hypoxic COPD patient gets oxygen.

10.3Oxygen Delivery & Equipment Safety

Airway, Respiration & Ventilation domain

Oxygen delivery isn't one-size-fits-all — the device has to match the patient's actual need

A Venturi mask is a specific tool for a specific problem. It delivers a precisely controlled percentage of oxygen, rather than the more variable concentration of a standard mask. That precision is useful in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), where the goal is enough oxygen and not more. A small number of COPD patients retain carbon dioxide (CO2) chronically and depend more on low oxygen levels than on rising CO2 as their trigger to breathe, and in those patients a large excess of oxygen can worsen ventilation. That is a reason to titrate, not a reason to withhold. The clinical rule has changed and the exam follows it: a hypoxic COPD patient gets oxygen. Aim for a saturation near the patient's own baseline, commonly the low 90s rather than 100%, and watch mental status and respiratory effort as you go. Leaving a hypoxic patient hypoxic is the larger and more immediate danger, and the old teaching that COPD patients should be denied oxygen out of fear of removing their drive to breathe is no longer taught.

Oxygen equipment has safety features that exist because oxygen systems are genuinely dangerous if mishandled

Oxygen systems are genuinely dangerous if they are mishandled, and the safety features of the equipment exist for that reason. The pin-index safety system on oxygen cylinders exists specifically to prevent the wrong regulator from being connected to the wrong gas cylinder, a real hazard given how many medical gases look similar but behave very differently under pressure. Two pieces of equipment are easily confused. The regulator steps the cylinder's very high internal pressure down to a usable level, while the flowmeter is what actually controls and displays how many liters per minute are being delivered to the patient. One small but real hazard is also worth knowing: adhesive tape or petroleum-based products near oxygen equipment pose a genuine fire risk, because oxygen dramatically accelerates combustion.

Cylinder pressure, safe residual, and not running out on the way in

A full portable oxygen cylinder reads roughly 2,000 psi. The number that matters operationally is the safe residual pressure of about 200 psi: at or below that, the cylinder is treated as empty and changed out, because the last of the pressure does not deliver a reliable flow and because a cylinder emptied completely can draw in contaminants. A gauge reading 300 psi at the start of a long transport is a problem to solve at the scene, not in the back of a moving ambulance.

Handling rules follow from the pressure involved. Secure cylinders so they cannot fall — a cylinder whose valve stem snaps off becomes a projectile driven by 2,000 psi. Open the valve fully, then back it off slightly. Keep the cylinder away from heat, and keep oil, grease, and petroleum products away from every fitting.

Key points · 10.3
  • Venturi mask delivers a precisely controlled oxygen percentage, unlike the more variable concentration of a standard mask.
  • A hypoxic COPD patient still gets oxygen — titrate toward their own baseline (often the low 90s) rather than withholding it. Never deny oxygen to a hypoxic patient.
  • Pin-index safety system prevents the wrong regulator from connecting to the wrong gas cylinder.
  • Regulator: steps the cylinder's high internal pressure down to a usable level.
  • Flowmeter: controls and displays liters per minute delivered to the patient.
  • Adhesive tape and petroleum-based products near oxygen equipment are a fire risk — oxygen dramatically accelerates combustion.
  • A full portable cylinder reads about 2,000 psi; safe residual pressure is about 200 psi, at which point it is treated as empty and swapped.
  • Secure cylinders against falling — a snapped valve stem at 2,000 psi makes the cylinder a projectile. Keep oil and grease away from all fittings.

Key terms in this chapter

Apnea
The absence of breathing.
Chronic
Persisting over a long period of time — contrast with acute.
Cyanosis
A bluish discoloration of the skin or mucous membranes, typically indicating inadequate oxygenation.
Distention
Swelling or stretching outward, such as abdominal distention.
Hypoxia
A deficiency of oxygen reaching the body's tissues.
Nasal flaring
Widening of the nostrils with each breath, a sign of respiratory distress, especially in children.
Pediatric
Relating to infants, children, and adolescents — generally patients under 18 years of age.
Sign
An objective finding that can be observed or measured by another person, such as a rash or an abnormal pulse — contrast with a symptom.
