Sine and cosine are functions: put in an angle, get out a number. This page draws their graphs straight from the unit circle, reads off their key features, and describes how they change, using the same rate of change and concavity language you used for polynomials and exponentials.

Tracing sine from the circle

Let a point travel counterclockwise around the unit circle, starting at (1,0)(1, 0). After it has turned through θ\theta radians, its height is sin⁡θ\sin\theta. Plot that height against θ\theta:

  • from θ=0\theta = 0 to π2\frac{\pi}{2} the point climbs from height 00 to the top, height 11;
  • from π2\frac{\pi}{2} to π\pi it comes back down to 00;
  • from π\pi to 3π2\frac{3\pi}{2} it drops to the bottom, −1-1;
  • from 3π2\frac{3\pi}{2} to 2π2\pi it rises back to 00, where it started.

After 2π2\pi the point repeats its trip, so the graph repeats: y=sin⁡θy = \sin\theta has period 2π2\pi. Turning backwards (negative θ\theta) continues the same wave to the left. The height never goes above 11 or below −1-1, so the range is [−1,1][-1, 1].

Cosine: the horizontal position

cos⁡θ\cos\theta is the point's xx-coordinate. It starts at 11, falls to 00 at π2\frac{\pi}{2}, reaches −1-1 at π\pi, returns to 00 at 3π2\frac{3\pi}{2} and to 11 at 2π2\pi. The shape is exactly the sine wave, moved π2\frac{\pi}{2} to the left: cosine reaches each value a quarter turn earlier than sine does. In symbols, cos⁡θ=sin⁡(θ+π2)\cos\theta = \sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right).

How fast sine changes

Think about the point on the circle. Near the right side of the circle (angles near 00) it moves almost straight up, so its height changes fast. Near the top it moves almost sideways, so its height barely changes. The graph shows the same thing: steep at the zeros, flat at the highest and lowest points.

Worked example 1. Use exact values to compare the average rates of change of sin⁡θ\sin\theta on [0,π6]\left[0, \frac{\pi}{6}\right], [π6,π3]\left[\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{3}\right] and [π3,π2]\left[\frac{\pi}{3}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right], and describe the graph on [0,π2]\left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right].

Each interval has length π6\frac{\pi}{6}. The average rate of change on [0,π6]\left[0, \frac{\pi}{6}\right] is 1/2−0π/6\frac{1/2 - 0}{\pi/6}=3π= \frac{3}{\pi}≈0.955\approx 0.955, the slope of the secant line through (0,0)(0, 0) and (π6,12)\left(\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{1}{2}\right).

On [π6,π3]\left[\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{3}\right]: 3/2−1/2π/6\frac{\sqrt{3}/2 - 1/2}{\pi/6}=3(3−1)π= \frac{3(\sqrt{3} - 1)}{\pi}≈0.699\approx 0.699. On [π3,π2]\left[\frac{\pi}{3}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]: 1−3/2π/6\frac{1 - \sqrt{3}/2}{\pi/6}=3(2−3)π= \frac{3(2 - \sqrt{3})}{\pi}≈0.256\approx 0.256.

All three are positive, so sine is increasing. Over equal intervals they get smaller, so the rate of change of sine is decreasing: the graph is concave down on [0,π2]\left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right].

The pattern holds everywhere. Sine is concave down where it is above the axis, on (0,π)(0, \pi), and concave up where it is below, on (π,2π)(\pi, 2\pi). The concavity changes at the zeros, where the graph is steepest. Cosine behaves the same way: concave down on (−π2,π2)\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right), where cos⁡θ>0\cos\theta > 0, and concave up on (π2,3π2)\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right).

Worked example 2. Describe how y=cos⁡θy = \cos\theta changes on (π,3π2)\left(\pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right).

The values go from cos⁡π=−1\cos\pi = -1 up to cos⁡3π2=0\cos\frac{3\pi}{2} = 0, so cosine is increasing. The graph is below the axis, so it is concave up: the rate of change of cosine is increasing too, from 00 at the lowest point toward its steepest at the zero 3π2\frac{3\pi}{2}.

Notice the two separate statements: one about how the function changes (increasing), one about how its rate of change changes (increasing). Keep them apart; they will be asked for separately when you model periodic situations.

Reading values from the graphs

Because the graphs come from the circle, every exact value you know is a point on them: (π6,12)\left(\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{1}{2}\right) on the sine curve, (2π3,−12)\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}, -\frac{1}{2}\right) on the cosine curve. The symmetry of the circle shows up too. Sine takes the same value at θ\theta and π−θ\pi - \theta, so its graph on [0,π][0, \pi] is symmetric about θ=π2\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}.
