The exam expects exact values of sine and cosine at the angles you meet most often, with no calculator. This page shows how to get each one in a few seconds from two triangles and the signs of the quadrants, and how to use them on circles of any size.

The first quadrant: three triangles of hypotenuse 1

Put the angle π6\frac{\pi}{6} (30∘30^\circ) on the unit circle and drop a segment from the point to the xx-axis. You get a 30-60-90 triangle with hypotenuse 1. Its sides are 1:3:21 : \sqrt{3} : 2 divided by 2: the height (across from 30∘30^\circ) is 12\frac{1}{2} and the base is 32\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}. So the point is (32,12)\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right).

At π3\frac{\pi}{3} (60∘60^\circ) the same triangle stands on its other leg, and the coordinates swap: (12,32)\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right). At π4\frac{\pi}{4} the 45-45-90 triangle with hypotenuse 1 has equal legs 12=22\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.

A quick check: the ray at π6\frac{\pi}{6} is close to the xx-axis, so the point is far to the right and not very high. The bigger value, 32≈0.87\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.87, is the xx-coordinate.

Every other quadrant: reference angles and signs

These are the special angles: every multiple of π6\frac{\pi}{6} and π4\frac{\pi}{4}. Each one makes the same triangle with the xx-axis as one of the three first-quadrant angles. The acute angle between the terminal ray and the xx-axis is the reference angle. The point has the same coordinates as at the reference angle, up to sign, and the quadrant gives the signs.

Worked example 1. Find cos⁡4π3\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} and sin⁡4π3\sin\frac{4\pi}{3}.

4π3\frac{4\pi}{3} is between π\pi and 3π2\frac{3\pi}{2}: Quadrant III. Its reference angle is 4π3−π=π3\frac{4\pi}{3} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{3}, where the point is (12,32)\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right).

In Quadrant III both coordinates are negative: cos⁡4π3=−12\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2} and sin⁡4π3=−32\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.

Worked example 2. Find tan⁡3π4\tan\frac{3\pi}{4}.

Quadrant II, reference angle π4\frac{\pi}{4}: the point is (−22,22)\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right). Then tan⁡3π4\tan\frac{3\pi}{4}=2/2−2/2= \frac{\sqrt{2}/2}{-\sqrt{2}/2}=−1= -1.

Angles outside [0,2π)[0, 2\pi) first move by whole turns: 11π4=2π+3π4\frac{11\pi}{4} = 2\pi + \frac{3\pi}{4} has the same terminal ray as 3π4\frac{3\pi}{4}, so the same values.

Circles of any radius

On a circle of radius rr centered at the origin, the point at angle θ\theta is the unit-circle point scaled by rr:

(x,y)(x, y)=(rcos⁡θ,rsin⁡θ)= (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta).

Worked example 3. A robot arm 2 meters long pivots at the origin. Its tip starts on the positive xx-axis and turns counterclockwise through 7π6\frac{7\pi}{6}. Where is the tip?

Quadrant III, reference angle π6\frac{\pi}{6}: cos⁡7π6=−32\cos\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} and sin⁡7π6=−12\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}.

Scale by 2: the tip is at (2⋅(−32),2⋅(−12))\left(2 \cdot \left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right), 2 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)=(−3,−1)= (-\sqrt{3}, -1).

These exact values are the raw material for the graphs in the next topic and for every no-calculator model in the rest of the unit, so practice until they come without thinking.
