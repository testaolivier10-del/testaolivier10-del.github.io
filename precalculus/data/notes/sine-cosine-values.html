The exam expects exact values of sine and cosine at the angles you meet most often, with no calculator. This page shows how to get each one in a few seconds from two triangles and the signs of the quadrants, and how to use them on circles of any size.

The first quadrant: three triangles of hypotenuse 1

Put the angle $\frac{\pi}{6}$ ($30^\circ$) on the unit circle and drop a segment from the point to the $x$-axis. You get a 30-60-90 triangle with hypotenuse 1. Its sides are $1 : \sqrt{3} : 2$ divided by 2: the height (across from $30^\circ$) is $\frac{1}{2}$ and the base is $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. So the point is $\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$.

At $\frac{\pi}{3}$ ($60^\circ$) the same triangle stands on its other leg, and the coordinates swap: $\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$. At $\frac{\pi}{4}$ the 45-45-90 triangle with hypotenuse 1 has equal legs $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

$\theta$$0$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$\frac{\pi}{4}$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$\frac{\pi}{2}$
$\cos\theta$$1$$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$\frac{1}{2}$$0$
$\sin\theta$$0$$\frac{1}{2}$$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$1$

A quick check: the ray at $\frac{\pi}{6}$ is close to the $x$-axis, so the point is far to the right and not very high. The bigger value, $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.87$, is the $x$-coordinate.

Every other quadrant: reference angles and signs

These are the special angles: every multiple of $\frac{\pi}{6}$ and $\frac{\pi}{4}$. Each one makes the same triangle with the $x$-axis as one of the three first-quadrant angles. The acute angle between the terminal ray and the $x$-axis is the reference angle. The point has the same coordinates as at the reference angle, up to sign, and the quadrant gives the signs.

Worked example 1. Find $\cos\frac{4\pi}{3}$ and $\sin\frac{4\pi}{3}$.

$\frac{4\pi}{3}$ is between $\pi$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$: Quadrant III. Its reference angle is $\frac{4\pi}{3} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{3}$, where the point is $\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$.

In Quadrant III both coordinates are negative: $\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

Worked example 2. Find $\tan\frac{3\pi}{4}$.

Quadrant II, reference angle $\frac{\pi}{4}$: the point is $\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)$. Then $\tan\frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}/2}{-\sqrt{2}/2} = -1$.

Angles outside $[0, 2\pi)$ first move by whole turns: $\frac{11\pi}{4} = 2\pi + \frac{3\pi}{4}$ has the same terminal ray as $\frac{3\pi}{4}$, so the same values.

Circles of any radius

On a circle of radius $r$ centered at the origin, the point at angle $\theta$ is the unit-circle point scaled by $r$:

$(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$.

Worked example 3. A robot arm 2 meters long pivots at the origin. Its tip starts on the positive $x$-axis and turns counterclockwise through $\frac{7\pi}{6}$. Where is the tip?

Quadrant III, reference angle $\frac{\pi}{6}$: $\cos\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}$.

Scale by 2: the tip is at $\left(2 \cdot \left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right), 2 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right) = (-\sqrt{3}, -1)$.

These exact values are the raw material for the graphs in the next topic and for every no-calculator model in the rest of the unit, so practice until they come without thinking.
