Many processes happen in stages. A store takes a discount and then adds tax. A thermostat reads a temperature in Celsius and a display converts it to Fahrenheit. Each stage is a function, and running one stage after another is called composition. This page shows how to evaluate a composite from a table, a graph or a formula, why order matters, which inputs a composite can take, and how to split a function into stages.

What a composite function does

The composite function $f(g(x))$ takes an input $x$, applies $g$, and then uses that output as the input of $f$. It is also written $(f \circ g)(x)$, read "f of g of x". The function written closest to $x$ acts first. Think of an assembly line: $g$ is the first machine and $f$ is the second.

Because the output of $g$ becomes an input of $f$, the work always goes from the inside out. On the exam, show the inner value: graders look for it.

Worked example 1 (from a table). A table gives $g(2) = 0$ and $f(0) = 4$. Find $f(g(2))$.

Inner value first: $g(2) = 0$.

Then the outer function at that value: $f(g(2)) = f(0) = 4$.

Order matters

Back at the store, take $d(p) = p - 15$ (the discount) and $t(p) = 1.08p$ (the tax). Discount first gives $t(d(p)) = 1.08(p - 15) = 1.08p - 16.2$. Tax first gives $d(t(p)) = 1.08p - 15$. They differ by $1.2$ dollars, whatever the price. Composition is not commutative: $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$ are usually different functions. There are exceptions, but never assume one.

Composites from formulas

To write $f(g(x))$ as a formula, replace every $x$ in the formula of $f$ by the whole expression $g(x)$, in parentheses.

Worked example 2. Let $f(x) = x^2 + 3$ and $g(x) = 2x - 1$. Find $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$.

$f(g(x)) = (2x - 1)^2 + 3$. The parentheses matter: the whole of $2x - 1$ is squared.

$g(f(x)) = 2(x^2 + 3) - 1 = 2x^2 + 5$.

At $x = 1$: $f(g(1)) = 1 + 3 = 4$, but $g(f(1)) = 2 \cdot 4 - 1 = 7$. Different functions.

Composition also explains transformations. With $g(x) = x - 2$, $f(g(x)) = f(x - 2)$ is the graph of $f$ moved $2$ units right. With $f(x) = 5 \cdot 2^x$ and $g(x) = x + 3$, $f(g(x)) = 5 \cdot 2^{x + 3} = 40 \cdot 2^x$: for an exponential function, a horizontal shift is the same as a vertical dilation.

Composites from graphs

A graph works like a table: read the inner function at $x$, then read the outer function at that output. To solve an equation such as $g(f(x)) = 2$, work from the outside in: first find every input of $g$ that gives $2$, then every $x$ with $f(x)$ equal to one of those inputs. Several inputs can share an output, so check for more than one.

Worked example 3. The parabola $g$ reaches $2$ at the inputs $-1$ and $3$. The line $f$ has $f(0) = -1$ and $f(2) = 3$. Solve $g(f(x)) = 2$.

Outer step: $g(u) = 2$ for $u = -1$ or $u = 3$.

Inner step: $f(x) = -1$ gives $x = 0$; $f(x) = 3$ gives $x = 2$.

So $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. Stopping at $-1$ and $3$ gives the values of $f(x)$, not of $x$.

The domain of a composite

An input $x$ belongs to the domain of $f(g(x))$ when two things hold: $x$ is in the domain of $g$, and the output $g(x)$ is in the domain of $f$.

Worked example 4. Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ and $g(x) = 6 - 2x$. Find the domain of $f(g(x)) = \sqrt{6 - 2x}$.

$f$ needs a non-negative input, so $6 - 2x \ge 0$.

Then $-2x \ge -6$, and dividing by $-2$ flips the inequality: $x \le 3$.

The identity function

The identity function $I(x) = x$ returns every input unchanged. Composing with it does nothing: $f(I(x)) = f(x)$ and $I(f(x)) = f(x)$. It plays the role for composition that $0$ plays for addition and $1$ plays for multiplication. You will need it in the next topic.

Taking a function apart

Reading a formula as stages helps you evaluate it, graph it and later undo it. Ask what is done to $x$ first (the inner function) and what is done to that result (the outer function).

FunctionInner $g(x)$Outer $f(x)$
$h(x) = (3x + 1)^4$$3x + 1$$x^4$
$h(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 9}$$x^2 + 9$$\sqrt{x}$
$h(x) = 2^{x - 5}$$x - 5$$2^x$

A decomposition is not unique, but the order of the stages is fixed by the formula. Check a pair by composing it: $f(g(x))$ must give back $h(x)$.
