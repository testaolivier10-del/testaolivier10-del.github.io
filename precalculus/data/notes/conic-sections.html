A double cone, two ice-cream cones point to point, can be sliced by a plane in different ways. A cut straight across gives a circle; a tilted cut gives an ellipse; a cut parallel to the side gives a parabola; a steep cut through both cones gives the two branches of a hyperbola. These curves are the conic sections. Each is an implicitly defined curve, with an equation in $x$ and $y$.

Parabolas

You know $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$, a parabola with vertex $(h, k)$ that opens up if $a > 0$ and down if $a < 0$. Swap the roles of $x$ and $y$ and you get a parabola that opens sideways:

$$x - h = a(y - k)^2.$$

It has vertex $(h, k)$ and opens right if $a > 0$, left if $a < 0$. A sideways parabola is not the graph of a function of $x$, since most $x$-values have two $y$-values.

Ellipses

An ellipse with center $(h, k)$ has the standard form

$$\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1, \qquad a > 0, \; b > 0.$$

It reaches $a$ units left and right of the center and $b$ units up and down. The farthest points along the longer direction are the vertices; the longer axis has length $2a$ or $2b$, whichever is larger. When $a = b$ the ellipse is a circle of radius $a$.

Worked example 1. Describe $\frac{(x - 1)^2}{16} + \frac{(y + 2)^2}{4} = 1$.

Center: $x - 1$ and $y + 2 = y - (-2)$ give $(1, -2)$.

Distances: $a^2 = 16$ so $a = 4$ across; $b^2 = 4$ so $b = 2$ up and down. The ellipse is wider than tall.

Vertices: $(1 \pm 4, -2)$, that is $(5, -2)$ and $(-3, -2)$; the long axis has length $8$. The top and bottom points are $(1, 0)$ and $(1, -4)$.

Hyperbolas

Change the plus sign to a minus and the curve breaks into two branches:

$$\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1.$$

This hyperbola opens left and right. Its vertices are $(h \pm a, k)$, and far from the center each branch gets closer and closer to the asymptotes $y - k = \pm\frac{b}{a}(x - h)$. If the $y$ term comes first, $\frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} - \frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} = 1$, it opens up and down with vertices $(h, k \pm b)$ and the same asymptotes.

Worked example 2. Describe $\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$.

Center $(0, 0)$, $a = 2$, $b = 3$; the $x$ term is positive, so it opens left and right. Vertices $(2, 0)$ and $(-2, 0)$. Asymptotes $y = \pm\frac{3}{2}x$.

It never meets the $y$-axis: $x = 0$ gives $-\frac{y^2}{9} = 1$, so $y^2 = -9$, which has no real solution.
