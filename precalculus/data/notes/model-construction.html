Choosing a model type is half the job. The other half is finding its constants, then using the model honestly: predicting with units, finding rates of change, and saying where the model stops making sense. This is the work of the exam's modeling question (Question 2), and it is where careful habits earn the most points.

Worked example 1 (a system). A garden center sold $14$, $30$ and $30$ hundred plants in months $t = 1$, $3$ and $5$. Find the quadratic model $S(t) = at^2 + bt + c$.

Substitute each point: $a + b + c = 14$, $9a + 3b + c = 30$, $25a + 5b + c = 30$.

Eliminate $c$: the second minus the first gives $8a + 2b = 16$; the third minus the second gives $16a + 2b = 0$.

Eliminate $b$: subtract to get $8a = -16$, so $a = -2$. Then $16(-2) + 2b = 0$ gives $b = 16$, and $-2 + 16 + c = 14$ gives $c = 0$.

The model is $S(t) = -2t^2 + 16t$. Check $t = 5$: $-50 + 80 = 30$.

The same model comes from transformations. Its maximum is at $(4, 32)$, so $S(t) = a(t - 4)^2 + 32$; the point $(0, 0)$ gives $0 = 16a + 32$ and $a = -2$.

Regression: store every constant

Real data rarely fall exactly on a curve. A calculator regression (linear, quadratic, cubic or quartic in this unit) chooses the constants that bring the model closest to all the data at once. Enter the inputs and outputs as two lists, choose the regression type you justified from the data and context, and store the result.

Store, do not round. If $a = -2.589285...$, keep every digit in the calculator and use the stored model for every later step. Give only the final answer correct to three decimal places. Rounding a constant early and then predicting with it is the most common way to lose a point on Question 2.

Using the model: values, rates and units

A prediction is an output with its unit: $S(6) = -72 + 96 = 24$ hundred plants. An average rate of change is output units per input unit.

Worked example 2. Find the average rate of change of $S$ on $[1, 3]$ and say what it means.

The average rate of change of $S$ on $[1, 3]$ is $\frac{S(3) - S(1)}{3 - 1} = \frac{30 - 14}{2} = 8$ hundred plants per month. It is the slope of the secant line through $(1, 14)$ and $(3, 30)$.

In context: from month $1$ to month $3$, sales rose by $8$ hundred plants per month on average.

Inverse proportion and inverse-square laws

Some contexts call for a rational model. If $y$ is inversely proportional to $x$, then $y = \frac{k}{x}$: doubling $x$ halves $y$, as when more painters share a job. An inverse-square law, $y = \frac{k}{x^2}$, divides $y$ by $4$ when $x$ doubles, as for the brightness of a light and your distance from it.

Worked example 3. Intensity $I$ is inversely proportional to the square of the distance $d$, and $I = 200$ at $d = 3$. Find $I(d)$ and $I(6)$.

$I = \frac{k}{d^2}$, so $200 = \frac{k}{9}$ and $k = 1800$. Then $I(d) = \frac{1800}{d^2}$ and $I(6) = \frac{1800}{36} = 50$.
