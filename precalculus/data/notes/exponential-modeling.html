When a quantity changes by the same factor over equal stretches of time, an exponential model $f(t) = ab^t$ describes it: a phone losing value, a medicine leaving the blood, a gym gaining members. This page builds such models three ways, rewrites them for other time units, and uses them to predict and to find when a value is reached. It is the content of Question 2 whenever the context is exponential.

Reading $a$ and $b$ in context

$a = f(0)$ is the initial value, the amount when $t = 0$. $b$ is the growth factor per unit of $t$: each unit, the amount is multiplied by $b$. A factor $b = 1.035$ means a $3.5\%$ increase per unit; $b = 0.8$ means the amount keeps $80\%$, a $20\%$ decrease. Always say what $t = 0$ is and what one unit of $t$ is.

Worked example 1 (two points). A phone costs $\$800$ new and is worth $\$512$ after $2$ years. Find $V(t) = ab^t$.

$a = V(0) = 800$.

Over the $2$-year gap the value is multiplied by $\frac{512}{800} = 0.64$. That is two yearly factors: $b^2 = 0.64$.

Take the square root (rational exponent $\frac{1}{2}$): $b = 0.64^{1/2} = 0.8$. So $V(t) = 800(0.8)^t$: the phone keeps $80\%$ of its value each year.

If neither point is at $t = 0$, find $b$ the same way, then work back to $a$. Through $(1, 30)$ and $(3, 270)$: $b^2 = 9$, so $b = 3$, and $a \cdot 3^1 = 30$ gives $a = 10$.

With real data, use exponential regression: enter the lists, run the regression, and store $a$ and $b$ at full precision. For the medicine data on the practice page, the calculator gives $b = 0.79686...$, a decrease of about $20.314\%$ per hour.

Other time units

Rational exponents convert the factor to another unit. If the yearly factor is $1.2$, the monthly factor $m$ satisfies $m^{12} = 1.2$, so $m = 1.2^{1/12}$. The model can be written $P(t) = 1200(1.2^{1/12})^{12t}$, which is the same function as $1200(1.2)^t$.

Do not split the percent: $20\%$ a year is not $\frac{20}{12}\%$ a month, because each month builds on the last. Likewise, $30\%$ a week is a daily factor of $1.3^{1/7} \approx 1.038$, not $1 + \frac{0.3}{7}$.

A half-life $h$ means the amount halves every $h$ units: $A(t) = A_0\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}$. After $15$ years with a $5$-year half-life, $80$ grams becomes $80\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 10$ grams.

Predicting and solving

Worked example 2. For $V(t) = 800(0.8)^t$, find $V(3.5)$ and the time when the phone is worth $\$100$.

Prediction: $V(3.5) = 800(0.8)^{3.5} = 366.357...$, about $\$366.36$.

Solving: graph $y = 800(0.8)^t$ and $y = 100$ and find the intersection with the calculator: $t = 9.318...$, so about $9.319$ years. (Store the value; do not round $b$ first.)

Where the model can be trusted

A model built from data is supported near that data. Between data points, predictions are usually good. Far past the last data point, nothing in the data shows the pattern continues: the phone may stop losing value at a scrap price, and the body may clear a medicine differently once little is left. Say this with the numbers: "the data stop at $t = 5$, so a prediction at $t = 24$ is not supported by the data." In context, the domain may also have a hard edge, such as a value that cannot fall below a floor.
