3.1 Periodic Phenomena

A periodic function repeats its outputs every P units of input, f(x + P) = f(x) , and the smallest such P is the period. Learn one cycle and you know the function everywhere: shift any input by whole periods into the cycle. Increasing and decreasing intervals, concavity and rates of change repeat every period, and the average rate of change over a full period is 0 .

  • A periodic function repeats its outputs: there is a positive number PP with f(x+P)=f(x)f(x + P) = f(x) for every input xx. The smallest such PP is the period.
  • One full cycle, any interval of length PP, holds all the information. Shift inputs by whole periods to reach any other input.
  • Over any interval one full period long, the average rate of change is 00, because the output ends where it started.
  • Two inputs can share an output without being a period apart: a cycle usually passes through each in-between value twice.

3.2 Sine, Cosine, and Tangent

An angle in radians is arc length over radius, so a full turn is 2π . Place the angle θ in standard position; its terminal ray meets the unit circle at (cos θ, sin θ) , and tan θ = (sin θ)/(cos θ) is the ray's slope. These definitions work for every angle, and the quadrant sets the signs.

  • Radians: θ=arc lengthradius\theta = \frac{\text{arc length}}{\text{radius}}. On the unit circle, an angle of θ\theta radians cuts off an arc of length exactly θ\theta.
  • Convert with π\pi radians =180∘= 180^\circ: multiply degrees by π180\frac{\pi}{180}, or radians by 180π\frac{180}{\pi}.
  • Signs follow the quadrant: in Quadrant II, cos⁡θ<0\cos\theta < 0 and sin⁡θ>0\sin\theta > 0; in Quadrant III both are negative; in Quadrant IV, cos⁡θ>0\cos\theta > 0 and sin⁡θ<0\sin\theta < 0.
  • Angles that differ by 2π2\pi share a terminal ray, so they have the same sine, cosine and tangent.

3.4 Sine and Cosine Function Graphs

The graph of y = sin θ is the height of the point at angle θ on the unit circle; y = cos θ is its horizontal position. Both repeat every 2π and stay between −1 and 1 , and cosine is sine shifted π/2 to the left. Each graph is concave down above the axis and concave up below it, steepest at its zeros and flat at its highest and lowest points.

  • Both graphs have domain all real numbers, range [−1,1][-1, 1] and period 2π2\pi.
  • Five key points per cycle: sine (0,0),(0, 0),(π2,1),\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, 1\right),(π,0),(\pi, 0),(3π2,−1),\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, -1\right),(2π,0)(2\pi, 0); cosine (0,1),(0, 1),(π2,0),\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, 0\right),(π,−1),(\pi, -1),(3π2,0),\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 0\right),(2π,1)(2\pi, 1).

  • Each graph is concave down above the θ\theta-axis and concave up below it, so its rate of change is decreasing where the graph is above the axis.

3.5 Sinusoidal Functions

A sinusoidal function is a sine or cosine graph after dilations and translations. Four numbers describe it: the midline y = (max + min)/2 , the amplitude (max − min)/2 , the period (the length of one cycle) and the frequency, one over the period. Sine is odd and cosine is even.

  • A sinusoidal function has the shape of a sine graph after dilations and translations. y=sin⁡θy = \sin\theta and y=cos⁡θy = \cos\theta are both sinusoidal, each with amplitude 1, midline y=0y = 0 and period 2π2\pi.
  • Amplitude is a distance, so it is never negative. Midline is a line, so write it as an equation: y=5y = 5.
  • Frequency =1period= \frac{1}{\text{period}}. A period of 8 seconds means 18\frac{1}{8} of a cycle per second; 220 cycles per second means a period of 1220\frac{1}{220} second.
  • Sine is odd, sin⁡(−θ)=−sin⁡θ\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta; cosine is even, cos⁡(−θ)=cos⁡θ\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta.

3.7 Sinusoidal Function Context and Data Modeling

To model a periodic context, get the midline and amplitude from the greatest and least values, the period (and b = 2π/period ) from the cycle length or frequency, and the shift from one landmark time. With data, sinusoidal regression does the same job. Use the model on the domain the context supports, and describe change on an interval as two statements: the direction of the quantity and the direction of its rate of change.

  • Five labeled points on one cycle are: a highest or lowest point, a midline crossing, the opposite extreme, the other midline crossing and the first extreme again, each a quarter period apart.
  • Easy landmarks: highest at t0t_0 gives acos⁡(b(t−t0))+da\cos(b(t - t_0)) + d with a>0a > 0; lowest at t0t_0 gives the same with a<0a < 0; midline and rising at t0t_0 gives asin⁡(b(t−t0))+da\sin(b(t - t_0)) + d with a>0a > 0.
  • Describe change on an interval in two separate statements: whether the quantity is increasing or decreasing, and whether its rate of change is increasing or decreasing (concave up or concave down).
  • A model fits the facts it was built from. Past the data, or if the real cycle drifts, predictions get worse.

3.9 Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Restricted to [−π/2 , π/2] , [0, π] and (−π/2 , π/2) , sine, cosine and tangent are invertible. sin⁻¹ , cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹ take a ratio and return the one angle in that interval. Inverse is not reciprocal, and sin⁻¹ (sin x) = x only for x in the restricted interval.

  • An inverse trigonometric function takes a ratio and returns an angle.
  • sin−1⁡\sin^{-1} and tan−1⁡\tan^{-1} return angles between −π2-\frac{\pi}{2} and π2\frac{\pi}{2}; cos−1⁡\cos^{-1} returns angles between 00 and π\pi.
  • sin−1⁡x\sin^{-1} x is the inverse function, not the reciprocal 1sin⁡x\frac{1}{\sin x}.
  • For a composite like cos−1⁡(cos⁡x)\cos^{-1}(\cos x), find the inner value first, then the angle in the right range.

they have no inverse on their whole domains → sin on [−π/2 , π/2] , cos on [0, π] , tan on (−π/2 , π/2) → sin⁻¹ , cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹ , whose outputs are angles in those intervals → you get x back only when x is already in [−π/2 , π/2]

inverse sine

3.10 Trigonometric Equations and Inequalities

To solve a trigonometric equation, isolate the function, take the principal value with the inverse, find the second angle by symmetry, then add periods until you have every solution in the interval and none outside it. Inequalities follow the equation: its solutions cut the interval into pieces, and a test value decides each piece.

  • Sine and cosine take most values twice per period; the inverse key gives only one of them.
  • Second angle: π−θ\pi - \theta for sine, 2π−θ2\pi - \theta (or −θ-\theta) for cosine, θ+π\theta + \pi for tangent.
  • For sin⁡(bx)=k\sin(bx) = k, solve for bxbx on the stretched interval first, then divide by bb.
  • For an inequality, solve the equation, then test one value in each piece.

an equation like sin x = 1/2 → one principal value, such as π/6 → the second angle in the same period → every solution in the interval, and no extras

trigonometric equation
An equation or inequality in which the unknown is the input of a trigonometric function, such as 2sin⁡x=12\sin x = 1 or cos⁡(2x)≤0\cos(2x) \le 0. It usually has two solutions per period; to find all of them in an interval, isolate the function, take the principal value, use symmetry for the second angle, and add periods.

3.11 The Secant, Cosecant, and Cotangent Functions

sec x = 1/(cos x) , csc x = 1/(sin x) and cot x = (cos x)/(sin x) . Each has vertical asymptotes where its denominator is 0 . Secant and cosecant equal their partners at ±1 and never take values strictly between −1 and 1 ; cotangent takes every real value and has period π .

  • Pair them by the letter they do not share: sec⁡\sec goes with cos⁡\cos, csc⁡\csc with sin⁡\sin.
  • sec⁡\sec has asymptotes where cos⁡x=0\cos x = 0; csc⁡\csc and cot⁡\cot where sin⁡x=0\sin x = 0.
  • Where cos⁡x\cos x decreases and stays positive, sec⁡x\sec x increases: reciprocals move the opposite way.
  • sec⁡\sec and csc⁡\csc have period 2π2\pi and range (−∞,−1]∪[1,∞)(-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty); cot⁡\cot has period π\pi and range (−∞,∞)(-\infty, \infty).

3.13 Trigonometry and Polar Coordinates

3.14 Polar Function Graphs

A polar function r = f(θ) assigns a signed distance to every direction. Read it from its graph of r against θ : zeros are visits to the pole, negative values are drawn on the opposite ray, and each hump draws a petal or loop. Circles ( r = a , r = acos θ ), limaçons and cardioids ( r = a + bcos θ ) and roses ( r = asin (nθ) ) are the families to know.

  • A polar function r=f(θ)r = f(\theta) gives a distance for each direction; the curve is all points (f(θ),θ)(f(\theta), \theta).
  • Each hump of the graph of rr against θ\theta draws one loop or petal; zeros of rr are visits to the pole.
  • r=acos⁡(nθ)r = a\cos(n\theta) and r=asin⁡(nθ)r = a\sin(n\theta) are roses: nn petals if nn is odd, 2n2n if nn is even.

3.15 Rates of Change in Polar Functions

For a polar function, the average rate of change of r on [a, b] is (r(b) − r(a))/(b − a) units per radian, the slope of a secant line on the graph of r against θ . The distance from the origin is |r| : it increases where r is positive and increasing or negative and decreasing, and decreases in the other two cases. Extremes of |r| give the farthest and closest points.

  • The average rate of change of rr on [a,b][a, b] is r(b)−r(a)b−a\frac{r(b) - r(a)}{b - a}: the slope of the secant line on the graph of rr against θ\theta.
  • Whether the curve moves toward or away from the origin depends on the sign of rr and on whether rr is increasing.
  • Positive and increasing, or negative and decreasing: moving away. The other two cases: moving closer.
  • The largest |r||r| is the farthest point; r=0r = 0 is a visit to the origin.
