Rational Functions and Zeros

The real zeros of a rational function are the numerator's zeros that are in its domain.

Practice 1: Procedural and Symbolic FluencyPractice 2: Multiple Representations

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A dose of medicine works only while its concentration stays above a threshold. Asking "when is the concentration at least 88?" is solving an inequality with a rational function. The answer comes from where the function is zero, where it is undefined, and its sign in between.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The numerator of rr is 00 at an input in the domain→that input is a real zero of rr
  2. The denominator is 00 at an input→that input is not in the domain, so it is never a zero
  3. You mark zeros and excluded inputs on a number line→they split it into intervals where rr keeps one sign: test one input in each
  4. The inequality is ≤\le or ≥\ge→include the zeros with brackets; excluded inputs always get parentheses

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Real zeros of p(x)q(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}: the zeros of pp that are in the domain (not zeros of qq).
  • A sign chart splits the line at the zeros of both numerator and denominator; one test value per interval gives its sign.
  • Solve a rational inequality from the chart; include zeros only for ≤\le or ≥\ge, never an excluded input.
  • With a calculator: graph, find every zero, then read the sign between them.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: x2−4x−2\frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} has zeros 22 and −2-2, because x2−4=0x^2 - 4 = 0 there.

What actually happens: At x=2x = 2 the denominator is 00, so 22 is not in the domain and not a zero. The only zero is −2-2.

1. The table uses r(x)r(x)=(x−2)(x+3)x+1= \frac{(x - 2)(x + 3)}{x + 1}. What are the real zeros of rr?

  1. x=−3x = -3 and x=2x = 2
  2. x=−3x = -3, x=−1x = -1 and x=2x = 2
  3. x=3x = 3 and x=−2x = -2
  4. x=−1x = -1
Show the answer

The real zeros of a rational function are the zeros of the numerator that are in its domain: x=2x = 2 and x=−3x = -3. The denominator's zero, −1-1, is excluded.

  • Correct: x=−3x = -3 and x=2x = 2: Right: the numerator is 00 there, and both are in the domain.
  • x=−3x = -3, x=−1x = -1 and x=2x = 2: At x=−1x = -1 the denominator is 00: r(−1)r(-1) is undefined, not 00.
  • x=3x = 3 and x=−2x = -2: The factor x−2x - 2 is 00 at x=2x = 2, and x+3x + 3 at x=−3x = -3.
  • x=−1x = -1: −1-1 is the input not in the domain.

2. The table uses r(x)r(x)=(x−2)(x+3)x+1= \frac{(x - 2)(x + 3)}{x + 1}. Solve r(x)>0r(x) > 0.

  1. (−3,−1)∪(2,∞)(-3, -1) \cup (2, \infty)
  2. (−∞,−3)∪(−1,2)(-\infty, -3) \cup (-1, 2)
  3. [−3,−1]∪[2,∞)[-3, -1] \cup [2, \infty)
  4. (2,∞)(2, \infty)
Show the answer

Read the signs: ++ on (−3,−1)(-3, -1) and (2,∞)(2, \infty). Strict inequality, so the zeros are left out; −1-1 is never included because it is not in the domain.

  • Correct: (−3,−1)∪(2,∞)(-3, -1) \cup (2, \infty): Right: the intervals marked ++, with the endpoints left out because rr must be strictly positive.
  • (−∞,−3)∪(−1,2)(-\infty, -3) \cup (-1, 2): These are the intervals where rr is negative.
  • [−3,−1]∪[2,∞)[-3, -1] \cup [2, \infty): At −3-3 and 22, r=0r = 0, which is not >0> 0; and −1-1 is not in the domain.
  • (2,∞)(2, \infty): The interval (−3,−1)(-3, -1) is positive too: r(−2)=4r(-2) = 4.

3. The table uses r(x)r(x)=(x−2)(x+3)x+1= \frac{(x - 2)(x + 3)}{x + 1}. Solve r(x)≤0r(x) \le 0.

  1. (−∞,−3)∪(−1,2)(-\infty, -3) \cup (-1, 2)
  2. (−∞,−3]∪[−1,2](-\infty, -3] \cup [-1, 2]
  3. (−∞,−3]∪(−1,2](-\infty, -3] \cup (-1, 2]

  • Correct: (−∞,−3]∪(−1,2](-\infty, -3] \cup (-1, 2]: Right: the negative intervals plus the zeros, without −1-1.
  • [−3,−1)∪[2,∞)[-3, -1) \cup [2, \infty): These are where r≥0r \ge 0.

Summary
