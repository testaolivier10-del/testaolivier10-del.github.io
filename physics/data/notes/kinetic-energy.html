Unit 3 is about energy: a single number, a scalar, that a system has and that can change form or move from one object to another without ever being created or destroyed. Energy is one of the most useful ideas in physics because it lets you skip the step-by-step motion and compare a "before" with an "after". This page starts with the simplest form, the energy of motion.

Energy of motion

A moving object can do things a still one cannot: a rolling cart can push a spring, a thrown ball can break a window. We say the moving object has kinetic energy, written K. For an object of mass m moving at speed v,

K = ½mv²

The SI unit of energy is the joule (J). Writing the units of ½mv² shows what a joule is: kg × (m/s)² = kg·m²/s². A 1 kg cart moving at about 1.4 m/s has roughly 1 J of kinetic energy, a small amount; a car on the highway has hundreds of thousands of joules.

This K is sometimes called translational kinetic energy, the energy of an object moving from place to place. Treat the object as a point object: its mass and its speed are all that matter.

Why the square matters

Mass and speed do not count equally. K is proportional to m, so doubling the mass doubles K. But K is proportional to v², so doubling the speed multiplies K by 2² = 4, and tripling it multiplies K by 9.

That is why speed matters so much on the road. With the same brakes, the braking force and so the deceleration are the same at any speed. From the kinematic equation v² = v₀² + 2aΔx with v = 0, the stopping distance is v₀²/(2a): it grows with the square of the speed, just like K. A car at 20 m/s needs four times the stopping distance of the same car at 10 m/s.

Worked example 1. A 1200 kg car moves at 10 m/s. What is its kinetic energy? What is it at 20 m/s?

Start from the definition: K = ½mv².

At 10 m/s: K = ½(1200 kg)(10 m/s)² = ½(1200)(100) kg·m²/s² = 6.0 × 10⁴ J.

At 20 m/s: K = ½(1200 kg)(20 m/s)² = 2.4 × 10⁵ J, four times as much, as the v² predicts.

Ratios like this are a favorite exam question: "if the speed is halved, the kinetic energy is…" Write K ∝ mv², put in the factors, and read the answer: half the speed gives (½)² = ¼ of the kinetic energy.

A scalar that is never negative

Velocity has a direction, carried by its sign on a line. Kinetic energy does not. Squaring the velocity removes the sign: a 2.0 kg cart moving left at 3.0 m/s has K = ½(2.0 kg)(−3.0 m/s)² = +9.0 J, exactly the same as moving right. Kinetic energy can be zero (for an object at rest) but never negative.

The kinetic energy of a system of several objects is the sum of each object's kinetic energy. Because each one is positive, they never cancel. Two 1.0 kg carts moving toward each other at 2.0 m/s have velocities that cancel as vectors, but the system's kinetic energy is 2.0 J + 2.0 J = 4.0 J.

It depends on who is watching

Speed depends on the reference frame, so kinetic energy does too. A passenger sitting in a train moving at 25 m/s has zero kinetic energy measured from the train and a large kinetic energy measured from the ground. Neither answer is wrong; they belong to different frames. On the exam, unless a frame is named, kinetic energy is measured from the ground.

Changes in kinetic energy

Much of Unit 3 is about how kinetic energy changes. The change is final minus initial, each computed separately:

ΔK = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i²

Worked example 2. A 0.40 kg cart speeds up from 1.5 m/s to 2.5 m/s. By how much does its kinetic energy change?

K_i = ½(0.40 kg)(1.5 m/s)² = 0.45 J. K_f = ½(0.40 kg)(2.5 m/s)² = 1.25 J.

ΔK = 1.25 J − 0.45 J = 0.80 J.

Check the trap: ½m(Δv)² = ½(0.40)(1.0)² = 0.20 J, four times too small. Square first, then subtract.

Solving K = ½mv² for the speed is the other common step: v = √(2K/m). Keep the 2, and take the square root at the end.
