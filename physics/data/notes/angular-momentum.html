Momentum, p = mv, measured how much motion an object carries in a straight line, and impulse measured how a force changes it. Rotation has its own pair: angular momentum and angular impulse. They behave so much like their straight-line partners that you can often reason by analogy. One new idea needs care: a moving point object can have angular momentum about a point even when nothing is spinning.

Angular momentum of a turning object

For a rigid object turning about a fixed axis, angular momentum is rotational inertia times angular velocity:

L = Iω

The unit is kg·m²/s. Its sign follows ω: positive for counterclockwise, negative for clockwise (with the usual choice). A bicycle wheel with I = 0.25 kg·m² spinning at 12 rad/s has L = 3.0 kg·m²/s.

Angular momentum of a point object

Now take a single small object, a puck, sliding in a straight line at constant velocity on a frictionless table. Pick a point P beside its path. The puck has angular momentum about P:

L = rmv sin θ

Here r is the distance from P to the puck and θ is the angle between r and the velocity. The product r sin θ is the perpendicular distance from P to the puck's line of motion, the lever arm of its momentum. So L is the momentum mv times that lever arm.

Two consequences surprise most students.

  • As the puck slides by, r grows and shrinks, but r sin θ stays equal to the fixed distance d from P to the line. So the puck's angular momentum about P is constant: L = mvd, the same at every position.
  • Angular momentum depends on the point you choose. About a point Q on the puck's own path, the lever arm is zero and L = 0.

Worked example 1. A 0.20 kg puck slides at 3.0 m/s along a line that passes 0.40 m from point P. What is its angular momentum about P?

L = mv(r sin θ) = mvd = (0.20 kg)(3.0 m/s)(0.40 m) = 0.24 kg·m²/s.

A puck of the same mass at 6.0 m/s on a line 0.20 m from P has the same L: twice the speed, half the lever arm.

A ball whirled on a string in a circle of radius r at speed v also has L = rmv about the center, since its velocity is perpendicular to r. That matches L = Iω with I = mr² and ω = v/r.

Angular impulse changes angular momentum

A net torque acting for a time Δt delivers an angular impulse, τΔt, and that is exactly the change in angular momentum:

τnetΔt = ΔL

If the torque changes with time, the angular impulse is the area under a graph of torque against time. Rearranged, τnet = ΔL/Δt: the net torque is the slope of a graph of L against time. When I is constant this is the same as τnet = Iα from Unit 5.

Worked example 2. A wheel (I = 0.50 kg·m²) starts at rest. A net torque of +4.0 N·m acts for 2.0 s, then nothing for 2.0 s, then −2.0 N·m for 2.0 s. Find L and ω at the end.

Add the signed areas under τ-t: (4.0)(2.0) + 0 + (−2.0)(2.0) = 8.0 − 4.0 = 4.0 kg·m²/s.

L = 4.0 kg·m²/s, so ω = L/I = (4.0 kg·m²/s)/(0.50 kg·m²) = 8.0 rad/s. The L-t graph rises with slope 4.0, stays flat, then falls with slope −2.0.

Keep τΔt and τΔθ apart. Torque times time changes the angular momentum; torque times angle (the work) changes the kinetic energy.

Going further: in full three-dimensional physics, angular momentum is a vector along the axis, found with a right-hand rule. This course stays with one plane: you describe L as clockwise or counterclockwise.
