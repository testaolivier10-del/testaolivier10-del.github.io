"How fast are you moving?" sounds like it has one answer. It does not. Sitting at your desk you are at rest relative to the room, moving at about 400 m/s relative to Earth's center as Earth spins, and much faster relative to the Sun. Each answer is right for a different observer. This page shows how to describe motion from different points of view and how to convert between them.

Reference frames

A reference frame is the point of view of an observer, together with a coordinate system that moves with that observer. Positions, displacements and velocities are all measured relative to some frame. When a problem says "a car moves at 20 m/s", it almost always means relative to the ground, but that is a choice, and sometimes another frame makes the problem easier.

You know this from experience. In a train waiting at a station, you see the next train start to move and feel, for a moment, that it is your own train moving backward. From your seat you can only see how the two trains move relative to each other, and either train moving gives the same view.

Adding velocities along a line

A train moves at +20.0 m/s relative to the ground. A passenger walks toward the front at +1.5 m/s relative to the train. Each second, the train carries her 20.0 m forward and she walks another 1.5 m, so relative to the ground she moves 21.5 m each second.

v(object relative to ground) = v(object relative to frame) + v(frame relative to ground)

Signs carry the direction. If she walks toward the back, her velocity relative to the train is −1.5 m/s, and relative to the ground it is −1.5 + 20.0 = +18.5 m/s: still forward, just slower than the train.

Worked example 1. A river flows east at 1.2 m/s. A boat moves at 3.6 m/s relative to the water. Find the boat's velocity relative to the bank going downstream (east) and upstream (west). East is positive.

Downstream: v = +3.6 m/s + 1.2 m/s = +4.8 m/s.

Upstream: v = −3.6 m/s + 1.2 m/s = −2.4 m/s.

A 300 m trip takes 300 ÷ 4.8 = 63 s downstream and 300 ÷ 2.4 = 125 s upstream. The round trip (about 190 s) is slower than on still water (600 ÷ 3.6 = 170 s), because the boat spends more time at the slow speed.

Velocity seen by a moving observer

Turn the equation around to find how one moving object looks from another. Subtract the observer's velocity:

v(A relative to B) = v(A relative to ground) − v(B relative to ground)

Worked example 2. On a highway (east positive), a bus moves at +18 m/s and a scooter at −12 m/s, both relative to the road. Find the scooter's velocity relative to the bus.

v(scooter, bus) = −12 m/s − (+18 m/s) = −30. m/s.

From the bus, the oncoming scooter approaches at 30. m/s. That is why head-on closing speeds are so dangerous: the two speeds add.

Swapping the observer flips the sign. If a car moves at +5 m/s relative to a bus, the bus moves at −5 m/s relative to the car. And an object at rest relative to one frame (a passenger sitting on the train) moves at that frame's velocity relative to the ground.

Accelerations agree

A cart on a flatcar speeds up from rest to 0.80 m/s relative to the flatcar in 2.0 s. The flatcar rolls at a steady 6.0 m/s. Relative to the ground, the cart goes from 6.0 m/s to 6.8 m/s in the same 2.0 s. The change in velocity, 0.80 m/s, is the same in both frames, so the acceleration is too: 0.40 m/s².

This works whenever the two frames move at a constant velocity relative to each other: the same velocity is added at every instant and cancels when you take Δv. If the frame itself speeds up or slows down (a braking truck), observers in it measure different accelerations. Unless a problem says otherwise, assume frames that move at a constant velocity relative to the ground.

Scope note

In this course, relative velocities are added along one line only (east and west, up and down the river). A boat crossing a river at an angle to the current is a two-dimensional problem and is not tested here.
