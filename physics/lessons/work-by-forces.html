Unit 3 · Topic 3.2

Work

Work is W = F d cos θ: energy carried into an object (positive) or out of it (negative) by a force along a displacement.

Practice 1: Creating RepresentationsPractice 2: Mathematical RoutinesPractice 3: Scientific Questioning and Argumentation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Pushing on a stuck car until your arms ache changes nothing about the car, yet a gentle shove on a shopping cart sends it rolling. In physics, work means energy moved into or out of an object by a force acting along a displacement, and that definition explains why effort alone does not count.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A force acts on an object while the object moves→the force does work W = F d cos θ, using only the component along the displacement
  2. The force has a component along the motion→the work is positive and energy enters the object
  3. The force opposes the motion (like kinetic friction)→the work is negative and energy leaves the object
  4. The force is perpendicular to the motion→it does no work and does not change the speed
  5. All the works on an object are added→the net work equals the change in kinetic energy: W_net = ΔK

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Work is energy moved into or out of a system by a force acting along a displacement: W = F d cos θ, with θ between the force and the displacement. It is a scalar, in joules.
  • Positive work adds energy, negative work removes it, and a force perpendicular to the motion does none.
  • The work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, W_net = ΔK.
  • Work by a varying force is the area under its force-position graph.
  • Kinetic friction does negative work that grows with the path length, even on a round trip.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Gravity does work on a crate dragged across a level floor, because gravity acts on it the whole time.

What actually happens: A force does work only through its component along the displacement. Gravity is vertical and the motion is horizontal, so gravity does zero work here.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

Diagram

A crate pulled across a rough floor

The crate starts from rest. Use g = 9.8 m/s².

1. How much work does the rope do on the crate over the 4.00 m? Give the value and its unit.

Type a number and its unit.

Show the answer

W = F_T d cos θ = (45.0 N)(4.00 m)(cos 30°) = 156 J. The angle is between the force and the displacement.

  • Answer: 156 J

2. How much work does kinetic friction do on the crate over the 4.00 m, in joules? Include the sign.

Type a number in J.

Show the answer

Friction points opposite the displacement (θ = 180°), so W = f_k d cos 180° = −(28.0 N)(4.00 m) = −112 J.

  • Answer: -112 J

3. How much work do the normal force and the gravitational force do on the crate over the 4.00 m?

  1. Zero each: each is perpendicular to the displacement
  2. +696 J by the normal force and −784 J by gravity
  3. Zero by the normal force, −784 J by gravity
  4. Both positive, since both act on the crate while it moves
Show the answer

Both forces are vertical and the crate moves horizontally, so θ = 90° and W = Fd cos 90° = 0 for each.

  • Correct: Zero each: each is perpendicular to the displacement: Right: cos 90° = 0, so a force at right angles to the motion does no work.
  • +696 J by the normal force and −784 J by gravity: These multiply each force by 4.00 m as if it pointed along the motion. Both are vertical; the motion is horizontal.
  • Zero by the normal force, −784 J by gravity: Gravity is also perpendicular to the horizontal displacement, so it does no work here.
  • Both positive, since both act on the crate while it moves: A force does work only through its component along the displacement. Both of these have none.

4. What is the net work done on the crate over the 4.00 m, in joules?

Type a number in J.

Show the answer

W_net = 155.9 J + (−112 J) + 0 + 0 = 43.9 J. By the work-energy theorem this is also the crate's change in kinetic energy.

  • Answer: 43.9 J

5. A 0.17 kg puck slides at 6.0 m/s onto rough ice, where a 0.50 N friction force slows it. How far does it slide before stopping, in meters?

Type a number in m.

Show the answer

Friction does all the work: −f d = 0 − ½mv², so d = mv²/(2f) = (0.17 kg)(6.0 m/s)²/(2 × 0.50 N) = 6.1 m.

  • Answer: 6.1 m

6. Take a sled as the system. A rope outside the system pulls it forward and does +40 J of work on it. What does this tell you?

  1. 40 J of energy left the sled
  2. 40 J of energy entered the sled
  3. The sled's speed is certain to increase
  4. The rope gained 40 J
Show the answer

Work by an external force is energy crossing the system boundary: +40 J means 40 J entered the sled. Whether the sled speeds up depends on the net work of all the forces.

  • 40 J of energy left the sled: Positive work brings energy in; negative work takes it out.
  • Correct: 40 J of energy entered the sled: Right: positive work by an external force is an energy transfer into the system.
  • The sled's speed is certain to increase: The net work decides the change in speed. Other forces, such as friction, may do negative work.
  • The rope gained 40 J: The rope gave energy to the sled; it did not gain it.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Work is W = F d cos θ: energy carried into an object (positive) or out of it (negative) by a force along a displacement. Perpendicular forces do none. The net work equals ΔK, and a varying force's work is the area under its force-position graph.
