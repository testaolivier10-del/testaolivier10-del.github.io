Start at question 1

Diagram

Three blocks in water

The blocks are at rest. The water has density 1000 kg/m³.

1. Rank the buoyant forces on the three blocks, from largest to smallest.

  1. Z, then Y, then X
  2. X, then Y, then Z
  3. X, Y and Z are equal
  4. Y, then X, then Z
Show the answer

F_b = ρ_water V_displaced g. Z displaces its whole volume, Y three quarters, X one half. The blocks have equal volumes, so Z > Y > X.

  • Correct: Z, then Y, then X: Right: more displaced water, larger buoyant force.
  • X, then Y, then Z: This reverses the ranking; floating higher means less water displaced.
  • X, Y and Z are equal: Equal volumes are not equal displaced volumes: X and Y are only partly under water.
  • Y, then X, then Z: Z, fully under water, displaces the most.

2. How does the buoyant force on block Y compare with the gravitational force on Y?

  1. They are equal, because Y is floating at rest
  2. The buoyant force is larger, because Y floats
  3. The buoyant force is smaller, because Y is partly above the water
  4. The buoyant force is larger, because Y is denser than X
Show the answer

Y is at rest with only two forces on it, so they balance: F_b = F_g. Floating means the buoyant force equals the weight, not that it exceeds it.

  • Correct: They are equal, because Y is floating at rest: Right: equilibrium of a floating object.
  • The buoyant force is larger, because Y floats: If F_b were larger, Y would accelerate upward until it displaced less water.
  • The buoyant force is smaller, because Y is partly above the water: Being partly out of the water lowers F_b only until it matches the weight.
  • The buoyant force is larger, because Y is denser than X: Y is denser than X, which is why it sits lower; it still floats with F_b = F_g.

3. Block Z rests on the bottom. Which statement about the forces on Z is correct?

  1. F_b is less than Z's weight; the floor's normal force makes up the rest
  2. There is no buoyant force on Z, because water is not pushing up from under it
  3. The buoyant force equals Z's weight, because Z is at rest
  4. The buoyant force on Z is smaller than on X, because Z is denser
Show the answer

Z displaces its full volume, so it does feel a buoyant force, but Z is denser than water, so F_b < F_g. At rest, F_b + F_N = F_g.

  • Correct: F_b is less than Z's weight; the floor's normal force makes up the rest: Right: three forces balance on the sunk block.
  • There is no buoyant force on Z, because water is not pushing up from under it: The model treats the water as pushing up on Z, giving a buoyant force ρVg; the floor adds a normal force.
  • The buoyant force equals Z's weight, because Z is at rest: At rest the net force is zero, but the normal force is part of the balance.
  • The buoyant force on Z is smaller than on X, because Z is denser: The buoyant force depends on the displaced volume, not the block's density: Z displaces the most.

4. Block X is pushed down until it is just fully under water and held there. How does the buoyant force on it now compare with before?

  1. Twice as large
  2. The same
  3. Half as large
  4. Four times as large
Show the answer

Floating, X displaced half its volume; fully under, it displaces all of it. F_b ∝ V_displaced, so it doubles. The extra push comes from the hand.

  • Correct: Twice as large: Right: twice the displaced volume, twice the buoyant force.
  • The same: The buoyant force depends on how much water is displaced, which doubled.
  • Half as large: Pushing it deeper displaces more water, not less.
  • Four times as large: The displaced volume doubled, so the force doubles, not quadruples.

Experimental setup

Squeezing a syringe

Two identical sealed syringes have their tips blocked. Syringe 1 holds 20 cm³ of air; syringe 2 holds 20 cm³ of water. A student pushes each plunger with the same force.

The plunger of syringe 1 moves in noticeably and springs back when released. The plunger of syringe 2 hardly moves at all.

5. What does the experiment show about the two fluids?

  1. Water is nearly incompressible; air is easily compressed
  2. Both fluids are incompressible, since neither leaks out
  3. The air is nearly incompressible, but the water is compressed
  4. Neither is a fluid, because they are trapped in the syringe
Show the answer

Squeezing changed the air's volume noticeably but not the water's. Liquids are close to incompressible because their particles are already touching; gas particles are far apart.

  • Correct: Water is nearly incompressible; air is easily compressed: Right: water's volume hardly changes under the push.
  • Both fluids are incompressible, since neither leaks out: Not leaking does not make a fluid incompressible; the air's volume clearly shrank.
  • The air is nearly incompressible, but the water is compressed: This reverses the observations: the air plunger moved in.
  • Neither is a fluid, because they are trapped in the syringe: A fluid is defined by flowing and taking its container's shape, which both do.

6. What happens to the density of the air in syringe 1 while the plunger is pushed in?

  1. It increases, because the same mass occupies a smaller volume
  2. It stays the same, because no air leaves the syringe
  3. It decreases, because the particles are pushed apart
  4. It stays the same, because density is a property of the material
Show the answer

The blocked tip keeps the mass of air fixed, while the volume shrinks. ρ = m/V, so the density rises. A compressible fluid's density can change.

  • Correct: It increases, because the same mass occupies a smaller volume: Right: same m, smaller V, larger ρ.
  • It stays the same, because no air leaves the syringe: The mass stays the same, but density is mass per volume and the volume shrank.
  • It decreases, because the particles are pushed apart: Pushing the plunger in squeezes the particles closer together, not farther apart.
  • It stays the same, because density is a property of the material: For an incompressible fluid density is fixed, but air is compressible.

7. When a fluid is treated as ideal on the exam, which property is it assumed to have?

  1. Its density stays the same as it flows and is squeezed
  2. The fluid sticks to the walls of its container
  3. The fluid has no mass, so it exerts no forces
  4. The fluid keeps its shape when it is poured
Show the answer

An ideal fluid is incompressible (constant density) and nonviscous (no internal friction), and its flow is smooth and steady.

  • Correct: Its density stays the same as it flows and is squeezed: Right: incompressible means constant density.
  • The fluid sticks to the walls of its container: Sticking to walls is a sign of viscosity, which an ideal fluid lacks.
  • The fluid has no mass, so it exerts no forces: An ideal fluid has mass and weight; only its friction is ignored.
  • The fluid keeps its shape when it is poured: No fluid keeps its shape; that is the difference between a fluid and a solid.

8. Why is liquid water much harder to compress than air?

  1. Water particles already touch; air particles are far apart
  2. Water particles are locked in fixed places, as in a solid
  3. Water has a larger mass than air, so it is harder to move
  4. Air particles attract each other more strongly than water particles do
Show the answer

In a liquid the particles touch and slide past each other; there is little empty space to squeeze out. In a gas most of the volume is empty space between particles.

  • Correct: Water particles already touch; air particles are far apart: Right: the spacing of the particles decides how compressible the fluid is.
  • Water particles are locked in fixed places, as in a solid: Water particles can slide past each other; that is why water flows.
  • Water has a larger mass than air, so it is harder to move: The two syringes hold the same volume; the difference is in particle spacing, not mass.
  • Air particles attract each other more strongly than water particles do: In a gas the attractions between particles are weak, which is why they spread out.

9. A woman of weight 600 N stands on one heel of area 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m². What pressure does the heel exert on the floor? Give the value and its unit.

Type a number and its unit.

Show the answer

P = F⊥/A = 600 N / 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 4.0 × 10⁶ Pa, about 40 times atmospheric pressure. The same weight on a flat shoe of 150 cm² gives only 4.0 × 10⁴ Pa.

  • Answer: 4.0 × 106 Pa

10. Treat blood as an ideal fluid. It flows at 0.30 m/s in an artery. At a narrowed section, the area is one third of the normal area. What is the blood's speed in the narrowed section? Give the value and its unit.

Type a number and its unit.

Show the answer

Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ with A₂ = A₁/3, so v₂ = 3v₁ = 0.90 m/s.

  • Answer: 0.90 m/s

11. A rectangular swimming pool is 10.0 m long, 5.0 m wide and filled to a depth of 2.0 m with water of density 1000 kg/m³. What is the mass of the water? Give the value and its unit.

Type a number and its unit.

Show the answer

V = 10.0 m × 5.0 m × 2.0 m = 100 m³. m = ρV = (1000 kg/m³)(100 m³) = 1.0 × 10⁵ kg, about 100 metric tons.

  • Answer: 1.0 × 105 kg

12. Three containers have different shapes: a tall narrow tube, a wide bowl and a cone that widens upward. All are filled with water to the same height. Where is the pressure at the bottom greatest?

  1. It is the same in all three
  2. In the wide bowl, because it holds the most water
  3. In the narrow tube, because the water is concentrated
  4. In the cone, because its walls push the water down
Show the answer

Pressure in a fluid at rest depends only on depth: P = P₀ + ρgh. Same depth of the same liquid, same pressure, whatever the amount or shape.

  • Correct: It is the same in all three: Right: only depth (and ρ and g) set the pressure.
  • In the wide bowl, because it holds the most water: More water means more total weight, but it is spread over a larger bottom area; the pressure is the same.
  • In the narrow tube, because the water is concentrated: Narrowness does not concentrate pressure; depth decides.
  • In the cone, because its walls push the water down: The walls do push on the water, but the result is still P = P₀ + ρgh.
