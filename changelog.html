What changed, and when.

Log

A&P: special senses and the autonomic nervous system 24 September 2026

  • A&PTwo more nervous system chapters are live: the special senses (taste and smell, vision, hearing and balance) and the autonomic nervous system (its anatomy, transmitters and receptor proteins, and how it controls the organs). The lab practical gains eye, ear and sympathetic chain stations, and the graph reader gains dark adaptation, the audiogram and autonomic blockade.

A&P: the peripheral nervous system 24 September 2026

  • A&PThe peripheral nervous system chapter is live: spinal nerves and plexuses, the cranial nerves, sensory and motor pathways, reflexes and the neurological exam. The lab practical gains cranial nerve, brachial plexus and dermatome stations, and the calculators gain reflex delay and the Glasgow Coma Scale.

A&P: nervous tissue and the central nervous system 23 September 2026

  • A&PTwo nervous system chapters are live: nervous tissue (organization, neurons and glia, the action potential in detail, synapses) and the central nervous system (brain regions, the meninges and cerebrospinal fluid, the spinal cord, and higher functions such as memory, language and sleep). The lab practical gains neuron, glia, brain, meninges and spinal cord stations, and the other tools gain nervous system items.
  • A&PThe tools now show only chapters that are published, so items still being checked no longer appear early.

A&P: the respiratory system 23 September 2026

  • A&PThe respiratory chapter is live: nine topics from the nose to the alveoli, the mechanics of breathing, gas exchange, oxygen and carbon dioxide transport, and the control of breathing, each with a lesson, full notes and practice questions. The lab practical gains four stations (nose, larynx, bronchial tree and alveoli), and predict the change, the pathway tracer, the graph reader and the calculators gain respiratory items.

A new course: Anatomy & Physiology (Beta) 23 September 2026

  • A&PThe first part of a full two-semester anatomy and physiology course is live as a Beta: the foundations (orientation to the body, chemistry and physics for physiology, cells, tissues, signals and homeostasis) and the whole cardiovascular system. Every topic has an interactive lesson and a full notes page, and they are built in an order where no page uses a term before the lesson that teaches it.
  • A&PTools built for physiology reasoning: a virtual lab practical on real figures, predict the change, a feedback loop builder, a pathway tracer, a graph reader, worked-example calculators and a word root builder, plus practice, spaced review, exams with a TEAS A&P practice mode, flashcards and a mastery dashboard.
  • SiteThe course follows current published sources and is pending review by a licensed A&P instructor. Where the science has moved on, it teaches the current explanation and notes the older answer an exam may still expect. Figures from OpenStax are used under CC BY 4.0 and credited on each figure.

Corrections checked against published guidelines 22 September 2026

  • NREMTThe questions the accuracy review could not fix with a word change are rebuilt, each against current published guidance: the 2025 AHA/AAP neonatal guideline, the NASEMSO National Model EMS Clinical Guidelines, the 2019 National EMS Scope of Practice Model and the standard definitions they rest on. Epinephrine for anaphylaxis no longer asks for a prior reaction. The sepsis screen now lists the actual SIRS criteria and says which extras are EMS additions. The formulary is the seven core drugs, not all of them. Delivery no longer suctions between head and shoulders, an open chest wound is sealed before you listen, and the blood-loss, airway-adjunct and carbon-monoxide questions each have one defensible answer.
  • OchemThe remaining chemistry items were checked against standard texts and IUPAC rules. The acyl substitution and Claisen mechanisms now draw the tetrahedral intermediate the collapse step happens on, and E2 arrows run from bond to bond.
  • SiteThese corrections follow current published guidelines but have not yet had formal review by a licensed EMT or paramedic instructor. A few where sources disagree or protocols vary are marked for that review. If your local protocol says otherwise, follow your protocol.

Every page checked for accuracy 22 September 2026

  • NREMTAbout 160 explanations pointed at the wrong answer. They named answers by position — “option 3”, “choice B” — but practice shuffles the answers for every student, and most of the numbers did not match even the stored order. In about forty the reference landed on the correct answer and called it wrong. Every one now names the answer it means by what it says.
  • NREMTClinical corrections across the bank, the notes and the reference pages, 214 questions in all. Status epilepticus is five minutes in the notes too. A flail segment is two or more ribs, not three. The OPA landmarks were swapped. Trendelenburg is head-down, not legs-up. An arrow is a low-velocity weapon. Children have more blood per kilogram than adults, not less. Rehab sits in the cold zone. The pelvic binder goes over the greater trochanters, not the iliac crests. Tourniquets go 2–3 inches above the wound, pregnant cardiac arrest uses the standard hand position with the uterus pushed aside, and the skill-sheet page lists ten stations because there are ten.
  • OchemCorrections in 58 textbook sections, 42 lessons, four mechanisms and 94 practice questions. A figure labeled 2-methylbutanal drew 3-methylpentanal. The Wolff–Kishner mechanism lost N2 in the wrong order. The rate-determining step is the highest transition state, and one lesson question was keyed to the other one. Diisopropylamine does not shuttle protons between enolates; leftover ketone does. PET and nylon are below their Tg at room temperature, not above it. Isobutyl, 2-methyl-2-butene and a bicyclo[3.3.1] ring now carry their right names.
  • OchemTwo tools gave wrong answers. The reaction predictor called benzyl bromide with KOtBu, and ammonia with a secondary halide, SN1 — both are SN2. The conformation lab’s 2-methylbutane curve had the wrong symmetry, and it said cyclohexane flips billions of times a second (about a hundred thousand is right).
  • SiteEvery correction was proposed by one reviewer and checked by a second before it went in; the ones that need a new drawing or a rewritten question are listed for the author rather than patched.

A map of every reagent in the course 22 September 2026

  • OchemAn eighth tool: the Reagent Roadmap. Pick where you start and where you need to end up — propan-2-ol to propan-1-ol, say — and it finds the shortest routes, with the reagents, the regio- and stereochemistry and the section that teaches each step. It can also look up everything one reagent does, or show what a group becomes, what makes it and what does not work on it. The routes are honest about substrates: an alkene made from a tertiary alcohol has no H on one carbon, so the map will not pretend it can become an alkyne.
  • OchemThe “Not quite” label under a wrong quiz answer, in every tool, was amber text on an amber tint at 2.3:1 contrast. It uses the darker amber the tags use now, and a tool page is on the accessibility check’s list so the next one is caught.

Flashcards for Organic Chemistry 22 September 2026

  • OchemThere is a flashcard deck now, under its own tab next to Review. Every concept in the course, the pKa, IR and NMR values, reagent menus and comparison tables from the notes, and the named reactions — 663 cards, filterable by chapter, by card type, and to the topics you have studied or the ones practice says you are shaky on. Grade each card Again, Hard, Good or Easy and it comes back just before you would forget it; twenty new cards a day, so the deck grows at a pace you can keep. Flip with a tap, Space or a swipe; every card links back to the section that teaches it. The cards are read out of the notes rather than typed out again, so a value corrected in the notes is corrected on its card. Your schedule syncs with your account and earns a little XP for the cards you review on schedule. Open the deck.

Practice that asks you to do the chemistry 22 September 2026

  • OchemOxidation & Reduction now asks what happens, not what things are called. 50 recall questions across the five redox sections are replaced by ones that hand you a molecule and a reagent: NaBH4 or LiAlH4 on a keto-ester, PCC and Jones on the same alcohol, DIBAL-H at −78 °C on an ester, oxidation states counted on real molecules, and three-step “what is Z” problems.
  • OchemSpectroscopy gives you the spectrum. 40 new questions hand you the data — IR bands, 1H shifts with integration and splitting, 13C counts with DEPT, molecular ions, isotope patterns and fragments, UV λmax — and ask which structure fits. They replace questions that asked what each technique measures, and they use the same values the notes teach.
  • Ochem100 one-line explanations in the enolate sections are now real ones, saying why the answer is right and why the tempting wrong answers are wrong. Rewriting them turned up three questions that needed fixing: one keyed a rule the chapter had already retired (thermodynamic control needs the enolates to interconvert, not the deprotonation to reverse), one overstated how badly malonate handles a secondary halide, and one left a second reaction open that made a wrong answer defensible.

Three structures the notes named and never drew 22 September 2026

  • OchemSuccinic anhydride, the β-lactam and a polymer taken apart are now drawn. The cyclic-anhydride paragraph gets its four structures, from succinic acid through the amic acid to succinimide. The β-lactam paragraph gets an ordinary amide, the four-membered ring and penicillin side by side, with the enzyme’s serine opening the ring. Polymer design gets polypropylene, PET and nylon 6,6 cut back to their monomers, with the PET cut to avoid marked. The polyamide rule now says which C–N bond to cut: the amide one, not the bond to the CH2.

Forty corrections, and three more checks 15 September 2026

  • NREMTSeven answers were simply keyed wrong. SLUDGE listed sweating instead of lacrimation. A choking two-year-old was keyed to the infant sequence of back blows and chest thrusts, when a child that age gets abdominal thrusts. Two START questions were wrong in opposite directions: a walking-wounded exclusion keyed red when it is yellow, and a patient with agonal breathing and no radial pulse keyed expectant, when under START anyone still breathing is immediate. An APGAR scored 8 when the stem totals 7 — and 7 was not even offered. Status epilepticus said ten minutes; the definition is five, which another question in the same bank already used.
  • NREMTFive questions had the previous question’s answer options. A question about a shallow-water diving injury offered four options about blood pressure. The stems and the explanations were right and untouched, which is why nobody caught it — the file parses, the ids are unique, every count matches. The explanations described the intended wrong answers closely enough to rebuild all five.
  • NREMTEleven pairs of questions contradicted each other, so a student meeting both was marked wrong on one. Suction time, OPA sizing, shock positioning, helmet removal, flail chest, red lights, chest seals, oxygen at 88%, and whether bleeding or airway comes first. Each now has one answer, and where the notes disagreed the notes changed too.
  • NREMTGuidance that had gone stale: a significant fall is more than 10 feet, not 20. Burns get about twenty minutes of cool running water, not “a brief period”. Newborns are no longer routinely suctioned. A palpable pulse during compressions is not evidence the compressions are working, and was keyed as if it were. Bleeding from a dialysis fistula gets firm pressure — the old answer told you not to fully occlude it, which is advice that can cost a patient their life to save a fistula.
  • NREMTQuestions about intubation, capnography and 12-lead interpretation now say whose hands are on the equipment, since none of it is EMT scope. The knowledge is worth having when you are the one ventilating; pretending it is your skill is not.
  • SiteA third answer tell, and this one was ours. Fixing the “longest answer is correct” tell moved explanation out of the keys and into the wrong answers, where it lands as a clause explaining why the option is wrong. On 182 questions exactly one option carries such a clause, and it is the answer 17% of the time against a 25% baseline. An option that argues against itself is rarely the key, and that is a third free rule for anyone who notices. It is now measured on every build, alongside a check that no question can inherit its neighbor’s options again.

Four checks, and the things they found 15 September 2026

  • NREMTThe notes and the questions disagreed about oxygen for COPD, and now they do not. Chapter 10 taught that too much oxygen suppresses a COPD patient’s drive to breathe, while the questions correctly said a hypoxic COPD patient still gets oxygen. Both are now the same rule: the physiology is real, it is a reason to titrate toward the patient’s own baseline, and it is never a reason to withhold oxygen from someone who is hypoxic.
  • NREMTBleeding control taught the wrong first move. The notes ran direct pressure → packing → tourniquet as one ladder for everything. Life-threatening extremity bleeding does not climb the ladder — it gets a tourniquet immediately — and the motorcycle scenario now scores it that way rather than rewarding a worked-up escalation. Elevation is gone from bleeding control entirely; it delayed the pressure that actually works.
  • NREMTThe stroke scenario scored a glucose check as a mistake, which contradicted the notes and the flow diagram. Hypoglycemia is the classic stroke mimic. The screen still goes first because it takes thirty seconds and needs nothing but your eyes, but the glucose now happens on the best path instead of never happening at all.
  • NREMTTwo answers were fixed rather than the bank around them: one told you to withhold glucose or give it, which is not an answer, and the tourniquet question made a tourniquet conditional on direct pressure failing first. The body map now names the liver under the lower right ribs, not just the spleen under the left, and stops calling every temporal fracture a basilar skull fracture — they are two different injuries with two different presentations.
  • OchemSeven molecules were drawn with methyl groups missing a hydrogen, acetone among them — a course whose first module is counting bonds was shipping structures that fail the count. All seven are fixed, and a valence check now fails the build on any recurrence. Drawings that deliberately show only the hydrogens under discussion, like the chairs, say so in the file.
  • OchemThe SN2 walkthrough numbered its intro card as a step, so every card read one ahead of its own heading — “Step 2 / 9” above “Step 1 · Identify”. The step rail also labeled itself from each step’s question stem, so it filled with sentences cut off mid-question and fell back to a bare “Step 2” where a step had no stem; it uses the short eyebrow now.
  • OchemAniline cannot simply be nitrated, and the directing-effects section implied it was the fastest substrate going. Nitrating acid protonates the amine to a deactivating meta director. The workaround — acetylate, nitrate, hydrolyze — is now spelled out, because it is exactly what gets asked. The pKa table also had the 1,3-diketone filed below phenol despite being more acidic.
  • SiteFour new checks run on every build: a valence check on molecule drawings, two answer-wording checks, and a scenario graph check. The wording ones measure a tell the position and length checks could not see — on 441 questions exactly one option contained an absolute word like “always” or “never”, and that option was the answer 6.6% of the time against a 25% baseline. A student who learns that one rule can eliminate an option on a fifth of the bank. Like the other ceilings, these start at what was measured, not at the target, so the number cannot get worse while the rewriting happens.

Telling us when something is wrong, and being told when something is due 15 September 2026

  • BothThere is a “Report a problem” link under every explanation now — in the results list after an exam, on the back of a flashcard, and under each Organic Chemistry question. Pick what is wrong from four options, add a sentence if you want to, send. No account needed and nothing about you is recorded with it. Sources promised a way to do this from the day it was written and never actually gave one; the only address on the site was at the bottom of the privacy policy.
  • BothThe site can remind you when something is due. One notification a day, at a time you pick, and only when you genuinely have questions waiting or a streak about to lapse — it stops by itself after three unanswered days. Off unless you turn it on, in Privacy. The number in it is worked out in your browser; we are told a number and a sentence, not what you have been studying.
  • SiteYou can delete your account yourself, from the account menu. It used to be an email request. The same screen offers you a backup file first and can erase this browser’s copy too, since your progress lives in both places and almost nobody expects that.
  • OchemA search page for the whole course — the textbook, all 58 lessons, the mechanism walkthroughs, the seven tools and every practice question, in one box. The textbook rail could already search its own prose, but nothing could find a lesson, a mechanism or a tool, so working out where something was explained meant guessing.
  • SiteSeveral colors were hard to read and now are not. The green used for small labels and buttons failed the contrast standard for text, as did the amber in the “watch out for this” boxes in the Organic Chemistry notes — which were, by some distance, the least readable thing on the site. Both are darker now.
  • OchemThe 3D figures in the textbook carried 208 keyboard stops that did nothing — the atoms are clickable in the tools, and the textbook was reusing pictures of them. Tabbing through a chapter now goes where you would expect, and the wide figures can be scrolled from the keyboard.
  • SiteThe privacy policy was wrong about two things, and is not now. It still named Google Fonts as serving the typefaces and receiving your IP address with every page — they have been served from this site for some time, and no font service is contacted at all. It also undersold signing in, saying it carried only your XP and streak: it carries the questions you have seen, what you missed on a timed exam, your mastery, your per-domain statistics and the textbook sections you have read. A page whose only value is being true is a bad page to leave stale.
  • OchemFour pages linked to the amines lesson with the label “Reactions”, which says nothing when you meet it at the bottom of a mechanism about something else. It reads “Amine reactions” now, fixed in the curriculum the pages are built from rather than one page at a time.
  • NREMTThe line under the heading on What the exam is like had been half-edited into nonsense — the same sentence twice, one paragraph opened inside another, and a stray mdash; where an em dash was meant.
  • SiteCorrections to questions are the point of the report link above; when one is made it is listed here, dated, as it always has been.

Coming back is easier than it was 14 September 2026

  • BothA streak now survives one bad day. Study seven days in a row and you earn a streak freeze: miss a single day and the run keeps going. You hold one at a time, and it is only spent when you come back and study — opening a page never uses it up.
  • BothThe daily goal eases off after a rough patch. Miss a couple of days and it halves, so coming back is possible rather than hopeless, then returns to your own number once you are studying again. If you set the goal yourself, it is never moved.
  • NREMTThe home page now leads with whatever is waiting for you — an exam you left half-finished, or the questions your own answers say are slipping — and takes you straight into it instead of onto the start screen. The review queue was always there; it was two screens in and easy to miss.
  • OchemWhen something is genuinely due for review, it is now the main button on the home page and the lesson steps back. A queue that keeps waiting is a queue of things you are in the middle of forgetting; a half-finished lesson is not.
  • BothAdding the site to your home screen works properly on iPhone. The icon was an SVG, which iOS quietly refuses, so an installed copy showed a screenshot of whatever page you happened to be on. There are real icons now, and ochem and the landing page can be installed too — not just NREMT.
  • BothThere is now a prompt offering to save your progress to an account, shown after a level-up or a finished exam rather than on arrival. Everything still works without one; the point is that progress kept only in a browser disappears when you clear it or switch phones, and nothing used to warn you.
  • SiteThe site now records, once a day at most, whether this browser has been back before — as a coarse band rather than a date, with the dates behind it never leaving your browser. Privacy says exactly what is sent, and the existing off switch covers it.

Seven corrections to the notes 14 September 2026

  • NREMTPediatric Rule of Nines. The figures given for a child omitted the arms and added up to 82%. They now read as they do on the mnemonics card — head and neck 18%, each leg 13.5%, everything else as in an adult — and total 100%.
  • NREMTScoop stretcher. The carrying-device table said it was not for a suspected spine injury. The joint position statement of the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma, the American College of Emergency Physicians and the National Association of EMS Physicians lists the scoop stretcher as a device for achieving spinal motion restriction, so the table now says so.
  • NREMTAnaphylaxis in infants. The notes said infants seldom have true anaphylaxis because the immune system is immature. That is not right: it happens, it is missed more often than it is absent, and epinephrine is under-used in that age group. Age is never a reason to withhold it.
  • NREMTHIV post-exposure prophylaxis. The 28-day course was described without its time window. It has to start as soon as possible, ideally within hours and never later than 72 hours.
  • NREMTThe midaxillary line ran “down to the ankle”. It is a landmark of the torso: down from the armpit, over the side of the chest and along the flank.
  • NREMTSTART triage. Red was a respiratory rate above 30 and yellow a rate below 30, which left a rate of exactly 30 in neither category. Yellow is now 30 or less.
  • NREMTA soft-tissue key point contradicted itself on cooling an amputated part, and a chapter heading read “gastrourinary”. Both are fixed.

The notes are written as a textbook 14 September 2026

  • NREMTEvery one of the forty chapters has been rewritten as prose. The notes were largely "term — fragment" bullets and arrow chains; they are now paragraphs that explain, in the register of a course textbook. Every fact, figure, name and mnemonic is unchanged, and the tables are untouched. Lists remain where a list is the honest form: the steps of a procedure, a set of criteria, named categories, the medications you carry.
