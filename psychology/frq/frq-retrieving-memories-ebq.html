Study

Source 1: Testing versus restudying a science passage

Introduction

Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke asked whether taking a test on a passage, with no feedback, helps students remember it better than studying it again.

Participants

120 undergraduates, ages 18 to 24, at Washington University in St. Louis, Missouri.

Method

Each student read two short science passages. For one passage, the student studied it for 7 minutes and then studied it again for 7 minutes. For the other, the student studied it for 7 minutes and then spent 7 minutes writing down as much as they could recall, with no feedback. Which passage got which treatment was balanced across students. Students were assigned to take a final recall test 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week later, scored as the percent of the passage's ideas recalled.

Results

After 5 minutes, students recalled 81% of the ideas from the restudied passage and 75% from the tested passage. After 2 days: 54% restudied, 68% tested. After 1 week: 42% restudied, 56% tested.

Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255.

Study

Source 2: Ungraded quizzes in high school biology

Introduction

Researchers tested whether short, ungraded quizzes in class improve end-of-unit test scores in a high school biology course.

Participants

96 tenth graders in four biology classes taught by the same teacher at a public high school in Ohio. Parents gave permission and students agreed to take part.

Method

For one unit, each class was randomly assigned to a review method for the last 10 minutes of every lesson. Two classes (48 students) took a 5-question ungraded quiz on the day's material and then saw the answers. Two classes (48 students) reread a summary of the same material. Three weeks after the unit ended, all students took the same 40-question unit test.

Results

Students in the quiz classes answered 79% of the unit test correctly on average; students in the reread classes answered 64%.

Source 3: What students predict and what they remember

Introduction

Researchers asked whether students can tell which study method works better for them.

Participants

60 first-year college students at a university in Oregon, ages 18 to 20, who gave informed consent.

Method

Each student learned 40 Swahili-English word pairs in two ways, 20 pairs each: one set by reading the pairs four times, the other by reading the pairs once and then testing themselves three times by covering the English word. Right after studying, students predicted what percent of each set they would remember a week later. One week later they were tested on all 40 pairs.

Results

Students predicted they would remember 70% of the pairs they reread and 55% of the pairs they tested themselves on. One week later they actually recalled 30% of the reread pairs and 61% of the self-tested pairs.
