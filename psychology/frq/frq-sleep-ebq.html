Study

Source 1: Sleep after learning

Introduction

Researchers tested whether a night of sleep after learning helps people keep what they learned.

Participants

48 university students aged 18 to 22 in one US city, who reported no sleep problems. All gave informed consent and were paid.

Method

At 9 p.m. every student studied 40 pairs of unrelated words (such as "river - candle") until they could recall at least 34. Students were randomly assigned to sleep normally in the lab (sleep group) or to stay awake all night under supervision (sleep-deprived group). Both groups then had a normal night of sleep the next night. Three days after learning, all students saw the first word of each pair and wrote the second.

Results

The sleep group recalled an average of 31 of 40 pairs; the sleep-deprived group recalled an average of 24. A difference this large would be very unlikely if sleep made no difference.

Invented study for practice.

Study

Source 2: Sleep and school grades

Introduction

Researchers asked whether high school students who sleep more on school nights earn higher grades.

Participants

600 students in grades 9 to 12 at four public high schools in one US state. Parents gave permission and students agreed to take part.

Method

Students wore a sleep tracker for four school weeks, giving their average sleep on school nights. Their end-of-term grade averages (on a 0 to 4 scale) came from school records with names removed.

Results

Students who slept more had higher grades. Students who averaged under 6 hours had an average grade of 2.6; 6 to 8 hours, 3.0; more than 8 hours, 3.3. The correlation between hours of sleep and grades was r=.32r = .32.

Study

Source 3: Short nights and learning new material

Introduction

Researchers asked whether several short nights make it harder to take in new material the next day.

Participants

40 high school students aged 15 to 17 at a two-week summer program at a boarding school. Parents gave permission and each student agreed to take part.

Method

Students were randomly assigned to 9 hours in bed or 5 hours in bed each night for five nights. Each afternoon they watched a 20-minute science lesson and then took a 20-question quiz on it. They also did a reaction-time task in which they pressed a key when a dot appeared; lapses were responses slower than half a second.

Results

By the fifth day, the 9-hour group averaged 15 of 20 on the quiz and the 5-hour group 11 of 20. The 5-hour group had about three times as many attention lapses. Their quiz scores were lowest on the days they had the most lapses.
