Unit 2 · Topic 2.8

Intelligence and Achievement

Intelligence tests measure a general ability (g) that shows up across many tasks, though some theories stress separate abilities.

Practice 1: Concept ApplicationPractice 2: Research Methods and DesignPractice 3: Data Interpretation

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Two students get the same IQ score. One was told before the test that "people like you usually do badly on this." Does the score mean the same thing for both? Intelligence tests are carefully built, widely used and, at times in history, badly misused. Reading a score well takes knowing how they work.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Scores on very different mental tests correlate→many researchers propose a general intelligence, g
  2. A test is standardized on a representative sample→scores can be placed on a normal curve and read as percentiles
  3. A test is reliable and valid→its scores are consistent and predict what they should
  4. Tests are given in biased ways or used to rank groups→scores are misread, as in the history of testing immigrants and the misuse of group differences

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • General intelligence (g) explains why different tests correlate; multiple-ability views stress separate strengths.
  • The original IQ was mental agechronological age×100\frac{\text{mental age}}{\text{chronological age}} \times 100. Modern tests are standardized: a normal curve, mean 100, SD 15.
  • A good test is reliable (test-retest, split-half: consistent) and valid (construct, predictive: measures what it should).
  • Scores are shaped by context: stereotype threat and lift, the Flynn effect, and the fact that within-group differences do not explain between-group gaps. Tests have been misused against immigrants and minority groups.
  • Achievement tests measure what you learned; aptitude tests predict what you can learn. A growth mindset sees ability as developable; a fixed mindset does not.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: If a trait is highly heritable within groups, then a difference between groups must be genetic too.

What actually happens: Within-group heritability says nothing about the cause of a gap between groups. Seeds from one bag grow taller in rich soil than in poor soil, though height within each field is mostly genetic.

4. Jonah's score is at the 84th percentile. What does that mean?

  1. He scored higher than about 84% of the norm group
  2. He answered about 84% of the test items correctly
  3. His IQ score is 84 points on this test
  4. He scored lower than about 84% of the norm group
Show the answer

A percentile rank is the percent of scores below a given score. The 84th percentile is one standard deviation above the mean, an IQ of 115.

  • Correct: He scored higher than about 84% of the norm group: Right: A percentile rank is the percent of scores below a given score. The 84th percentile is one standard deviation above the mean, an IQ of 115.
  • He answered about 84% of the test items correctly: A percentile compares a score with other people's scores; it is not the percent of items answered correctly.
  • His IQ score is 84 points on this test: An IQ of 84 is about one standard deviation below the mean, near the 16th percentile.
  • He scored lower than about 84% of the norm group: That reverses it. The 84th percentile means 84% scored below him.

6. A college admissions test is given to applicants, and their scores correlate r = .45 with their first-year college grades. What property of the test does this correlation measure?

  1. Test-retest reliability
  2. Standardization
  3. Split-half reliability
  4. Predictive validity
Show the answer

Validity is whether a test measures or predicts what it is supposed to. A correlation between test scores and later grades measures predictive validity.

  • Test-retest reliability: Test-retest reliability compares the test with itself on two occasions, not with grades.
  • Standardization: Standardization is giving the test the same way and comparing to a norm group, not linking scores to grades.
  • Split-half reliability: Split-half reliability compares the two halves of the same test.
  • Correct: Predictive validity: Right: Validity is whether a test measures or predicts what it is supposed to. A correlation between test scores and later grades measures predictive validity.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Intelligence tests measure a general ability (g) that shows up across many tasks, though some theories stress separate abilities. Modern scores are standardized on a normal curve (mean 100, SD 15) and must be reliable and valid. Scores are shaped by context (stereotype threat and lift, the Flynn effect), group gaps are not explained by within-group heritability, tests have a history of misuse, and beliefs about ability (growth and fixed mindsets) affect effort.
