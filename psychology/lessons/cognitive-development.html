Unit 3 · Topic 3.4

Cognitive Development Across the Lifespan

Piaget described four stages of thinking, from object permanence in infancy, through the egocentrism and failed conservation of the preschool years, to logical and then abstract thought; research supports his order more than his ages.

Practice 1: Concept Application

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Pour juice from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin one, and a 4-year-old will often insist there is more juice now. An 8-year-old will roll her eyes: you only poured it. Same juice, same eyes. What changed between 4 and 8 is how the child thinks.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Children act on the world and meet things their schemas do not fit→they assimilate what fits and accommodate their schemas when it does not
  2. Infants learn through senses and actions→they develop object permanence by about their first birthday
  3. Preschoolers think with symbols but focus on one feature at a time→they play make-believe, but are egocentric and fail conservation
  4. School-age children reason logically about concrete things→they conserve; by adolescence many reason about abstract and hypothetical ideas
  5. Support from a more skilled person targets tasks in the zone of proximal development→children learn more than they could alone (Vygotsky)

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Piaget: children build understanding by assimilating new experiences and accommodating their schemas, moving through four stages.
  • Sensorimotor (birth to about 2): object permanence. Preoperational (about 2 to 7): make-believe play, egocentrism, no conservation. Concrete operational (7 to 11): logic about real things. Formal operational (12+): abstract thought.
  • Theory of mind, understanding that others have their own beliefs, usually appears around age 4 to 5.
  • Vygotsky: learning happens in the zone of proximal development with scaffolding from others. In adulthood, crystallized intelligence holds or grows while fluid intelligence declines; dementia is disease, not normal aging.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A 4-year-old who says the tall glass has more juice is not paying attention.

What actually happens: She is attending closely, to the height. Preoperational thinkers focus on one feature and do not yet mentally reverse the pour, so they do not conserve.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

Study

Conservation of liquid at ages 4, 6 and 8

Introduction

Researchers asked at what age children understand that pouring water into a differently shaped glass does not change how much water there is.

Participants

90 children from three public elementary schools in one city: 30 aged 4, 30 aged 6 and 30 aged 8 (about half girls). Parents gave informed consent, each child agreed to play "a water game," and children could stop at any time. Results were reported only as group totals.

Method

Each child saw two identical short glasses with the same amount of water and agreed they held the same amount. While the child watched, the researcher poured one glass into a tall, thin glass and asked, "Is there more water in this glass, more in that glass, or the same in both?" A child passed the task by saying "the same" and giving a reason, such as "You only poured it." Each child was tested once.

Results

The percentage of children who passed rose with age: 10% at age 4, 40% at 6 and 87% at 8. Most children who failed said the tall glass had more water.

Discussion

Understanding of conservation grew sharply between 4 and 8, but there was wide variation within each age group.

Invented study for practice.

1. Which research design did this study use?

  1. A cross-sectional study comparing age groups at one time
  2. A longitudinal study, following the same children from 4 to 8
  3. An experiment, randomly assigning children to ages 4, 6 and 8
  4. A case study of one child’s thinking
Show the answer

Different people of different ages, tested at the same time, is a cross-sectional design.

  • Correct: A cross-sectional study comparing age groups at one time: Right: three groups of different ages were each tested once.
  • A longitudinal study, following the same children from 4 to 8: Each child was tested once; the age groups were different children.
  • An experiment, randomly assigning children to ages 4, 6 and 8: Age cannot be randomly assigned.
  • A case study of one child’s thinking: The study tested 90 children on the same task.

2. Most 4-year-olds said the tall glass held more water. In Piaget’s terms, why?

  1. They lack object permanence and forget the poured water exists
  2. They are formal operational and reason about hypotheses
  3. They have no theory of mind about the researcher
  4. They are preoperational: they focus on height alone
Show the answer

Conservation, the idea that amount stays the same when shape changes, marks the move from preoperational to concrete operational thought.

  • They lack object permanence and forget the poured water exists: They watched the water and talked about it, so they know it exists.
  • They are formal operational and reason about hypotheses: Formal operational thinking comes around adolescence and would conserve easily.
  • They have no theory of mind about the researcher: The task asks about amounts of water, not about what another person thinks.
  • Correct: They are preoperational: they focus on height alone: Right: preoperational children center on one feature and do not yet conserve.

5. Maria can do long division only when her tutor breaks each problem into steps and gives hints; without help she gets stuck. In Vygotsky’s terms, what are long-division problems for Maria right now?

  1. In her zone of proximal development, with scaffolding
  2. Beyond her ability, so she is not ready to learn them
  3. A sign she is still in the sensorimotor stage
  4. Something she has mastered on her own
Show the answer

Vygotsky stressed that learning moves forward through guided help from more skilled people.

  • Correct: In her zone of proximal development, with scaffolding: Right: tasks she can do with help but not alone are in her ZPD, and support that is later removed is scaffolding.
  • Beyond her ability, so she is not ready to learn them: She can do them with help, which is exactly where Vygotsky said learning happens.
  • A sign she is still in the sensorimotor stage: Long division is far beyond infancy; this is about social support, not Piaget’s stages.
  • Something she has mastered on her own: She cannot yet do them without help.

6. A puppet named Sally puts a ball in a basket and leaves. Anne moves it to a box. A child is asked where Sally will look for the ball. Most 3-year-olds say "the box"; most 5-year-olds say "the basket." What has the older child developed?

  1. Object permanence: knowing the ball still exists
  2. Conservation: knowing amounts stay the same
  3. Crystallized intelligence: stored knowledge
  4. Theory of mind: others can hold false beliefs
Show the answer

Passing a false-belief task is a classic sign of theory of mind, typically around age 4 to 5.

  • Object permanence: knowing the ball still exists: Both ages know the ball exists; the difference is understanding Sally’s belief.
  • Conservation: knowing amounts stay the same: Nothing changed shape or amount.
  • Crystallized intelligence: stored knowledge: This is a skill of reading minds, not stored facts.
  • Correct: Theory of mind: others can hold false beliefs: Right: the 5-year-old knows Sally did not see the move and so believes something untrue.

7. Ruth, 80, forgets where she put her glasses and needs longer to learn a new phone. Her neighbor, 78, gets lost on his daily walk and no longer recognizes his son. Which comparison is most accurate?

  1. Both show normal aging, which includes not recognizing family
  2. Ruth shows dementia because she is older than her neighbor
  3. Ruth: normal aging; her neighbor: likely dementia
  4. Both show dementia, which is a normal part of aging
Show the answer

Dementia is a decline in memory and thinking severe enough to disrupt daily life, most often from Alzheimer’s disease.

  • Both show normal aging, which includes not recognizing family: Failing to recognize close family is not normal aging.
  • Ruth shows dementia because she is older than her neighbor: Age alone does not make a diagnosis; the kind of change matters.
  • Correct: Ruth: normal aging; her neighbor: likely dementia: Right: mild slowing is normal; losing the way home and failing to recognize family point to disease.
  • Both show dementia, which is a normal part of aging: Dementia is caused by disease and is not a normal part of aging.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Piaget described four stages of thinking, from object permanence in infancy, through the egocentrism and failed conservation of the preschool years, to logical and then abstract thought; research supports his order more than his ages. Vygotsky added that learning moves forward with scaffolding in the zone of proximal development. In adulthood, crystallized intelligence holds while fluid intelligence slowly declines, and dementia is a disease, not normal aging.
