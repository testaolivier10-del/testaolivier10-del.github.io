Unit 2 · Topic 2.6

Retrieving Memories

Retrieval depends on cues.

Practice 1: Concept ApplicationPractice 2: Research Methods and Design

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

You walk into the kitchen and forget why. You go back to the couch, and it comes back. A word sits on the tip of your tongue until a friend says the first letter. Memories are often stored but stuck. Retrieval is about finding the right cue.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A memory is stored with features of the setting, body state and mood→those features become retrieval cues
  2. The same cues are present at the test→retrieval is easier (context-dependent, state-dependent and mood-congruent memory)
  3. Students practice pulling information out of memory→retrieval routes get stronger (the testing effect)
  4. Rereading makes notes feel familiar→students overestimate what they know, a failure of metacognition

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Recall produces information with few cues; recognition picks it from options, which act as retrieval cues, so it is usually easier.
  • Retrieval is easier when cues match learning: the place (context-dependent), the body's state (state-dependent), and the mood (mood-congruent).
  • Retrieval practice (self-quizzing) beats rereading for long-term memory: the testing effect.
  • Metacognition is judging your own learning. Familiarity from rereading feels like knowing but is not; testing yourself is the honest check.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Rereading notes and highlighting until they feel familiar is the best way to study.

What actually happens: Familiarity is not memory. Testing yourself, with the notes closed, builds far stronger long-term memory and shows you what you do not know yet.

A teacher randomly assigned 90 ninth graders to review a science lesson in one of two ways for the same 20 minutes. Just before the unit test, each student predicted what percent they would score.

1. Which concept do the test scores show?

  1. Context-dependent memory
  2. Mood-congruent memory
  3. Infantile amnesia
  4. The testing effect
Show the answer

Students who practiced pulling the information out of memory (quizzes) scored higher than students who reread, after the same review time. That is the testing effect.

  • Context-dependent memory: Both groups took the test in the same setting. The difference is in how they reviewed.
  • Mood-congruent memory: Mood was not measured or changed.
  • Infantile amnesia: Infantile amnesia is the lack of memories from early childhood, unrelated to how teenagers study.
  • Correct: The testing effect: Right: Students who practiced pulling the information out of memory (quizzes) scored higher than students who reread, after the same review time. That is the testing effect.

3. The rereading group predicted a higher score than the quiz group but actually scored lower. Which concept best explains the mistaken predictions?

  1. Poor metacognition: rereading feels familiar, which students mistake for knowing
  2. State-dependent memory: the students were in a different body state when predicting
  3. Mood-congruent memory: the rereaders felt more cheerful, so they predicted high
  4. Recognition: students recognized the notes, so their predictions were accurate
Show the answer

Metacognition is judging your own learning. Rereading makes material feel familiar, so students think they know it; quizzing exposes gaps, so it feels harder even though it works better.

  • Correct: Poor metacognition: rereading feels familiar, which students mistake for knowing: Right: Metacognition is judging your own learning. Rereading makes material feel familiar, so students think they know it; quizzing exposes gaps, so it feels harder even though it works better.
  • State-dependent memory: the students were in a different body state when predicting: Nothing about the students' body state differed between groups.
  • Mood-congruent memory: the rereaders felt more cheerful, so they predicted high: Mood was neither changed nor measured. The rereaders misjudged how well they knew the material, which is a judgment about their own learning.
  • Recognition: students recognized the notes, so their predictions were accurate: Recognizing the notes is exactly what misled the rereaders; their predictions were too high.

4. On a test, Grace cannot answer "Name the four lobes of the cerebral cortex," but on a later question she easily picks the occipital lobe from a list of options. Which concept explains the difference?

  1. Context-dependent memory
  2. Recognition is easier than recall
  3. Infantile amnesia
  4. Maintenance rehearsal
Show the answer

Recall means producing information with few cues; recognition means identifying it among options, which act as retrieval cues. Recognition usually succeeds where recall fails.

  • Context-dependent memory: Both questions were on the same test in the same room, so the context did not change.
  • Correct: Recognition is easier than recall: Right: Recall means producing information with few cues; recognition means identifying it among options, which act as retrieval cues. Recognition usually succeeds where recall fails.
  • Infantile amnesia: Infantile amnesia is about early childhood memories, not test questions.
  • Maintenance rehearsal: Maintenance rehearsal is repeating information to hold it. It does not explain why options help.

5. Walking into the kitchen, Tom forgets why he came. He goes back to the couch where he had the thought, and it comes back to him. Which concept explains this?

  1. State-dependent memory
  2. Mood-congruent memory
  3. Context-dependent memory
  4. Recognition
Show the answer

Returning to the place where the thought was formed brought back cues from that context, which helped him retrieve it.

  • State-dependent memory: State-dependent memory depends on an internal state (calm, sleepy, under a drug). Tom changed his location.
  • Mood-congruent memory: Mood-congruent memory is recalling memories that fit your mood. Tom's mood is not part of the story.
  • Correct: Context-dependent memory: Right: Returning to the place where the thought was formed brought back cues from that context, which helped him retrieve it.
  • Recognition: Recognition is picking the right answer from options. Tom produced the thought himself once the cues returned.

6. A friend shows Ben a photo of the beach where they vacationed, and a flood of details about the trip come back. In this example the photo acts as what?

  1. A sensory memory
  2. Maintenance rehearsal
  3. A retrieval cue
  4. Mood-congruent memory
Show the answer

A retrieval cue is any stimulus that helps bring a stored memory back. The photo connects to the stored memory of the trip.

  • A sensory memory: Sensory memory lasts seconds. The photo brings back a memory stored years ago.
  • Maintenance rehearsal: Maintenance rehearsal is repeating information to hold it. Seeing a photo is not repeating.
  • Correct: A retrieval cue: Right: A retrieval cue is any stimulus that helps bring a stored memory back. The photo connects to the stored memory of the trip.
  • Mood-congruent memory: Mood-congruent memory is recall that matches a mood. The photo is an external cue, not a mood.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Retrieval depends on cues. Recognition is usually easier than recall because the options are cues. Matching the place, body state or mood of learning helps retrieval. Practicing retrieval (the testing effect) strengthens memory more than rereading, and good metacognition means checking what you know by testing yourself instead of trusting familiarity.
