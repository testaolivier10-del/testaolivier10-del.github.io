Treatments for psychological disorders fall into two broad groups. Psychotherapies work through conversation, learning and practice with a trained professional. Biomedical therapies change the brain's chemistry or activity directly. Each approach grew out of a perspective on what causes disorders, so the fastest way to learn them is to ask: what does this approach think the problem is, and how does it go after it? The second question, just as important, is what the evidence says. Treatment is very often effective, and asking for help is a sign of strength.

Psychodynamic and humanistic therapies

Psychodynamic therapy assumes symptoms grow from unconscious conflicts, often rooted in early relationships. The goal is insight. In free association the client says whatever comes to mind, and the therapist listens for patterns; in dream interpretation they look for hidden meaning in dreams. Modern psychodynamic therapy is shorter and more face-to-face than Freud's original psychoanalysis, and some forms have research support, though less than CBT for most disorders.

Humanistic therapy assumes people have a natural drive to grow, blocked when they feel accepted only under conditions. In person-centered therapy, the therapist is genuine, shows empathy through active listening (restating and clarifying the client's words and feelings) and offers unconditional positive regard. The client leads; the therapist does not diagnose or direct.

Behavioral therapies

Behavioral therapies treat problem behaviors as learned, so they can be unlearned with the same conditioning principles you met in Unit 3.

TechniqueLearning usedHow it works
Exposure therapyclassical (extinction)face the feared object or situation, in real life or in imagination, until the fear fades because nothing bad happens
Systematic desensitizationclassical (counterconditioning)learn relaxation, then pair it with a ranked list of feared scenes, from least to most frightening
Aversion therapyclassicalpair an unwanted behavior (drinking) with something unpleasant (nausea) so the behavior brings discomfort
Token economyoperant (secondary reinforcers)earn tokens for target behaviors and trade them for rewards
Biofeedbackoperant (feedback)watch a live signal of a body process, such as muscle tension, and learn to change it

Exposure-based treatments are among the best-supported treatments for phobias, panic disorder and OCD (where the person also stops doing the ritual, called exposure and response prevention). Aversion therapy's effects often fade outside the clinic, so it is used rarely and with other supports.

Cognitive and cognitive-behavioral therapies

Cognitive therapies assume that how we interpret events shapes how we feel. In depression, a common target is the cognitive triad: negative views of oneself, the world and the future. Cognitive restructuring teaches a person to catch a distorted thought, test it against evidence and replace it with a more accurate one. Rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT) does this head on, disputing irrational "must" beliefs such as "I must be liked by everyone."

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) combines changing thoughts with changing behavior, with homework between sessions. It has the broadest research support of any psychotherapy, especially for depression and anxiety disorders. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) is a form of CBT that balances acceptance with change and teaches mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and relationship skills. It was developed for people with intense emotions and self-harm, including many with borderline personality disorder.

Worked example: name the therapy from the therapist's words. A therapist says, "What is the evidence for the thought that you ruin everything? What would you tell a friend who said that?"

Step 1. What is being targeted? A thought, "I ruin everything."

Step 2. What is the therapist doing to it? Testing it against evidence, not reflecting feelings (humanistic) or searching for its unconscious roots (psychodynamic).

Step 3. Name it and apply it. "This is cognitive therapy: the therapist uses cognitive restructuring, helping the client test the distorted belief that he ruins everything and replace it with a more accurate one."

Formats and the things every therapy needs

Therapy can be individual or in a group. Group therapy costs less per person, shows people they are not alone, and lets them practice new ways of relating; family therapy treats the family as a system, so patterns between members can change. Across all approaches, one of the most consistent predictors of improvement is the therapeutic alliance: trust between therapist and client, with shared goals. Cultural humility, a therapist's ongoing effort to learn about each client's culture and admit the limits of their own view, strengthens that alliance.

Good treatment is evidence-based practice: the best research (often meta-analyses that combine many studies), the clinician's expertise, and the client's culture, values and preferences, together. Ethical principles apply throughout: informed consent to treatment, confidentiality (with narrow exceptions, such as a serious risk of harm, explained at the start), avoiding harm, and competence.

History matters here too. Starting in the 1950s, deinstitutionalization moved many patients out of large state hospitals into the community, helped by new antipsychotic drugs. Community services were never fully funded, and many people were left without enough care.

For depression, combining medication with psychotherapy tends to work better than either alone, and for depression and several anxiety disorders the gains from CBT tend to last longer after treatment ends than the gains from medication alone.

Myth check. Hypnosis does not recover accurate memories or take people back to childhood; it raises confidence in "memories" while adding false ones. It can help with pain and anxiety. And the lobotomy, which cut connections to the prefrontal cortex in the 1940s and 1950s and left many people withdrawn and impaired, is history, not treatment.

Reading a treatment study

To show that a treatment works, researchers randomly assign people with the same diagnosis to the treatment or to a control, such as a waitlist (treatment is offered after the study). The control matters because symptoms often ease somewhat over time and because expecting help can help. If the treatment group improves significantly more than the control, the treatment probably caused the extra improvement. If two treatments differ by an amount that is not statistically significant, the difference could plausibly be due to chance, so the study cannot say one is better. Ethical treatment studies describe informed consent, the right to withdraw and review by an institutional review board.
