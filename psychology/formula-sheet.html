Statistics you must know

The exam gives you no formula sheet and no calculator. These are the statistics it asks you to work out by hand or read from a study, in our own words: what each means, when to use it, and the trap that costs points.

Three habits that save points

  • Interpret, do not define. "The mean is the average" earns nothing; "the group that slept 8 hours recalled 5 more words on average" does.
  • Name the direction. Say which group was higher, or whether r is positive or negative, and what that means for these participants.
  • Correlation is not causation. Only an experiment with random assignment supports a cause-and-effect claim.

Ideas to read off a study

  • Skew. In a positively skewed distribution (a long tail to the right) the mean is pulled above the median; in a negatively skewed one, below it. Descriptive Statistics
  • Scatterplots. Points rising left to right show a positive correlation; falling, a negative one; a round cloud, little or none. Correlation and Scatterplots
  • Effect size. How large a difference or relationship is, separate from whether it is statistically significant. A big sample can make a tiny effect significant. Statistical Significance and Effect Size
  • Read the graph first. On the article analysis question, the statistic part asks what a difference between groups means for these participants. Name the groups, the direction and what it shows. Statistical Significance and Effect Size

On exam day

  • No calculator. The AP® Psychology exam allows no calculator. Every number it asks for (a mean, a median, a range, a share under the normal curve) can be worked out by hand.
  • No formula sheet. Nothing on this page is printed in Bluebook: know what each statistic means and how to read it from a table or graph.
