3.1 Estimators

A statistic used to estimate a parameter is an estimator, and its value from one sample is a point estimate. Judge an estimator by its sampling distribution: it is unbiased when that distribution is centered at the parameter, and it has low variability when the distribution is narrow. Random selection removes bias; larger random samples reduce variability. Neither fixes the other.

  • An estimator is a statistic used to estimate a parameter: p^\hat{p} for pp, x¯\bar{x} for μ\mu. One sample's value is a point estimate.
  • Bias is about the center: an unbiased estimator has a sampling distribution centered at the parameter.
  • Variability is about the spread of the sampling distribution. For random samples, larger samples give less variability.
  • Bias comes from the method, so a larger sample does not fix it. Variability comes from sample size, so a better method alone does not fix it.
  • The best estimator has no bias and low variability: random selection plus a large enough sample.

a statistic such as p-hat is used as an estimator of the parameter, and its value is a point estimate → the statistic has a sampling distribution, with a center and a spread → the estimator is unbiased: it is right on average, though single samples still miss → the spread of the sampling distribution shrinks, so a typical estimate lands closer to the parameter → the center moves away from the parameter, and a bigger sample of the same kind does not move it back

3.2 Sampling Distributions for Sample Proportions

For random samples, p-hat is centered at p with a spread that shrinks as n grows. It has mean p and standard deviation √(p(1 − p)/n) (accurate when the sample is at most 10% of the population) and is approximately normal when np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10 . Then z = (p-hat − p)/(σ_(p-hat)) turns a question about a sample proportion into a normal probability, which you can tie back to a claim about p .

  • μp^=p\mu_{\hat{p}} = p and σp^\sigma_{\hat{p}}=p(1−p)n= \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}, using the true pp.
  • 10% condition: nn at most 10% of the population, so the standard deviation formula holds when sampling without replacement.
  • Large counts condition: np≥10np \ge 10 and n(1−p)≥10n(1 - p) \ge 10, so the shape is approximately normal. Never "n≥30n \ge 30" for proportions.
  • When large counts fails, the distribution is skewed: right when pp is near 0, left when pp is near 1.
  • Four times the sample size halves σp^\sigma_{\hat{p}}.

3.3 Constructing a Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion

A confidence interval for p is p-hat ± z*√(p-hat(1 − p-hat)/n) : the point estimate plus or minus a margin of error. Check random, 10% and large counts (sample successes and failures at least 10) in words first. Larger samples shrink the margin of error; higher confidence levels stretch it.

  • Interval = statistic ± (critical value)(standard error). For one proportion: p^±z∗p^(1−p^)n\hat{p} \pm z^*\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}.
  • z∗z^*: 1.645 for 90%, 1.96 for 95%, 2.576 for 99%. Area below z∗z^* is C+1−C2C + \frac{1 - C}{2}.
  • Conditions in words: random sample; nn at most 10% of the population; at least 10 successes and 10 failures in the sample.
  • Margin of error =z∗⋅SE= z^* \cdot \text{SE}. It shrinks with a larger nn (4 times nn halves it) and grows with the confidence level.
  • Name the procedure in full: one-sample z interval for a proportion.

in 95% of random samples, p-hat lands within 1.96 standard deviations of p → in 95% of samples, p is within 1.96 standard deviations of p-hat → we use the standard error √(p-hat(1 − p-hat)/n) in its place → p-hat ± z*√((p-hat(1 − p-hat))/n) captures p for the stated share of samples

3.4 Justifying a Claim Based on a Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion

Interpret an interval with its level, its ends and the parameter in context; interpret the level as the long-run capture rate of the method. Values inside the interval are plausible. An interval gives convincing evidence for a claim like "greater than 0.60" only when every value in it is greater than 0.60; a claimed value inside the interval is plausible, which is not the same as proven.

  • Interval: "We are 95% confident that the interval from __ to __ captures the true proportion of all __."
  • Level: "If we took many random samples of this size and built a 95% interval from each, about 95% of the intervals would capture the true proportion."
  • Values inside the interval are plausible. A claim is supported (convincing evidence) only when the whole interval is on the claimed side.
  • Never: "95% probability that pp is in the interval", "95% of the data", or anything about the sample proportion.
  • Higher confidence gives a longer interval, so more values stay plausible.

each sample gives its own interval, while the true proportion stays fixed → we say we are 95% confident in one interval, because we cannot tell whether it is one of the misses → it is a plausible value, so the interval gives no convincing evidence against it (and does not prove it) → that value is not plausible, so the interval gives convincing evidence that the parameter is greater (or less) than it

plausible value
A value inside a confidence interval: the data are consistent with it as the parameter's value. A plausible value is not proven; values outside the interval are not plausible at that confidence level.
convincing evidence
The wording for a conclusion that the data support a claim about a parameter, as in "the interval gives convincing evidence that the true proportion is greater than 0.60". Evidence is never proof.

3.5 Setting Up a Test for a Population Proportion

Set up a test before looking at the data. Write hypotheses about p ( H_0:p = p_0 and a one-sided or two-sided H_a chosen from the question), p defined in context, a significance level α , the procedure named (one-sample z test for a proportion), and the conditions checked in words, with large counts using p_0 .

  • H0:p=p0H_0: p = p_0 always has the equals sign; HaH_a is p<p0p < p_0, p>p0p > p_0 or p≠p0p \ne p_0.
  • Hypotheses are about the parameter pp, never p^\hat{p}. Define pp in context right after them.
  • The direction of HaH_a comes from the question, before the data: never from which way the sample went.
  • α\alpha is the standard of evidence, set in advance. Smaller α\alpha, stricter standard.
  • Procedure: one-sample z test for a proportion. Conditions: random, 10%, large counts with p0p_0.

3.6 p-Values

A p-value measures how surprising the data are if H_0 is true. Assuming H_0 is true, standardize the sample proportion with z = (p-hat − p_0)/(√(p_0(1 − p_0)/n)) and find the tail area in the direction of H_a (both tails for ≠ ). That area is the p-value: the chance of a result at least as extreme as the one observed if H_0 is true. Small p-values are evidence against H_0 ; they are never the probability that H_0 is true.

  • Test statistic: zz=p^−p0p0(1−p0)/n= \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n}}, with p0p_0 in the square root.
  • p-value: the probability, assuming H0H_0 is true, of a statistic at least as extreme as observed, in the direction of HaH_a. Two-sided: double one tail.
  • Interpret: "Assuming the true proportion of all __ is p0p_0, there is a __ probability of getting a sample proportion of __ or less (more) by chance in a random sample of __."
  • A simulation estimates a p-value: the share of simulated results, made assuming H0H_0, at least as extreme as the real one.
  • Not the probability that H0H_0 is true. A large p-value does not prove H0H_0.

3.7 Carrying Out a Test for a Population Proportion

A complete test runs state, plan, do, conclude. It states hypotheses about p with α , names the one-sample z test for a proportion and checks random, 10% and large counts with p_0 , computes z and the p-value, then concludes: compare the p-value with α , reject or fail to reject H_0 , and say whether there is convincing evidence for H_a about the true proportion of all ..., in context. Never accept H_0 , and claim cause only from a randomized experiment.

  • Four steps: State (H0H_0, HaH_a, α\alpha, pp defined), Plan (procedure, conditions), Do (zz, p-value), Conclude.
  • Conclusion: "Because the p-value of __ is less than α\alpha = __, we reject H0H_0. There is convincing evidence that the true proportion of all __ is __ than __."
  • Fail to reject: "there is not convincing evidence that ...", still naming the direction of HaH_a. Never "accept H0H_0" or "prove".
  • A two-sided test at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05 almost always rejects when p0p_0 is outside the 95% interval, and fails to reject when it is inside.
  • Random sampling lets you generalize; only random assignment lets you claim cause.

the result is too unusual under H_0 : reject H_0 , and there is convincing evidence for H_a → fail to reject H_0 : there is not convincing evidence for H_a (which does not make H_0 true) → the conclusion applies to the population that was sampled → the test can show a change but not what caused it

3.8 Potential Errors When Performing Tests

A Type I error rejects a true H_0 (probability α ); a Type II error fails to reject a false H_0 (probability β , against a specific alternative). Describe each with the condition first and its consequence in context. Power = 1 − β is the chance of detecting a real effect; it grows with the sample size, with α , and with the distance between the true value and p_0 .

  • Type I: H0H_0 true, but we reject it (a false alarm). Probability α\alpha.
  • Type II: HaH_a true, but we fail to reject H0H_0 (a missed effect). Probability β\beta, which depends on the true value.
  • Describe an error with the condition first: "if the true proportion of all __ is __, but we find (do not find) convincing evidence that ...", then the consequence in context.
  • Power =1−β= 1 - \beta: the probability of rejecting H0H_0 when a specific alternative is true.
  • More power: larger nn (best), larger α\alpha, or a true value farther from p0p_0. For a fixed nn, lowering α\alpha raises β\beta.

the test rejects H_0 : a Type I error, with probability α → the test fails to reject H_0 : a Type II error, with probability β → the test rejects H_0 with probability power = 1 − β → the curves overlap less or the cutoff is easier to pass, so power rises and β falls

Type I error
Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true: finding convincing evidence for an effect that is not there. Its probability is the significance level <math class="stat-m"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math>.
Type II error
Failing to reject the null hypothesis when the alternative is true: missing an effect that is there. Its probability, <math class="stat-m"><semantics><mrow><mi>β</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\beta</annotation></semantics></math>, depends on the true value of the parameter.

The probability that a test rejects the null hypothesis when a specific alternative value is true, <math class="stat-m"><semantics><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mi>β</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">1 - \beta</annotation></semantics></math>. It increases with the sample size, with <math class="stat-m"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math>, and with the distance between the true value and the null value.

3.9 Sampling Distributions for the Difference Between Sample Proportions

For two independent random samples, p-hat_1 − p-hat_2 is centered at p_1 − p_2 and has standard deviation √((p_1(1 − p_1))/(n_1) + (p_2(1 − p_2))/(n_2)) : the variances add. When all four large counts are at least 10, its distribution is approximately normal, so you can find probabilities with z-scores.

  • Mean: μp^1−p^2\mu_{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}=p1−p2= p_1 - p_2.
  • Standard deviation: σp^1−p^2\sigma_{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}=p1(1−p1)n1+p2(1−p2)n2= \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}. Add variances, never standard deviations.
  • Shape: approximately normal when n1p1n_1 p_1, n1(1−p1)n_1(1-p_1), n2p2n_2 p_2 and n2(1−p2)n_2(1-p_2) are each at least 10.
  • Conditions: two independent random samples (or two randomly assigned groups), and each sample less than 10% of its population when sampling without replacement.

each sample proportion has its own chance error, and so does their difference p-hat_1 − p-hat_2 → the difference is centered at p_1 − p_2 , so it is an unbiased estimator of the true difference → the variances add: σ² = (p_1(1 − p_1))/(n_1) +(p_2(1 − p_2))/(n_2) , and the standard deviation is its square root → the sampling distribution is approximately normal, so z-scores and normal areas give probabilities

To estimate p_1 − p_2 , use the two-sample z interval (p-hat_1 − p-hat_2) ± z*√((p-hat_1(1 − p-hat_1))/(n_1) + (p-hat_2(1 − p-hat_2))/(n_2)) after checking random, 10% and large counts for each sample. Interpret it as capturing the true difference in proportions, in context and with the order of subtraction named.

3.11 Justifying a Claim Based on a Confidence Interval for the Difference Between Two Population Proportions

To judge a claim with an interval for p_1 − p_2 , look at where 0, or the claimed value, sits. Entirely above or below 0 gives convincing evidence of a difference in that direction; an interval that contains 0 gives no convincing evidence of a difference, which is not proof of equality. State the reason, the evidence and the parameter in context.

  • Entirely above 0: convincing evidence p1>p2p_1 > p_2. Entirely below 0: convincing evidence p1<p2p_1 < p_2. Contains 0: no convincing evidence of a difference.
  • Write the claim with the reason first: "Because every value in the interval is greater than 0, the interval gives convincing evidence that ..."
  • A 95% interval and a two-sided test at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05 nearly always agree about whether 0 is plausible.
  • Containing 0 never proves the proportions are equal; very large samples can make even tiny differences convincing.

3.12 Setting Up a Test for the Difference Between Two Population Proportions

A two-sample z test for p_1 − p_2 starts from H_0:p_1 − p_2 = 0 and an alternative chosen from the question. Because the test assumes H_0 , it pools the samples, p_c = (x_1 + x_2)/(n_1 + n_2) , and checks large counts with p_c , along with two independent random samples (or random assignment) and the 10% condition.

  • Hypotheses: H0:p1−p2=0H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0 and Ha:p1−p2>0H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0, <0< 0 or ≠0\ne 0, with each pp defined as a true proportion in context.

3.13 Carrying Out a Test for the Difference Between Two Population Proportions

A two-sample z test standardizes the observed difference with the pooled standard error, z = (p-hat_1 − p-hat_2)/(√(p_c(1 − p_c)(1/(n_1) + 1/(n_2)))) , finds the p-value in the direction of H_a , compares it with α , and concludes about the true proportions in context, never accepting H_0 .

  • Test statistic: zz=p^1−p^2pc(1−pc)(1n1+1n2)= \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{p_c(1 - p_c)\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}, with pcp_c=x1+x2n1+n2= \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}.
  • p-value: area beyond zz in the direction of HaH_a; double one tail for a two-sided test.
  • Conclusion: "Because the p-value of ... is less than (greater than) α\alpha = ..., we reject (fail to reject) H0H_0. There is (is not) convincing evidence that the true proportion ..."
  • Reject: a Type I error is possible. Fail to reject: a Type II error is possible.

3.14 Setting Up a Chi-Square Test for Homogeneity or Independence

A chi-square test compares a two-way table of observed counts with the counts expected if H_0 were true. Choose independence (one sample, two variables) or homogeneity (several samples or groups, one variable), state H_0 and H_a in context, find each expected count as row total times column total over the grand total, and check random, 10% and expected counts at least 5.

  • Independence: one random sample, two categorical variables. H0H_0: no association between them in the population.
  • Homogeneity: separate random samples or randomly assigned groups, one categorical variable. H0H_0: the distribution is the same in every population.
  • Expected count =row total×column totalgrand total= \frac{\text{row total} \times \text{column total}}{\text{grand total}}; do not round expected counts.
  • Conditions: random; 10% for each sample; every expected count at least 5.

3.15 Carrying Out a Chi-Square Test for Homogeneity or Independence

Carrying out a chi-square test: find each expected count, add the components ((O − E)²)/E to get χ² , use the chi-square distribution with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom for the right-tail p-value, compare it with α , and conclude about the association or the distributions in context. The largest components show where the association lies.

  • χ2=∑(O−E)2E\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} over every cell; each term is a chi-square component.
  • Degrees of freedom: df\text{df}=(rows−1)(columns−1)= (\text{rows} - 1)(\text{columns} - 1).
  • The p-value is always the right-tail area: only large χ2\chi^2 values are evidence against H0H_0.
  • Conclude: "There is (is not) convincing evidence of an association between ... and ... for ..." (independence) or "that the distribution of ... differs among ..." (homogeneity).
