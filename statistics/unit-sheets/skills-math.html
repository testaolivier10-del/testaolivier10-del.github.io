Fractions, Decimals and Percents

A part of a whole is the part divided by the whole, a decimal from 0 to 1, and a percent is that decimal times 100. Pick the whole from the words of the question, and compare groups of different sizes with decimals or percents, never raw counts.

  • A part of a whole is the part divided by the whole: partwhole\frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}}. The result is a decimal from 0 to 1.
  • A percent is a decimal times 100: 0.375=37.5%0.375 = 37.5\%. To go back, divide by 100.
  • The whole is the group the question says "of": "what part of the sport players" tells you to divide by the number of sport players.
  • Compare groups of different sizes with decimals or percents, not counts.

that number goes on the bottom of the fraction → that number goes on top → the decimal is between 0 and 1; times 100 gives the percent

percent
A percent is a part out of 100: the decimal form of a part of a whole, multiplied by 100. For example, 0.75 is 75%.
part of a whole
A part of a whole is the count in one group divided by the count in the whole group it belongs to, giving a number from 0 to 1.

Formulas, Order of Operations and Square Roots

Evaluate a formula by substituting values, then following the order of operations: grouped terms first (brackets, fraction bars, roots), then powers, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. ∑ adds a list of terms, and a square root covers everything under it, so type brackets on a calculator.

  • Order of operations: brackets (and anything grouped by a fraction bar or root) first, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.
  • A square root a\sqrt{a} is the nonnegative number whose square is aa: 0.25=0.5\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5. Squares are never negative: (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9.
  • Summation: ∑xi\sum x_i says add every value; ∑(xi−5)2\sum (x_i - 5)^2 says subtract 5 from each value, square each result, then add.
  • Show the work the way the exam asks: the formula, the numbers substituted, then the answer.

substitute the numbers for the letters first → work out what is inside them before anything else → every reader of the formula gets the same answer → type it on a calculator with brackets around everything it covers

Inequalities in Words

Translate words into inequalities before computing: at least is ≥ , at most and no more than are ≤ , more than is > , fewer than is < . For counts, change strict inequalities to include the next whole number ( X < 3 is X ≤ 2 ), and list the included values to check.

  • At least kk: X≥kX \ge k. At most kk and no more than kk: X≤kX \le k. More than kk: X>kX > k. Fewer than kk: X<kX < k. No fewer than kk: X≥kX \ge k.
  • For counts, X<kX < k is X≤k−1X \le k - 1 and X>kX > k is X≥k+1X \ge k + 1: "fewer than 3" is 0, 1 or 2.
  • "At least one" is every count except 0.
  • For continuous measurements, whether the exact boundary is included makes almost no difference.

"at least 3" is X ≥ 3 , "fewer than 3" is X < 3 → "at least", "at most", "no more than" and "inclusive" include it; "more than" and "fewer than" do not → X < 3 becomes X ≤ 2 , and X > 4 becomes X ≥ 5 → the slip "fewer than 3 is 3 or fewer" shows up at once

Factorials and Combinations

A factorial n! multiplies n by each whole number below it and counts the orders of n items ( 0! = 1 ). A combination _nC_k = (n!)/(k! (n − k)!) counts the ways to choose k of n when order does not matter; it equals _nC_(n − k) .

  • A factorial n!=n×(n−1)×⋯×2×1n! = n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1 counts the orders of n different items; 0!=10! = 1.
  • A combination nCk{}_nC_k=(nk)= \binom{n}{k}=n!k!(n−k)!= \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!} counts the ways to choose k of n items when order does not matter.
  • Ordered choices overcount: 10×9×8=72010 \times 9 \times 8 = 720 counts each set of 3 six times, so 10C3=120{}_{10}C_3 = 120.
  • On a calculator or in Bluebook, type 10C3{}_{10}C_3 as 10C3 or nCr(10, 3).

there are n choices for the first place, n − 1 for the next, and so on: n! orders → n×(n − 1)×⋯ for k places, but each set of k appears k! times → _nC_k = (n!)/(k! (n − k)!) counts the sets of k → _nC_k = _nC_(n − k) , and _nC_0 = _nC_n = 1

Lines: Slope and Intercept

A line y = a + bx has slope b , the change in y for each one-unit increase in x (rise over run), and y-intercept a , the value of y when x = 0 . Interpret both in context with units, and the intercept only when x = 0 makes sense.

  • A line is y=a+bxy = a + bx. The slope bb is the change in y for each one-unit increase in x; its units are y units per x unit.
  • Slope from two points: bb=y2−y1x2−x1= \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}, rise over run, subtracting in the same order on top and bottom.
  • The y-intercept aa is the value of y when x=0x = 0. Interpret it only when x=0x = 0 makes sense.
  • In context: "For each additional hour, the cost increases by 3 dollars."

a is the y-intercept and b is the slope → y changes by b : up if b > 0 , down if b < 0 → y equals a , which has meaning only if x = 0 makes sense in context → slope = rise over run, then solve for a from one point
