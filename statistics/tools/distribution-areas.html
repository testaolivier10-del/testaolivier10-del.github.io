Distribution areas

Shade a region and read its probability, as the exam asks.

About this tool

A probability for a normal variable is an area under its curve; for a binomial count it is the total height of some bars. Drag the boundary, pick the direction, and read the probability. Each answer is written the way the exam wants it: the distribution, its parameters, the boundary and the direction.

How the areas are found

Every area is computed exactly: the normal one from the standard normal distribution after z=(x−μ)/σz = (x - \mu)/\sigma, the binomial one by adding P(X=k)P(X = k)=(nk)pk(1−p)n−k= \binom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n - k} over every count in the event. The shaded region is the region added up.

  • Table or technology. Table A gives the area to the left of zz rounded to two places. Its answer can differ from technology in the third or fourth decimal; both are accepted.
  • Words to symbols. "At most 3" is X≤3X \le 3, "fewer than 3" is X≤2X \le 2, "at least 3" is X≥3X \ge 3, "more than 3" is X≥4X \ge 4. For a count the difference matters; for a normal variable it does not, because one value has probability 0.
  • The empirical rule. About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of a normal distribution lies within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean. It is an approximation; the exact areas are 0.6827, 0.9545 and 0.9973.
  • The normal shape over a binomial. The curve with mean npnp and SD np(1−p)\sqrt{np(1 - p)} is drawn only to compare shapes. Every binomial probability here is exact.
