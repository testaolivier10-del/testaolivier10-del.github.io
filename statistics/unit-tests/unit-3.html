Getting to school at three high schools

A district takes a separate random sample of students at each of its three high schools and asks how they usually get to school. (Invented data.)

1. Which test fits the district's question of whether the travel mode distributions differ among the schools?

  1. A chi-square test for homogeneity
  2. A chi-square test for independence
  3. Three one-proportion z tests
  4. A two-sample z test for the bus proportions
Show the answer

Several samples or groups, one categorical variable: homogeneity.

  • Correct: A chi-square test for homogeneity: Right: three separate random samples, one categorical variable compared across them.
  • A chi-square test for independence: Independence uses one sample classified by two variables. Here the district chose how many students to sample at each school.
  • Three one-proportion z tests: Separate tests for one category at a time would not compare the whole distributions at once.
  • A two-sample z test for the bus proportions: That compares two schools on one category. There are three schools and three categories.

2. Which null hypothesis fits this test?

  1. The three schools have the same number of students
  2. The distribution of travel mode is different at each school
  3. Bus, car and walking are equally common at each of the schools
  4. The travel mode distribution is the same at all three schools
Show the answer

H0H_0: the distributions are the same; HaH_a: at least one differs.

  • The three schools have the same number of students: The test compares the travel mode distributions, not school sizes.
  • The distribution of travel mode is different at each school: That is the alternative: at least one school's distribution differs.
  • Bus, car and walking are equally common at each of the schools: Equal shares for each mode is not the claim. H0H_0 says the schools share one distribution, whatever it is.
  • Correct: The travel mode distribution is the same at all three schools: Right: homogeneity means "same distribution" in every population.

3. Which statement checks the 10% condition for this test?

  1. Each expected count is at least 10% of its row total
  2. At least 10% of each sample walks or bikes
  3. Each sample is less than 10% of the students at its own school
  4. The 400 students are less than 10% of the district's adults
Show the answer

10%: each sample is less than 10% of its population when sampling without replacement.

  • Each expected count is at least 10% of its row total: That is not a condition. Expected counts are checked against 5.
  • At least 10% of each sample walks or bikes: That is not the condition. The 10% condition compares sample size with population size.
  • Correct: Each sample is less than 10% of the students at its own school: Right: with three samples drawn without replacement, check each against its own population.
  • The 400 students are less than 10% of the district's adults: The populations are the students at each school, not the district's adults.

4. Which of these changes to the table would make the chi-square statistic larger, with the totals kept the same?

  1. Expected counts that equal the observed counts
  2. Observed counts that sit closer to their expected counts
  3. Listing the schools in a different order
  4. Observed counts farther from their expected counts
Show the answer

The statistic measures how far the observed counts are from what H0H_0 predicts.

  • Expected counts that equal the observed counts: Then each term is 0 and χ2=0\chi^2 = 0, the smallest it can be.
  • Observed counts that sit closer to their expected counts: Closer counts give smaller terms and a smaller χ2\chi^2.
  • Listing the schools in a different order: Reordering the rows changes no count, so χ2\chi^2 is the same.
  • Correct: Observed counts farther from their expected counts: Right: χ2\chi^2 adds up (O−E)2E\frac{(O - E)^2}{E}, so larger gaps between observed and expected give a larger value.

A city council selects a random sample of 600 residents, and 324 of them (p^=0.54\hat{p} = 0.54) favor longer library hours. A computer builds three confidence intervals for the proportion of all residents who favor longer hours, at 90%, 95% and 99% confidence, and lists them in random order.

5. Which interval was built with 99% confidence?

  1. Interval 1
  2. Interval 2
  3. Interval 3
  4. There is no way to tell, because they come from the same sample
Show the answer

Higher confidence needs a larger critical value (2.576 for 99%), so the margin of error is largest. Interval 2 reaches farthest from 0.54 on each side (from 0.488 to 0.592).

  • Interval 1: Interval 1, from 0.500 to 0.580, is in the middle: it is the 95% interval.
  • Correct: Interval 2: Right: the largest margin of error comes from the largest critical value.
  • Interval 3: Interval 3 has the smallest margin of error, so it has the lowest confidence, 90%.
  • There is no way to tell, because they come from the same sample: Same sample, same p^\hat{p} and standard error, but different critical values, so different margins of error.

6. What is the margin of error of Interval 1?

  1. 0.040
  2. 0.080
  3. 0.540
  4. 0.020
Show the answer

The margin of error is half the distance between the ends: 0.580−0.5002\frac{0.580 - 0.500}{2}=0.040= 0.040. Equivalently, it is the distance from p^=0.54\hat{p} = 0.54 to either end.

  • Correct: 0.040: Right: half the distance between the two ends.
  • 0.080: That is the whole distance from one end to the other. The margin of error is half of it.
  • 0.540: That is the center of the interval, the point estimate, not the margin of error.
  • 0.020: That is the standard error. The margin of error is the standard error times z∗=1.96z^* = 1.96.

7. Suppose the council had surveyed 2,400 residents and still found p^=0.54\hat{p} = 0.54. About what would the margin of error of the 95% interval be?

  1. 0.020
  2. 0.010
  3. 0.040
  4. 0.080
Show the answer

The margin of error is z∗p^(1−p^)/nz^*\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/n}. With 4 times the sample size it is divided by 4=2\sqrt{4} = 2: about 0.0402=0.020\frac{0.040}{2} = 0.020.

  • Correct: 0.020: Right: 4 times the sample size halves the margin of error.
  • 0.010: The sample size is inside a square root, so 4 times the residents divides the margin by 2, not 4.
  • 0.040: A larger sample gives a smaller margin of error; it does not stay the same.
  • 0.080: More residents give a more precise estimate, so the margin of error shrinks.

8. For a new poll, the council wants a 95% margin of error of at most 0.02. Using 0.54 as its estimate of the proportion, what is the smallest sample size it needs?

  1. 2,385
  2. 2,386
  3. 2,401
  4. 1,681
Show the answer

Solve 1.960.54(0.46)n≤0.021.96\sqrt{\frac{0.54(0.46)}{n}} \le 0.02: n≥(1.960.02)2(0.54)n \ge \left(\frac{1.96}{0.02}\right)^2(0.54)(0.46)(0.46)=2385.6= 2385.6. Round up to 2,386 residents (always round a sample size up).

  • 2,385: The calculation gives 2,385.6, and a sample size is always rounded up, or the margin would be a bit over 0.02.
  • Correct: 2,386: Right: (1.960.02)2(0.54)(0.46)\left(\frac{1.96}{0.02}\right)^2(0.54)(0.46)=2385.6= 2385.6, rounded up.
  • 2,401: That uses 0.5 in place of 0.54. It is the safe choice when there is no estimate, but this question says to use 0.54.
  • 1,681: That uses z∗=1.645z^* = 1.645, the critical value for 90% confidence. For 95%, use 1.96.

9. A biologist wants the largest wingspan among a park's 3,000 bats. She captures a random sample of 30 bats and uses the largest wingspan in the sample. Which describes this estimator?

  1. Unbiased, because the sample was chosen at random
  2. Biased: it tends to underestimate the largest wingspan
  3. Biased: it tends to overestimate the largest wingspan
  4. Unbiased, because a sample of 30 bats is large enough
Show the answer

The sample maximum can equal the population maximum but can never exceed it. So its sampling distribution sits at or below the true value: it tends to underestimate, which is bias.

  • Unbiased, because the sample was chosen at random: Random selection does not make every statistic unbiased. The sample maximum cannot go above the true maximum, so it tends to fall short.
  • Correct: Biased: it tends to underestimate the largest wingspan: Right: no sample can show a wingspan larger than the largest in the park, and most samples miss the largest bat.
  • Biased: it tends to overestimate the largest wingspan: A sample cannot contain a bat larger than the largest bat in the park, so it cannot overestimate.
  • Unbiased, because a sample of 30 bats is large enough: A larger sample would reduce the shortfall, but the sample maximum still tends to be too small.

10. In a large city, 50% of voters favor a new transit tax. A random sample of 400 voters is taken. Find the probability that 55% or more of the sample favors the tax.

Type a number. A table answer and a calculator answer are both accepted.

Show the answer

Large counts: 400(0.5)=200≥10400(0.5) = 200 \ge 10 twice. σp^\sigma_{\hat{p}}=0.5(0.5)400= \sqrt{\frac{0.5(0.5)}{400}}=0.025= 0.025, so zz=0.55−0.500.025= \frac{0.55 - 0.50}{0.025}=2.00= 2.00 and P(p^≥0.55)P(\hat{p} \ge 0.55)=P(Z≥2.00)= P(Z \ge 2.00)=0.0228= 0.0228.

  • Answer: 0.0228

11. A meal-kit company says that 25% of adults in the state have used its type of service. Based on the interval from 0.214 to 0.262, what can you conclude?

  1. 0.25 is plausible: no convincing evidence against the company's figure
  2. The company is right, because 0.25 is inside the interval
  3. The company is wrong, because the sample proportion, 0.238, is not 0.25
  4. The company is wrong, because 0.25 is not the center of the interval
Show the answer

0.25 is inside the interval, so it is a plausible value for the true proportion. The interval does not give convincing evidence that the company is wrong.

  • Correct: 0.25 is plausible: no convincing evidence against the company's figure: Right: a value inside the interval cannot be ruled out.
  • The company is right, because 0.25 is inside the interval: Plausible is not proven; values such as 0.22 are plausible too.
  • The company is wrong, because the sample proportion, 0.238, is not 0.25: A sample proportion is expected to differ a little from pp; the interval accounts for that.
  • The company is wrong, because 0.25 is not the center of the interval: The center is the sample proportion. Any value inside the interval is plausible.

12. A clinic says that more than half of its patients get an appointment within a week. A reporter wants to find evidence for the clinic's claim. Which hypotheses fit, where pp is the true proportion of all the clinic's patients seen within a week?

  1. H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5, Ha:p>0.5H_a: p > 0.5
  2. H0:p>0.5H_0: p > 0.5, Ha:p=0.5H_a: p = 0.5
  3. H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5, Ha:p<0.5H_a: p < 0.5
  4. H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5, Ha:p≠0.5H_a: p \ne 0.5
Show the answer

The claim the reporter wants evidence for goes in the alternative: Ha:p>0.5H_a: p > 0.5, with H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5.

  • Correct: H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5, Ha:p>0.5H_a: p > 0.5: Right: evidence for "more than half" means Ha:p>0.5H_a: p > 0.5.
  • H0:p>0.5H_0: p > 0.5, Ha:p=0.5H_a: p = 0.5: Reversed. A test looks for evidence for HaH_a, so the claim to support goes there.
  • H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5, Ha:p<0.5H_a: p < 0.5: That would look for evidence that fewer than half are seen within a week.
  • H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5, Ha:p≠0.5H_a: p \ne 0.5: The claim has a direction, "more than half", so the alternative is one-sided.
