A tutoring program's claim

A tutoring company claims its 6-week program raises math test scores by more than 10 points on average. A district took a random sample of 40 students who completed the program and compared each student's score before and after (invented data). The differences (after minus before) show no strong skew.

Summary of the 40 differences, after minus before, in points
Sample sizeMean differenceStandard deviation of differences95% confidence interval for the mean difference
4011.211.9from 7.4 to 15.0 points

1. Does the interval give convincing evidence that students improve at all, on average?

  1. No, because 10 is in the interval
  2. Yes, because every value in the interval is greater than 0
  3. No, because some students' scores went down
  4. Yes, because the sample mean of 11.2 points is well above 0 points
Show the answer

The claim "some improvement" means μd>0\mu_d > 0. Since 0 is outside the interval and every plausible value is positive, there is convincing evidence of improvement.

  • No, because 10 is in the interval: Whether 10 is inside matters for the company's claim. For "any improvement" the value to check is 0.
  • Correct: Yes, because every value in the interval is greater than 0: A mean difference of 0 (no change) is not plausible, since the interval runs from 7.4 to 15.0.
  • No, because some students' scores went down: Individual students can decline while the mean difference is clearly positive.
  • Yes, because the sample mean of 11.2 points is well above 0 points: A positive sample mean alone could be chance. The interval, entirely above 0, is the evidence.

2. The district wants a narrower 95% interval in its next study. What should it change?

  1. Use a larger random sample of students
  2. Use a 99% confidence level
  3. Compare two separate groups of students instead of before and after
  4. Leave out the students whose scores dropped
Show the answer

Margin of error =t∗sdn= t^*\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}: a larger sample shrinks it. Four times the students roughly halves it.

  • Correct: Use a larger random sample of students: A larger nn makes sdn\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} smaller (and t∗t^* slightly smaller), so the interval narrows at the same confidence.
  • Use a 99% confidence level: A higher level needs a larger t∗t^*, which widens the interval.
  • Compare two separate groups of students instead of before and after: Two separate groups add student-to-student variation, which usually widens the interval.
  • Leave out the students whose scores dropped: Dropping data that do not fit is not honest and makes the sample unrepresentative.

3. A parent reads the interval and says, "So 95% of students gain between 7.4 and 15.0 points." What is wrong?

  1. Nothing; that is the meaning of 95% confidence
  2. It should say 95% of the 40 sampled students
  3. The interval estimates the mean gain, not individual gains
  4. It should say that 5% of students gain less than 7.4 points
Show the answer

A confidence interval is about a parameter, here μd\mu_d. With sd=11.9s_d = 11.9, single students' gains spread far wider than 7.4 to 15.0.

  • Nothing; that is the meaning of 95% confidence: 95% confidence describes how often the method captures the true mean, not where 95% of individual values fall.
  • It should say 95% of the 40 sampled students: Even for the sample, the interval does not describe individual gains.
  • Correct: The interval estimates the mean gain, not individual gains: Individual differences have standard deviation 11.9 points, so many students gain far more or less than this range.
  • It should say that 5% of students gain less than 7.4 points: The interval makes no statement about the share of students above or below a value.

μA−μB\mu_A - \mu_B (city A minus city B)99%from −45-45 to 130 dollars

4. A reporter writes, "The study proves that rents are the same in the two cities." What is wrong?

  1. Nothing is wrong, because 0 is inside the interval from −45-45 to 130
  2. The interval shows city A is more expensive
  3. A 99% interval is too wide to say anything
  4. 0 is plausible, but so are differences up to 130 dollars
Show the answer

Failing to find convincing evidence of a difference is not evidence of no difference.

  • Nothing is wrong, because 0 is inside the interval from −45-45 to 130: Being in the interval makes 0 plausible, not proven.
  • The interval shows city A is more expensive: Negative values are in the interval too, so no direction is established.
  • A 99% interval is too wide to say anything: It says something useful: no convincing evidence of a difference, and how big a difference is plausible.
  • Correct: 0 is plausible, but so are differences up to 130 dollars: A plausible value is not a proven one. The interval also allows city A to cost up to 130 dollars more, or city B up to 45 dollars more.

5. If the group had built a 95% interval from the same large samples instead, which statement is true? (For large samples, t∗t^* is close to 2.58 for 99% and 1.96 for 95%.)

  1. It would be narrower, about −24-24 to 109 dollars, and still contain 0
  2. It would be wider, so it would contain 0 with more room to spare
  3. It would be the same interval, since the data are the same
  4. It would be narrower and would exclude 0, since 95% is less strict
Show the answer

Center 42.5 dollars; 95% margin about 66 dollars; interval about −24-24 to 109 dollars, which contains 0. The conclusion does not change.

  • Correct: It would be narrower, about −24-24 to 109 dollars, and still contain 0: Same center, −45+1302=42.5\frac{-45 + 130}{2} = 42.5; the margin shrinks from 87.5 to about 87.5×1.962.58=6687.5 \times \frac{1.96}{2.58} = 66, so the interval still reaches below 0.
  • It would be wider, so it would contain 0 with more room to spare: A lower confidence level gives a narrower interval, not a wider one.
  • It would be the same interval, since the data are the same: The critical value changes with the confidence level, so the margin changes.
  • It would be narrower and would exclude 0, since 95% is less strict: It is narrower, but its lower end, about −24-24, is still below 0.

6. What range of differences in mean rent does the interval treat as plausible?

  1. From city A costing 45 dollars more to city A costing 130 dollars more
  2. From city B costing 45 dollars more to city A costing 130 dollars more
  3. Differences near the center of 42.5 dollars, and not the ends
  4. Differences in individual rents from −45-45 to 130 dollars
Show the answer

μA−μB\mu_A - \mu_B from −45-45 to 130: B up to 45 dollars more expensive, or A up to 130 dollars more expensive, on average.

  • From city A costing 45 dollars more to city A costing 130 dollars more: A negative difference, μA−μB=−45\mu_A - \mu_B = -45, means city B costs more.
  • Correct: From city B costing 45 dollars more to city A costing 130 dollars more: Negative values mean B is more expensive on average, positive values mean A is, so the interval covers both directions.
  • Differences near the center of 42.5 dollars, and not the ends: Every value in the interval is plausible, not just the center.
  • Differences in individual rents from −45-45 to 130 dollars: The interval is about the difference in mean rents, not individual apartments.

7. Incomes in a town are strongly skewed right. A researcher takes a random sample of 8 households. What shape does the sampling distribution of x¯\bar{x} have?

  1. Approximately normal, because sample means are normal
  2. Skewed left, the opposite of the population
  3. Skewed right, though less skewed than the incomes
  4. Exactly as skewed as the incomes
Show the answer

For a skewed population and a small sample, the sampling distribution of x¯\bar{x} is skewed in the same direction, less strongly. A normal model would need n≥30n \ge 30.

  • Approximately normal, because sample means are normal: Sample means become approximately normal only for a normal population or a large sample.
  • Skewed left, the opposite of the population: Averaging pulls the shape toward normal; it does not flip the direction of skew.
  • Correct: Skewed right, though less skewed than the incomes: With n=8n = 8 the central limit theorem cannot be counted on, so some of the population's right skew remains.
  • Exactly as skewed as the incomes: Averaging 8 values already reduces the skew, though not enough to call the shape normal.

8. As the degrees of freedom increase, what happens to t∗t^* for 95% confidence?

  1. It increases toward 3
  2. It decreases toward z∗=1.96z^* = 1.96
  3. It stays the same, at 1.96, whatever the sample size
  4. It decreases toward 0
Show the answer

Table B: the 95% column falls from 12.71 at df 1 toward 1.96 in the infinite row.

  • It increases toward 3: The heaviest tails, and the largest t∗t^*, come with the fewest degrees of freedom.
  • Correct: It decreases toward z∗=1.96z^* = 1.96: More data make ss a steadier estimate of σ\sigma, so the t distribution gets closer to the standard normal: 2.776 at df 4, 2.064 at df 24, 1.984 at df 100.
  • It stays the same, at 1.96, whatever the sample size: t∗t^* is larger than 1.96 at each finite df, and the gap shrinks as df grows.
  • It decreases toward 0: t∗t^* approaches z∗=1.96z^* = 1.96, not 0: the middle 95% of a normal curve still has width.

9. A student sees a sample mean of 27 minutes for battery charging time and then writes Ha:μ<30H_a: \mu < 30 because 27 is below 30. The original question was whether the mean differs from 30. What is the error?

  1. The direction of HaH_a was chosen from the data instead of the question
  2. The hypothesized mean should be 27
  3. There is no error, because the data showed which direction to test
  4. The alternative should be μ>30\mu > 30
Show the answer

Hypotheses are set from the research question, before looking at the data.

  • Correct: The direction of HaH_a was chosen from the data instead of the question: The question asked about a difference in either direction, so Ha:μ≠30H_a: \mu \ne 30. Letting the sample pick the side inflates the chance of a false rejection.
  • The hypothesized mean should be 27: The hypothesized mean comes from the question (30); 27 is the sample result.
  • There is no error, because the data showed which direction to test: Choosing the side after seeing the data makes the test look stronger than it is.
  • The alternative should be μ>30\mu > 30: The question is two-sided, so neither one-sided alternative fits.

10. A random sample of 20 students at a large university slept a mean of 7.55 hours a night with standard deviation 1.09 hours. For H0:μ=8H_0: \mu = 8 against Ha:μ≠8H_a: \mu \ne 8, what is the p-value?

Type a number. A table answer and a calculator answer are both accepted.

Show the answer

tt=7.55−81.09/20= \frac{7.55 - 8}{1.09/\sqrt{20}}=−1.85= -1.85, df=19\text{df} = 19. Two-sided: pp=2P(t<−1.85)= 2P(t < -1.85)=0.080= 0.080.

11. Independent random samples of 60 nurses are taken at each of two hospitals, one with 450 nurses and one with 2,000. Which condition check is needed for the standard deviation formula?

  1. The total of 120 has to be less than 10% of the 2,450 nurses
  2. Each sample has to be less than 10% of its own hospital's nurses
  3. The two hospitals need the same number of nurses
  4. Each sample needs at least 10 nurses
Show the answer

Independence within each sample: n1<0.10N1n_1 < 0.10N_1 and n2<0.10N2n_2 < 0.10N_2. Here 60>4560 > 45 at the smaller hospital.

  • The total of 120 has to be less than 10% of the 2,450 nurses: Each sample is drawn from its own population, so each is checked separately.
  • Correct: Each sample has to be less than 10% of its own hospital's nurses: The 10% condition is checked for each sample. 60 is less than 10% of 2,000 (200) but not of 450 (45), so it fails for the first hospital.
  • The two hospitals need the same number of nurses: Population sizes can differ; each sample just needs to be a small share of its own population.
  • Each sample needs at least 10 nurses: That is not a condition for means; the large counts condition is for proportions.

12. A 95% interval for μA−μB\mu_A - \mu_B is from 2.1 to 6.5. What is the 95% interval for μB−μA\mu_B - \mu_A from the same data?

  1. From 2.1 to 6.5
  2. From −2.1-2.1 to −6.5-6.5
  3. From −6.5-6.5 to −2.1-2.1
  4. From 6.5 to 2.1
Show the answer

If μA−μB\mu_A - \mu_B is between 2.1 and 6.5, then μB−μA\mu_B - \mu_A is between −6.5-6.5 and −2.1-2.1.

  • From 2.1 to 6.5: The new parameter is the negative of the old one, so the interval changes sign.
  • From −2.1-2.1 to −6.5-6.5: Write the lower end first: −6.5-6.5 is less than −2.1-2.1.
  • Correct: From −6.5-6.5 to −2.1-2.1: Reversing the order of subtraction flips the sign of every value: the interval keeps its width and changes sides of 0.
  • From 6.5 to 2.1: That reverses the ends without changing signs, and the lower end must come first.
