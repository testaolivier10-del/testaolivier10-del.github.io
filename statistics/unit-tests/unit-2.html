Simulated sample proportions

In a large city, 40% of voters support a new park tax. A computer took 500 random samples of 100 voters each and found the sample proportion p^\hat{p} of supporters in each sample. The histogram shows the 500 values of p^\hat{p} (simulated data).

1. What does one of the 500 values in the histogram represent?

  1. The opinion of one voter, support or not, in a single sample
  2. The proportion of supporters among the city's voters, 0.40
  3. The number of samples that had 40 supporters out of 100
  4. The proportion of supporters in one random sample of 100 voters
Show the answer

Each value is a statistic: the proportion of supporters in one random sample of 100 voters.

  • The opinion of one voter, support or not, in a single sample: One voter gives a yes or no; a sample of 100 gives a proportion.
  • The proportion of supporters among the city's voters, 0.40: That is the parameter, one fixed number.
  • The number of samples that had 40 supporters out of 100: The bar heights count samples; each value is a proportion.
  • Correct: The proportion of supporters in one random sample of 100 voters: Right: one sample, one p^\hat{p}.

2. Where is the distribution of p^\hat{p} centered, and why?

  1. Near 0.40, the proportion of all the city's voters who support the tax
  2. Near 0.50, since voters either support the tax or they do not
  3. Near 100, the number of voters in each sample
  4. Near 0.60, the proportion of voters who do not support it
Show the answer

The values are centered near 0.40 (their mean is about 0.401), the population proportion: the sampling distribution of p^\hat{p} is centered at p.

  • Correct: Near 0.40, the proportion of all the city's voters who support the tax: Right: μp^=p\mu_{\hat{p}} = p.
  • Near 0.50, since voters either support the tax or they do not: Two possible answers do not make each one half as likely.
  • Near 100, the number of voters in each sample: The values are proportions between 0 and 1.
  • Near 0.60, the proportion of voters who do not support it: 0.60 is the other group; the histogram shows supporters.

3. If the computer had taken samples of 400 voters instead of 100, how would the histogram change?

  1. Same center, but four times as spread out
  2. Same center, but about half as spread out
  3. The center would move from 0.40 to 0.10
  4. It would not change, since the city has the same voters
Show the answer

Same center, 0.40, but less spread: four times the sample size cuts the spread of p^\hat{p} in half, from about 0.049 to about 0.024. Larger random samples give statistics closer to the parameter.

  • Same center, but four times as spread out: More voters per sample make p^\hat{p} less variable, not more.
  • Correct: Same center, but about half as spread out: Right: spread shrinks like 1n\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}, and 4=2\sqrt{4} = 2.
  • The center would move from 0.40 to 0.10: The center stays at the population proportion, 0.40.
  • It would not change, since the city has the same voters: The population is the same, but the sample size changes the spread.

4. In this setting, which is a parameter and which is a statistic?

  1. 0.40 is a statistic; each p^\hat{p} is a parameter
  2. Both are statistics, since both are proportions
  3. 0.40 is a parameter; each p^\hat{p} is a statistic
  4. Both are parameters, since they describe the same voters
Show the answer

A parameter describes the population (0.40 of all the city's voters). A statistic describes a sample (p^\hat{p} for each sample of 100), and it varies from sample to sample.

  • 0.40 is a statistic; each p^\hat{p} is a parameter: That is reversed: the 0.40 describes every voter in the city.
  • Both are statistics, since both are proportions: Being a proportion does not decide it; population or sample does.
  • Correct: 0.40 is a parameter; each p^\hat{p} is a statistic: Right: population number, then sample numbers.
  • Both are parameters, since they describe the same voters: Each p^\hat{p} describes only its own sample of 100.

Events for one roll of a die

A fair die is rolled once. Event V: the roll is even (2, 4, 6). Event F: the roll is a 5. Event G: the roll is greater than 3 (4, 5, 6).

5. Which pair of events is mutually exclusive?

  1. V and G
  2. F and G
  3. G and "the roll is 6"
  4. V and F
Show the answer

V is 2, 4, 6 and F is 5: no outcome is in both. V and G share 4 and 6; F and G share 5.

  • V and G: They share 4 and 6, so they can happen together.
  • F and G: A 5 is greater than 3, so F and G happen together on a 5.
  • G and "the roll is 6": A 6 is in G, so they overlap.
  • Correct: V and F: Right: an even roll can never be a 5.

6. What is the probability that the roll is even or a 5?

  1. 36\frac{3}{6}
  2. 46\frac{4}{6}
  3. 16\frac{1}{6}
  4. 336\frac{3}{36}
Show the answer

V and F are mutually exclusive, so the probabilities add: 36+16=46\frac{3}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{4}{6} (the rolls 2, 4, 5, 6).

  • 36\frac{3}{6}: That is the even rolls alone, without the 5.
  • Correct: 46\frac{4}{6}: Right: 2, 4, 5 and 6.
  • 16\frac{1}{6}: That is the 5 alone.
  • 336\frac{3}{36}: That multiplies the probabilities, which does not give the chance of one or the other.

7. A student finds the probability that the roll is even or greater than 3 as 36+36=1\frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = 1. What went wrong?

  1. Nothing went wrong, since each event has probability one half
  2. The student should have multiplied the probabilities to get one fourth
  3. The events overlap at 4 and 6, so those rolls were counted twice
  4. The die has 6 faces, so the answer should have been divided by 6
Show the answer

V and G are not mutually exclusive: 4 and 6 are in both, so adding counts them twice. Listing the outcomes, 2, 4, 5, 6, gives 46\frac{4}{6}.

  • Nothing went wrong, since each event has probability one half: A probability of 1 would mean a 1 or a 3 could never be rolled, which is false.
  • The student should have multiplied the probabilities to get one fourth: Multiplying does not give the chance of one event or the other.
  • Correct: The events overlap at 4 and 6, so those rolls were counted twice: Right: adding works only for mutually exclusive events.
  • The die has 6 faces, so the answer should have been divided by 6: The fractions already use 6 as the denominator.

8. A survey has 400 adults who rent and 100 who own their home. To compare how the two groups commute, why use percents instead of counts?

  1. The groups differ in size, so counts favor the larger group
  2. Percents are more accurate than counts
  3. Counts are not allowed on the axis of a bar chart
  4. Percents remove any association between where people live and how they commute
Show the answer

With 4 times as many renters, renter counts will be larger in almost every category. Percents put the two groups on the same scale.

  • Correct: The groups differ in size, so counts favor the larger group: Right: percents of each group compare like with like.
  • Percents are more accurate than counts: A percent is computed from the counts, so it is no more accurate; it is just on a common scale.
  • Counts are not allowed on the axis of a bar chart: Bar charts can show counts; the problem here is the unequal group sizes.
  • Percents remove any association between where people live and how they commute: Percents reveal association more clearly; they do not remove it.

9. A cafe recorded 180 orders. In the morning there were 80 coffee and 25 tea orders; in the afternoon, 30 coffee and 45 tea orders (invented data). What proportion of the afternoon orders were tea?

Type a number. A table answer and a calculator answer are both accepted.

Show the answer

There were 30+45=7530 + 45 = 75 afternoon orders, and 45 were tea: 4575=0.6\frac{45}{75} = 0.6.

  • Answer: 0.6

10. Two students simulate the same chance process. One runs 50 trials and estimates 0.12; the other runs 1,000 trials and estimates 0.09. Which estimate is more trustworthy, and why?

  1. 0.12, because the first student got a larger share of successes
  2. 0.09, because estimates from more trials stay closer to the true value
  3. Each is equally trustworthy, since both used the same model
  4. Neither one, because two simulations should give the same answer
Show the answer

Estimates from more trials vary less around the true value (the law of large numbers), so 0.09 from 1,000 trials is more trustworthy.

  • 0.12, because the first student got a larger share of successes: A larger estimate is not a better one.
  • Correct: 0.09, because estimates from more trials stay closer to the true value: Right: 1,000 trials give a steadier estimate than 50.
  • Each is equally trustworthy, since both used the same model: The model is the same, but 50 trials leave much more room for chance.
  • Neither one, because two simulations should give the same answer: Simulations vary by chance; more trials reduce that variation.

11. A forecast says the probability of rain tomorrow is 0.3. What does this mean?

  1. On many days like tomorrow, it would rain on about 30% of them
  2. It will rain for about 30% of the hours tomorrow, about 7 hours
  3. It will rain in about 30% of the forecast area tomorrow, in some towns
  4. It will not rain tomorrow, since 0.3 is less than half
Show the answer

A probability is a long-run share: over many days with conditions like tomorrow's, it rains on about 30% of them.

  • Correct: On many days like tomorrow, it would rain on about 30% of them: Right: probability as a long-run relative frequency.
  • It will rain for about 30% of the hours tomorrow, about 7 hours: The forecast is about whether it rains, not how long it rains.
  • It will rain in about 30% of the forecast area tomorrow, in some towns: The probability is about rain at a place, not the share of the area.
  • It will not rain tomorrow, since 0.3 is less than half: Unlikely is not impossible: it rains on about 3 days in 10 like this one.

12. At a store, 40% of customers use a coupon, and 10% of customers both use a coupon and spend more than $50. Given that a customer uses a coupon, what is the probability that the customer spends more than $50?

Type a number. A table answer and a calculator answer are both accepted.

Show the answer

P(over $50|coupon)P(\text{over } $50 \mid \text{coupon})=P(over $50∩coupon)P(coupon)= \frac{P(\text{over } $50 \cap \text{coupon})}{P(\text{coupon})}=0.100.40= \frac{0.10}{0.40}=0.25= 0.25.

  • Answer: 0.25
