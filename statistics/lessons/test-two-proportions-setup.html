Setting Up a Test for the Difference Between Two Population Proportions

A two-sample z test for p_1 − p_2 starts from H_0:p_1 − p_2 = 0 and an alternative chosen from the question.

Practice 2: Collect DataPractice 4: Interpret Results

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A city hands out a new recycling bin to 160 randomly chosen households and leaves 140 with the old one. A month later, 78% of the new bin households sort correctly against 65% of the others. Before anyone runs the numbers, the test has to be set up: what exactly is being claimed, which procedure fits, and are its conditions met?

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The question compares two groups on a yes or no outcome→the parameter is p1−p2p_1 - p_2, and the procedure is a two-sample z test
  2. The null hypothesis says there is no difference→H0:p1−p2=0H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0, and HaH_a says >>, << or ≠\ne as the question asks, chosen before seeing the data
  3. If H0H_0 is true, both samples come from populations with one common proportion→estimate it by pooling: pcp_c=x1+x2n1+n2= \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}, total successes over total sample size
  4. The test is computed as if H0H_0 were true→check large counts with pcp_c: n1pcn_1 p_c, n1(1−pc)n_1(1 - p_c), n2pcn_2 p_c, n2(1−pc)n_2(1 - p_c) each at least 10, plus random and 10%

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Hypotheses: H0:p1−p2=0H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0 and Ha:p1−p2>0H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0, <0< 0 or ≠0\ne 0, with each pp defined as a true proportion in context.
  • Pooled proportion: pcp_c=x1+x2n1+n2= \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}, the combined successes over the combined sample size. Never the average of p^1\hat{p}_1 and p^2\hat{p}_2.
  • Conditions: two independent random samples or randomly assigned groups; 10% for each sample; large counts with pcp_c.
  • Name it in full: "a two-sample z test for p1−p2p_1 - p_2".

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The pooled proportion is the average of the two sample proportions.

What actually happens: Pool the counts, not the proportions: add the successes and divide by the total sample size. With unequal sample sizes the two answers differ, and only the pooled counts give the right estimate.

The null is no difference; the alternative is the direction of the research question.

2. How should pNp_N be defined in context?

  1. The proportion of the 160 new bin households in this study that sorted correctly
  2. The chance that a household chosen at random picks the new bin over the old one
  3. The number of households in the study that sorted correctly with the new bin
  4. The true share of such households that would sort correctly with the new bin
Show the answer

Define each parameter as a true proportion, in context.

  • The proportion of the 160 new bin households in this study that sorted correctly: That is p^N=0.781\hat{p}_N = 0.781, a statistic we already know.
  • The chance that a household chosen at random picks the new bin over the old one: Households did not choose; the city assigned the bins at random.
  • The number of households in the study that sorted correctly with the new bin: A proportion is a fraction of a group, not a count.
  • Correct: The true share of such households that would sort correctly with the new bin: Right: in an experiment, the parameter is the proportion of subjects like these who would succeed with that treatment.

5. A researcher looks at her data, sees that p^1>p^2\hat{p}_1 > \hat{p}_2, and then writes Ha:p1−p2>0H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0. What is wrong?

  1. The direction of HaH_a must come from the question, not the data
  2. HaH_a should say p1−p2=0p_1 - p_2 = 0
  3. HaH_a should be written about the sample proportions, p^1−p^2\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2
  4. One-sided alternatives are not allowed for two proportions
Show the answer

Set hypotheses from the question, before the data.

  • Correct: The direction of HaH_a must come from the question, not the data: Right: choosing a one-sided test because of the sample result makes evidence look stronger than it is.
  • HaH_a should say p1−p2=0p_1 - p_2 = 0: The equality belongs in H0H_0.
  • HaH_a should be written about the sample proportions, p^1−p^2\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2: Hypotheses are about parameters, never sample statistics.
  • One-sided alternatives are not allowed for two proportions: One-sided alternatives are fine when the question has a direction.

6. A clinic randomly assigns 30 patients to each of two treatments for a rare reaction. In all, 6 of the 60 patients have the reaction. Why is the large counts condition not met for a two-sample z test?

  1. 30(0.90)=2730(0.90) = 27 is more than 10
  2. The groups have the same size, 30
  3. Random assignment was used instead of random sampling
  4. pc=0.10p_c = 0.10, so 30(0.10)=330(0.10) = 3 is less than 10 in each group
Show the answer

Each of the four counts with pcp_c must reach 10.

  • 30(0.90)=2730(0.90) = 27 is more than 10: That count passes. It is the other count, 30(0.10)=330(0.10) = 3, that fails.
  • The groups have the same size, 30: Equal groups are fine. The small number of reactions expected in each group is the problem.
  • Random assignment was used instead of random sampling: Random assignment satisfies the random condition. The counts are the problem.
  • Correct: pc=0.10p_c = 0.10, so 30(0.10)=330(0.10) = 3 is less than 10 in each group: Right: with so few reactions overall, each group expects only 3, too few for a normal model.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

A two-sample z test for p1−p2p_1 - p_2 starts from H0:p1−p2=0H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0 and an alternative chosen from the question. Because the test assumes H0H_0, it pools the samples, pcp_c=x1+x2n1+n2= \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}, and checks large counts with pcp_c, along with two independent random samples (or random assignment) and the 10% condition.
