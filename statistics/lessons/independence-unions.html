Unit 2 · Topic 2.7

Independent Events and Unions of Events

Events are independent when P(A|B) = P(A) ; then the probability of both is the product P(A)⋅P(B) .

Practice 3: Analyze Data

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A phone reviewer tests two brands and finds that 60% of each brand's batteries last a full day. Does knowing the brand help you predict the battery? Not at all, and that is what independence means. A Venn diagram of students in two classes shows the other half of this topic: how to add probabilities that overlap.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Knowing that B happened leaves the probability of A unchanged→A and B are independent: P(A|B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A)
  2. Put P(A|B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A) into the multiplication rule→for independent events, P(A∩B)=P(A)⋅P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)
  3. Add P(A) and P(B) for "A or B"→the outcomes in both are counted twice
  4. Subtract the overlap once→the general addition rule: P(A∪B)P(A \cup B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Events are independent when knowing one happened does not change the probability of the other: P(A|B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A). Then, and only then, P(A∩B)=P(A)⋅P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B).
  • To check independence, compare P(A|B)P(A \mid B) with P(A)P(A), or P(A∩B)P(A \cap B) with P(A)⋅P(B)P(A) \cdot P(B), using the numbers.
  • The union A∪BA \cup B is "A or B or both". The general addition rule: P(A∪B)P(A \cup B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).
  • A Venn diagram shows events as overlapping circles; fill the overlap first.
  • Mutually exclusive is not independent: events that cannot happen together affect each other completely.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: Mutually exclusive events are independent, because one has nothing to do with the other.

What actually happens: If two events cannot happen together, knowing one happened makes the other impossible, so its probability drops to 0. They are as dependent as events can be.

Spanish and art students

At a school, 120 students are in the arts and languages program. 50 take Spanish, 40 take art, and 15 take both. One of the 120 students is chosen at random (invented data).

1. What is the probability that the student takes Spanish or art?

  1. 0.75
  2. 0.125
  3. 0.625
  4. 0.375
Show the answer

Addition rule: P(S∪R)P(S \cup R)=P(S)= P(S)+P(R)−P(S∩R)+ P(R) - P(S \cap R)=50120= \frac{50}{120}+40120−15120+ \frac{40}{120} - \frac{15}{120}=75120= \frac{75}{120}=0.625= 0.625, where S is Spanish and R is art.

  • 0.75: That is 90120\frac{90}{120}: the 15 students in both were counted twice.
  • 0.125: That is 15120\frac{15}{120}, the students who take both, not either.
  • Correct: 0.625: Right: 75 of the 120 students take at least one of the two.
  • 0.375: That is 45120\frac{45}{120}, the students who take neither.

2. Given that the student takes art, what is the probability that the student takes Spanish?

  1. 0.3
  2. 0.125
  3. 0.417
  4. 0.375
Show the answer

Given art, the 40 art students are the total, and 15 of them take Spanish: 1540=0.375\frac{15}{40} = 0.375.

  • 0.3: That is 1550\frac{15}{50}, the probability of art given Spanish.
  • 0.125: That is 15120\frac{15}{120}, the joint probability.
  • 0.417: That is 50120\frac{50}{120}, the probability of Spanish for all students.
  • Correct: 0.375: Right: 15 of the 40 art students.

3. Are "takes Spanish" and "takes art" independent events?

  1. No: P(Spanish given art) is 0.375 but P(Spanish) is about 0.417
  2. Yes, because 15 students take both classes, so the events overlap
  3. No, because they are mutually exclusive events at this school
  4. Yes, because the students chose their own classes
Show the answer

Compare P(Spanish|art)P(\text{Spanish} \mid \text{art})=1540= \frac{15}{40}=0.375= 0.375 with P(Spanish)P(\text{Spanish})=50120= \frac{50}{120}≈0.417\approx 0.417. They differ, so the events are not independent.

  • Correct: No: P(Spanish given art) is 0.375 but P(Spanish) is about 0.417: Right: knowing art changes the probability of Spanish.
  • Yes, because 15 students take both classes, so the events overlap: Overlapping is not independence; independence compares probabilities.
  • No, because they are mutually exclusive events at this school: They are not mutually exclusive: 15 students take both.
  • Yes, because the students chose their own classes: How classes were chosen does not settle it; compare the probabilities.

4. Are "Brand Y" and "battery lasted a full day" independent?

  1. No: more Brand Y phones (90) than Brand X phones (60) lasted a full day
  2. No: a phone can be Brand Y and last a full day, so they overlap
  3. Yes: a phone is either Brand X or Brand Y and is not both at once
  4. Yes: the probability of a full day is 0.6 with or without knowing the brand
Show the answer

P(full day|Brand Y)P(\text{full day} \mid \text{Brand Y})=90150= \frac{90}{150}=0.6= 0.6, the same as P(full day)=0.6P(\text{full day}) = 0.6 (and Brand X is 60100=0.6\frac{60}{100} = 0.6 too). Knowing the brand does not change the probability, so the events are independent.

  • No: more Brand Y phones (90) than Brand X phones (60) lasted a full day: Counts depend on how many of each brand were tested; compare probabilities.
  • No: a phone can be Brand Y and last a full day, so they overlap: Overlap is not how independence is checked.
  • Yes: a phone is either Brand X or Brand Y and is not both at once: That makes the brands mutually exclusive, which is a different idea.
  • Correct: Yes: the probability of a full day is 0.6 with or without knowing the brand: Right: equal conditional and unconditional probabilities.

6. In a town, 55% of households have a streaming subscription, 30% have cable, and 15% have both. What is the probability that a randomly chosen household has streaming or cable?

Type a number. A table answer and a calculator answer are both accepted.

Show the answer

General addition rule: 0.55+0.30−0.150.55 + 0.30 - 0.15=0.70= 0.70. Subtract the 15% with both, who were counted in each group.

  • Answer: 0.7

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Events are independent when P(A|B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A); then the probability of both is the product P(A)⋅P(B)P(A) \cdot P(B). Check independence with the numbers, never by assumption alone. The probability of A or B is P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B), subtracting the overlap that would be counted twice; for mutually exclusive events the overlap is 0.
