Unit 3 · Topic 3.10

Constructing a Confidence Interval for the Difference Between Two Population Proportions

To estimate p_1 − p_2 , use the two-sample z interval (p-hat_1 − p-hat_2) ±z*√((p-hat_1(1 − p-hat_1))/(n_1) + (p-hat_2(1 − p-hat_2))/(n_2)) after checking random, 10% and large counts for each sample.

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A transit agency asks random samples of city and suburban residents whether they would ride a new train line: 62% of 400 city residents say yes, and 54% of 300 suburban residents do. The gap in the samples is 8 percentage points. But how big is the gap for everyone in the region? A confidence interval for the difference gives a range of plausible values.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. You want the true difference p1−p2p_1 - p_2 but only have two samples→the sample difference p^1−p^2\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 is the point estimate
  2. Sample differences vary with standard deviation p1(1−p1)n1+p2(1−p2)n2\sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}→replace each pp by its p^\hat{p} to get the standard error, since the pps are unknown
  3. The sampling distribution is approximately normal when the four counts reach 10→go z∗z^* standard errors each way: (p^1−p^2)(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)±z∗p^1(1−p^1)n1+p^2(1−p^2)n2\pm z^* \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}
  4. The method captures the true difference in about 95% of samples→say "we are 95% confident that the interval captures the true difference in proportions", naming the order of subtraction

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Two-sample z interval: (p^1−p^2)(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)±z∗p^1(1−p^1)n1+p^2(1−p^2)n2\pm z^* \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}.
  • Conditions: two independent random samples or two randomly assigned groups; each sample under 10% of its population; at least 10 successes and 10 failures in each sample.
  • Interpret: "We are 95% confident that the interval from ... to ... captures the true difference in the proportions of ... (group 1 minus group 2)."
  • Random assignment allows a cause-and-effect conclusion; random sampling allows generalizing to the populations.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A 95% interval for p1−p2p_1 - p_2 means 95% of the people in group 1 are that much more likely to say yes.

What actually happens: The interval estimates one number, the true difference in the two population proportions. It says nothing about individuals; the 95% describes how often the method captures that number.

1. What is the point estimate of pC−pRp_C - p_R, the difference between the true proportions of city and rural shoppers who use the app?

  1. 0.5370.537
  2. 0.5250.525
  3. 8181
  4. 0.150.15
Show the answer

The point estimate is p^C−p^R\hat{p}_C - \hat{p}_R.

  • 0.5370.537: That is the proportion for both samples together, 279520\frac{279}{520}. It mixes the groups instead of comparing them.
  • 0.5250.525: That averages the two sample proportions. The estimate of a difference is a difference.
  • 8181: That subtracts the counts, 180−99180 - 99. The samples have different sizes, so compare proportions, not counts.
  • Correct: 0.150.15: Right: p^C−p^R\hat{p}_C - \hat{p}_R=0.60−0.45= 0.60 - 0.45=0.15= 0.15.

2. Which statement correctly checks the large counts condition for a confidence interval for pC−pRp_C - p_R?

  1. The counts 180, 120, 99 and 121 are each at least 10
  2. 300(0.5)=150300(0.5) = 150 and 220(0.5)=110220(0.5) = 110 are each at least 10
  3. Each sample proportion is between 0.1 and 0.9
  4. The two sample sizes, 300 and 220, are each at least 30
Show the answer

An interval uses p^1\hat{p}_1 and p^2\hat{p}_2: n1p^1n_1\hat{p}_1, n1(1−p^1)n_1(1 - \hat{p}_1), n2p^2n_2\hat{p}_2, n2(1−p^2)n_2(1 - \hat{p}_2) are each at least 10.

  • Correct: The counts 180, 120, 99 and 121 are each at least 10: Right: for an interval, use the observed successes and failures in each sample: 300−180=120300 - 180 = 120 and 220−99=121220 - 99 = 121.
  • 300(0.5)=150300(0.5) = 150 and 220(0.5)=110220(0.5) = 110 are each at least 10: There is no reason to use 0.5. An interval uses each sample's own p^\hat{p}.
  • Each sample proportion is between 0.1 and 0.9: That is not the condition. The counts of successes and failures must each reach 10.
  • The two sample sizes, 300 and 220, are each at least 30: Comparing nn with 30 is not the check for proportions. Count successes and failures in each sample.

6. A researcher takes independent random samples of 400 nurses and 350 teachers and asks whether each works a second job. She wants to estimate how much the true proportions differ. Which procedure fits?

  1. Two one-sample z intervals, checked for overlap
  2. A two-sample z interval for pN−pTp_N - p_T
  3. A one-proportion z test of H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5
  4. A one-sample z interval for pp, using the 750 people combined
Show the answer

Estimate a difference of two proportions with a two-sample z interval.

  • Two one-sample z intervals, checked for overlap: Overlap of two intervals is a rough and misleading comparison. One interval for the difference answers the question directly.
  • Correct: A two-sample z interval for pN−pTp_N - p_T: Right: two independent random samples, a categorical variable, and an estimate of a difference.
  • A one-proportion z test of H0:p=0.5H_0: p = 0.5: Nothing here is about 0.5, and the goal is to estimate, not to test one proportion.
  • A one-sample z interval for pp, using the 750 people combined: Combining the samples estimates one overall proportion. The question compares two groups.
