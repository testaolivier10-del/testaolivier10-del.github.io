Setting Up a Test for a Population Mean or Population Mean Difference

To set up a t test for a mean, name the parameter in context, write H_0:μ = μ_0 and an alternative whose direction comes from the question, and check random, 10% and normal/large sample.

Practice 2: Collect DataPractice 4: Interpret Results

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A delivery company promises 30 minutes on average. A manager thinks it is longer. Before anyone computes a number, the test has to be set up: which parameter, which hypotheses, which direction, and whether the conditions let a t procedure be trusted. Most lost points on inference questions happen here, not in the arithmetic.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Start from the question, before the data→it gives the parameter, the hypothesized mean μ0\mu_0 and the direction of HaH_a
  2. Write H0:μ=μ0H_0: \mu = \mu_0 and HaH_a with >>, << or ≠\ne→define μ\mu in context: "the true mean ... of all ..."
  3. If each individual gives two linked measurements→the data are paired: test H0:μd=0H_0: \mu_d = 0 on the differences, with the order of subtraction stated
  4. Check random, 10% and normal/large sample in words→a one-sample (or paired) t test can be trusted, with df=n−1\text{df} = n - 1

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • One-sample t test: H0:μ=μ0H_0: \mu = \mu_0 against Ha:μ>μ0H_a: \mu > \mu_0, μ<μ0\mu < \mu_0 or μ≠μ0\mu \ne \mu_0.
  • Paired t test: a one-sample t test on the differences, H0:μd=0H_0: \mu_d = 0. The order of subtraction sets the direction of HaH_a.
  • Hypotheses use parameters (μ\mu, μd\mu_d), never statistics (x¯\bar{x}), and the direction comes from the question, never from the sample result.
  • Conditions: random; 10% when sampling without replacement; normal population, n≥30n \ge 30, or a graph of the sample (or the differences) without strong skew or outliers.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The sample mean was below 30, so the alternative should be Ha:μ<30H_a: \mu < 30.

What actually happens: The alternative comes from the research question asked before the data. If the question is "different from 30", the alternative is μ≠30\mu \ne 30 whatever the sample shows.

Delivery times

A delivery company advertises a mean delivery time of 30 minutes in one city. A manager suspects the true mean is longer. She takes a random sample of 45 of last month's thousands of deliveries in the city and records each delivery time (invented data).

The study at a glance
ClaimSuspicionSampleVariable
mean delivery time is 30 minutesthe mean is greater than 3045 deliveries, chosen at randomdelivery time in minutes

1. Which hypotheses should the manager test?

  1. H0:μ=30H_0: \mu = 30, Ha:μ≠30H_a: \mu \ne 30
  2. H0:μ=30H_0: \mu = 30, Ha:μ>30H_a: \mu > 30
  3. H0:μ>30H_0: \mu > 30, Ha:μ=30H_a: \mu = 30
  4. H0:x¯=30H_0: \bar{x} = 30, Ha:x¯>30H_a: \bar{x} > 30
Show the answer

H0:μ=30H_0: \mu = 30 and Ha:μ>30H_a: \mu > 30, where μ\mu is the true mean delivery time of all the company's deliveries in the city. The hypothesized mean is μ0=30\mu_0 = 30.

  • H0:μ=30H_0: \mu = 30, Ha:μ≠30H_a: \mu \ne 30: The manager suspects longer times before seeing the data, so the alternative is one-sided: greater than 30.
  • Correct: H0:μ=30H_0: \mu = 30, Ha:μ>30H_a: \mu > 30: The null is the advertised value; the alternative is the manager's suspicion, longer deliveries.
  • H0:μ>30H_0: \mu > 30, Ha:μ=30H_a: \mu = 30: The null hypothesis holds the equals sign and the claim being tested; the suspicion belongs in HaH_a.
  • H0:x¯=30H_0: \bar{x} = 30, Ha:x¯>30H_a: \bar{x} > 30: Hypotheses are about the population mean μ\mu, never the sample mean x¯\bar{x}.

2. Why is a one-sided alternative appropriate here?

  1. The sample mean will turn out to be greater than 30
  2. One-sided tests are easier to reject with
  3. The manager suspected longer delivery times before collecting data
  4. The sample size is larger than 30
Show the answer

A one-sided HaH_a is set by a directional question stated in advance ("is the mean longer than advertised?").

  • The sample mean will turn out to be greater than 30: Choosing the direction from the sample result is a common error; the question decides it.
  • One-sided tests are easier to reject with: Ease of rejecting is not a reason; the research question is.
  • Correct: The manager suspected longer delivery times before collecting data: The direction of HaH_a comes from the question asked before the data, not from the sample result.
  • The sample size is larger than 30: Sample size affects the conditions, not the direction of the alternative.

3. The manager has not graphed the delivery times. Is the normal/large sample condition met?

  1. Yes, because n=45≥30n = 45 \ge 30
  2. No, because delivery times are probably skewed
  3. Yes, because 45 is less than 10% of last month's deliveries
  4. No, because a graph of the sample is required for each test
Show the answer

Normal/large sample: n=45≥30n = 45 \ge 30. (Still, a quick graph is good practice, to catch extreme outliers.)

  • Correct: Yes, because n=45≥30n = 45 \ge 30: With a sample of at least 30, the central limit theorem makes the sampling distribution of x¯\bar{x} approximately normal.
  • No, because delivery times are probably skewed: Some skew is fine with n=45n = 45; only very strong skew or extreme outliers would worry us.
  • Yes, because 45 is less than 10% of last month's deliveries: That is the 10% condition, which is about independence.
  • No, because a graph of the sample is required for each test: A graph of the sample is required only when n<30n < 30 and the population is not known to be normal.

4. A student writes "H0:x¯=30H_0: \bar{x} = 30" for this study. What is wrong?

  1. It should use 45 instead of 30
  2. Nothing, x¯\bar{x} and μ\mu mean the same thing
  3. It should be a two-sided statement
  4. It is about the sample mean, which is known once the data are in
Show the answer

Use μ\mu (the parameter) in hypotheses, never x¯\bar{x}.

  • It should use 45 instead of 30: 45 is the sample size, not the hypothesized mean.
  • Nothing, x¯\bar{x} and μ\mu mean the same thing: x¯\bar{x} is a statistic from one sample; μ\mu is the population parameter.
  • It should be a two-sided statement: The null hypothesis is an equality; the problem is the symbol, not the sides.
  • Correct: It is about the sample mean, which is known once the data are in: Hypotheses describe the population. Write H0:μ=30H_0: \mu = 30, with μ\mu defined in context.

5. A sleep researcher wants to know whether college students at a large university sleep a mean amount different from 8 hours a night. Which hypotheses fit?

  1. H0:μ=8H_0: \mu = 8, Ha:μ≠8H_a: \mu \ne 8
  2. H0:μ=8H_0: \mu = 8, Ha:μ<8H_a: \mu < 8
  3. H0:μ≠8H_0: \mu \ne 8, Ha:μ=8H_a: \mu = 8
  4. H0:μ=7.4H_0: \mu = 7.4, Ha:μ≠7.4H_a: \mu \ne 7.4
Show the answer

H0:μ=8H_0: \mu = 8, Ha:μ≠8H_a: \mu \ne 8, where μ\mu is the true mean nightly sleep of students at the university.

  • Correct: H0:μ=8H_0: \mu = 8, Ha:μ≠8H_a: \mu \ne 8: "Different from" points to a two-sided alternative, with 8 hours as the hypothesized mean.
  • H0:μ=8H_0: \mu = 8, Ha:μ<8H_a: \mu < 8: The question asks about any difference, not only less sleep.
  • H0:μ≠8H_0: \mu \ne 8, Ha:μ=8H_a: \mu = 8: The equality belongs in the null hypothesis.
  • H0:μ=7.4H_0: \mu = 7.4, Ha:μ≠7.4H_a: \mu \ne 7.4: The hypothesized value comes from the question (8 hours), not from a sample result.

6. A study measures reaction time for 20 sets of identical twins; one twin in each set is randomly assigned to drink coffee and the other water. Which analysis fits?

  1. A test comparing two independent groups of 20
  2. A paired t test on the 20 within-twin differences
  3. A one-sample t test on the 20 coffee times alone
  4. A one-proportion z test on the share of faster coffee twins
Show the answer

Matched pairs design: compute coffee minus water within each twin pair and test H0:μd=0H_0: \mu_d = 0 with a paired t test (df 19).

  • A test comparing two independent groups of 20: The twins are matched, so the two groups are not independent.
  • Correct: A paired t test on the 20 within-twin differences: Twins are matched pairs, so each pair gives one difference (coffee minus water), and the test is on the mean difference.
  • A one-sample t test on the 20 coffee times alone: The water twins carry the comparison; leaving them out loses the point of the design.
  • A one-proportion z test on the share of faster coffee twins: That throws away the size of each difference; the reaction times are quantitative.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

To set up a t test for a mean, name the parameter in context, write H0:μ=μ0H_0: \mu = \mu_0 and an alternative whose direction comes from the question, and check random, 10% and normal/large sample. For paired data, test the mean difference μd\mu_d with a paired t test on the differences, saying which way you subtracted.
