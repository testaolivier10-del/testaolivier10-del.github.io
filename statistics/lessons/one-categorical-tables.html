Unit 1 · Topic 1.3

Tabular Representation and Summary Statistics for One Categorical Variable

A frequency table counts each category; a relative frequency divides each count by the total, so relative frequencies are between 0 and 1, add to 1, and compare groups of different sizes.

Practice 3: Analyze DataPractice 4: Interpret Results

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A city newspaper asks 160 adults where they get their news. Fifty-six say social media and 40 say television. A second city asks 500 adults and 130 say television. Which city leans more on television? The counts 40 and 130 cannot answer it, because the samples are different sizes. Dividing each count by its total can.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Each individual falls in one category of a categorical variable→counting each category gives a frequency table
  2. Divide each count by the total→the relative frequencies are parts of the whole, between 0 and 1, and they add to 1
  3. Relative frequencies all divide by their own totals→samples of different sizes can be compared fairly
  4. The relative frequency of a category in a sample is p^\hat{p}→it is a statistic that estimates p, the proportion in the whole population

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • A frequency table lists each category of a categorical variable and its count (frequency).
  • A relative frequency is a count divided by the total. Relative frequencies are between 0 and 1 and add to 1 when categories do not overlap.
  • Count = relative frequency × total, so a relative frequency table needs the total to get back to counts.
  • The sample proportion p^\hat{p} is the relative frequency of a category in a sample, a statistic. The population proportion pp is the same quantity for the whole population, a parameter.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The second city has 130 television viewers against 40, so a larger share of its adults watch television.

What actually happens: Compare relative frequencies: 40160=0.25\frac{40}{160} = 0.25 and 130500=0.26\frac{130}{500} = 0.26. The shares are almost the same; the second sample is simply bigger.

3. Which statement about the relative frequencies of the five news sources is true?

  1. They add to 160, the number of adults who were asked
  2. They add to 1, because each adult is in exactly one category
  3. They are equal to one another, since each adult chose a single source
  4. They add to 100, because each one is a percent of the sample
Show the answer

When categories do not overlap and cover everyone, the relative frequencies add to 1 (or 100%).

  • They add to 160, the number of adults who were asked: The frequencies add to 160. Each relative frequency is a count over 160, so together they add to 1.
  • Correct: They add to 1, because each adult is in exactly one category: Right: every adult chose one main source, so the five parts make up the whole sample.
  • They are equal to one another, since each adult chose a single source: Each adult chose one source, but the sources were chosen different numbers of times.
  • They add to 100, because each one is a percent of the sample: As percents they add to 100%. As relative frequencies (decimals) they add to 1.

4. For print newspapers, p^=12160=0.075\hat{p} = \frac{12}{160} = 0.075. What does p^\hat{p} describe?

  1. The city’s adults, as a population proportion
  2. The 12 adults
  3. The news sources that the city’s adults can choose from
  4. The 160 adults in the sample
Show the answer

A sample proportion p^\hat{p} is the part of the sample in a category. It is a statistic, used to estimate the population proportion p.

  • The city’s adults, as a population proportion: That would be p, the population proportion, which is unknown here.
  • The 12 adults: The 12 are the part; p^\hat{p} compares them with the whole sample of 160.
  • The news sources that the city’s adults can choose from: The sources are the categories; p^\hat{p} is a number about adults in one category.
  • Correct: The 160 adults in the sample: Right: p^\hat{p} is a sample proportion, a statistic. It estimates p for the city.

5. A gym asked 60 members which class they like best: 12 chose yoga, 18 chose cycling and 30 chose weights. What is the relative frequency of cycling?

Type a number. A table answer and a calculator answer are both accepted.

Show the answer

1860=0.30\frac{18}{60} = 0.30, so 30% of the members asked chose cycling.

  • Answer: 0.3

6. A survey asked 200 students, "Which of these apps do you use? Check every one you use." The relative frequencies add to 1.85. What explains this?

  1. Someone added the relative frequencies incorrectly
  2. The survey asked more students than it reported in the total
  3. Students could check more than one app, so categories overlap
  4. Relative frequencies are supposed to add to 2 for a survey
Show the answer

Relative frequencies add to 1 only when each individual falls in exactly one category. With "check every one", students fall in several.

  • Someone added the relative frequencies incorrectly: The sum is correct; it is over 1 because the categories overlap.
  • The survey asked more students than it reported in the total: The total is 200; overlap explains the sum.
  • Correct: Students could check more than one app, so categories overlap: Right: one student can be counted in several categories, so the parts add to more than the whole.
  • Relative frequencies are supposed to add to 2 for a survey: They add to 1 only when each individual is in exactly one category.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

A frequency table counts each category; a relative frequency divides each count by the total, so relative frequencies are between 0 and 1, add to 1, and compare groups of different sizes. In a sample, the relative frequency of a category is the sample proportion p^\hat{p}, a statistic that estimates the population proportion pp.
