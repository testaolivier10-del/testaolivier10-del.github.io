The one-substituent rule

For a monosubstituted cyclohexane, the dominant (major) conformation is always the chair with the substituent equatorial — because equatorial avoids the 1,3-diaxial clashes that axial experiences. The size of the preference is exactly the substituent's A-value. Methylcyclohexane (A-value 1.7 kcal/mol) is about 95% equatorial-methyl at room temperature; tert-butylcyclohexane (A-value ~4.7-5+ kcal/mol) is over 99.9% equatorial.

Multiple substituents: add the A-values (roughly)

With two or more substituents, compare the two chairs by adding up which substituents are axial vs. equatorial in each, and pick whichever chair has less total "axial penalty." For example, in a disubstituted ring, if one chair puts a large group axial and a small group equatorial, and the other chair does the reverse, the chair with the larger group equatorial is strongly favored — its A-value dominates the comparison.

This same equatorial-preference logic explains disaccharide and steroid conformations in biochemistry — the same chair-flip, A-value framework you just learned for simple cyclohexanes governs which shape glucose (a cyclohexane-like ring) actually adopts.

Why this connects directly to E2 elimination

E2 elimination (Module 6) has a strict geometric requirement: the leaving group and the hydrogen being removed must be anti-periplanar — 180° apart, which on a cyclohexane ring means both must be axial (axial-axial is the only 180° relationship on a chair; equatorial groups are never anti-periplanar to each other). This means an E2 reaction on a cyclohexane ring can only happen through whichever of the two interconverting chairs happens to have the leaving group axial — even if that chair is the minority, higher-energy conformation. Conformational analysis is literally how you predict which product forms.
