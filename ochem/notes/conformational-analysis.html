Alkanes & Conformations · Section 31 of 117

Conformational analysis

Practice this — interactive lesson

This section is where the chapter's parts come together into a working procedure: given a substituted cyclohexane, decide which chair it prefers and by how much — and then use that to predict what the molecule will do. The last part is the important one. Conformational analysis is not a descriptive exercise; it is how you predict which product a reaction gives.

The one-substituent rule

For a monosubstituted cyclohexane the answer is always the same: the major conformation is the chair with the substituent equatorial, because equatorial avoids the 1,3-diaxial clashes that axial cannot. The size of the preference is exactly the substituent's A-value.

Methylcyclohexane (A = 1.7) is about 95% equatorial-methyl at room temperature. tert-Butylcyclohexane (A ≈ 4.9) is over 99.9%. There is no case where a lone substituent prefers axial on steric grounds.

Multiple substituents: add the axial penalties

With two or more substituents the procedure is mechanical. Draw both chairs. For each, note which substituents are axial. Add up the A-values of the axial ones — that is that chair's penalty. The chair with the smaller total wins, and the difference between the totals is the preference.

Adding A-values is an approximation, because an A-value only measures a group against hydrogens. Two substituents that end up near each other also interact directly, and there are two corrections worth carrying:

Direct interactionWhere it appearsCost (kcal/mol)
CH₃/CH₃ gauchetwo equatorial groups on adjacent carbons0.9
CH₃/CH₃ 1,3-diaxialtwo axial groups on the same face, 1,33.7

Both are butane's gauche clash turning up on a ring, and both cost more than A-value addition alone would charge: a pair of axial methyls facing each other across the ring is worth 3.7, not the 1.7 you get by treating each one as though its neighbors were hydrogens. Add the A-values first, then look at the drawing for either of these two patterns and add the correction if you see one.

Worked example — trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane, drawn out

The name is not the answer; the drawing is. Run the whole procedure once and it becomes mechanical.

Step 1 — turn the name into faces. Trans means opposite faces, so one methyl is up and one is down. (Handed a wedge/dash hexagon instead, read it straight off: wedge up, hash down.)

Step 2 — draw one chair and number it. Pick any carbon as C1 and go round to C6 in either direction. Number the drawing, not your head.

Step 3 — place each group on its face. On C1 draw the bond that points up and hang a methyl on it; on C2 draw the bond that points down and hang the other methyl on it. The axial directions alternate round the ring, so C1-up and C2-down are the same kind of bond — both axial, or both equatorial.

Step 4 — read the labels. Choose the chair in which C1's up bond is the outward one, and both methyls come out equatorial. Chair A: ee.

Step 5 — flip and re-read. Faces unchanged, labels reversed: both methyls are axial. Chair B: aa.

Step 6 — add up. Chair B carries two axial methyls: 1.7 + 1.7 = 3.4. Chair A carries none, but its two equatorial methyls sit on adjacent carbons and are therefore gauche: 0.9. Chair A wins by 3.4 − 0.9 = 2.5 kcal/mol, roughly 70:1, against a measured 2.7. Naive A-value addition would have said 3.4; the gauche correction is what brings it into line.

Worked example — cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane

Cis on adjacent carbons means both methyls on the same face, which forces one axial and one equatorial in either chair.

Chair A: methyl 1 axial, methyl 2 equatorial. Penalty 1.7. Chair B: the reverse. Penalty 1.7.

The two chairs are equal in energy and equally populated. Neither is preferred, and the molecule is permanently carrying 1.7 kcal/mol of axial strain it cannot escape.

That is why the cis isomer is measurably less stable than the trans isomer of the same compound. Set the totals side by side: cis carries one axial methyl (1.7) plus a gauche interaction between its axial and equatorial methyls (0.9), so 2.6, against trans's 0.9 — a gap of about 1.7, and the measured difference is close to 2 kcal/mol. Two compounds with identical formulas and identical connectivity, differing only in the face a methyl group sits on, and the difference shows up in their heats of combustion.

Worked example — when the groups are different sizes

cis-1-tert-butyl-4-methylcyclohexane. Cis on carbons 1 and 4 means one group axial and one equatorial in either chair.

Chair A: tert-butyl axial (4.9), methyl equatorial. Penalty 4.9. Chair B: methyl axial (1.7), tert-butyl equatorial. Penalty 1.7.

Chair B wins by 3.2 kcal/mol. The bulkier group takes equatorial and the smaller one absorbs the axial cost — which is the general rule whenever you cannot have everything equatorial.

Work out cis/trans before you draw anything. Whether two substituents can both be equatorial is fixed by their cis/trans relationship and their positions on the ring, and getting that wrong invalidates everything downstream. The pattern: on 1,2 and 1,4 rings, trans allows both equatorial; on 1,3 rings, cis allows both equatorial. It flips because the axial directions alternate around the ring.
Worked example — a 1,3 case, where the rule reverses

cis-1,3-dimethylcyclohexane. Same procedure, and the point is that the answer comes out of the drawing rather than out of the pattern you half-remember.

Faces. Cis means the same face: both methyls up.

Draw and place. Number a chair C1–C6. Axial directions alternate: up at C1, down at C2, up at C3. So C1 and C3 are both axial-up carbons — which means if you put both methyls on the up face, either both land axial or both land equatorial, never one of each.

Chair A. Both equatorial. Penalty 0, and no gauche correction, because 1,3 groups are not on adjacent carbons.

Chair B. Both axial, and both pointing up out of the same face — the two methyls stare straight at each other. That is the CH₃/CH₃ 1,3-diaxial interaction: about 3.7 kcal/mol, plus one CH₃/H diaxial contact each. Call it roughly 5.4 kcal/mol.

Verdict. Chair A by a mile; cis-1,3-dimethylcyclohexane is essentially all diequatorial. And note the reversal against the 1,2 case: on 1,3 carbons it is cis that gets both groups equatorial, because the axial directions alternate and 1 and 3 are two steps apart, not one. That is the whole content of the pitfall above — and if you draw the chair you never have to remember it.

This same reasoning governs the shapes of sugars and steroids. Glucose adopts the chair that places every one of its bulky hydroxyl and hydroxymethyl groups equatorial — it is the only common hexose that can manage it, which is a large part of why glucose is the sugar life settled on. The chair-flip and A-value framework you have just learned for methylcyclohexane is exactly the framework used to analyze a steroid nucleus.

Looking ahead: why this connects directly to E2 elimination

Everything from here to the end of the section is a preview. E2, its leaving group, its base and the Zaitsev rule about which alkene it prefers are all taught in Substitution & Elimination; nothing later in this chapter depends on it. It is here because it is the most striking evidence that conformation controls reactivity, and it will read very differently once you have met the mechanism.

E2 elimination (Substitution & Elimination) carries a strict geometric requirement: the leaving group and the hydrogen being removed must be anti-periplanar, 180° apart. On a cyclohexane ring, the only way to achieve 180° between substituents on adjacent carbons is for both to be axial — one axial-up and one axial-down. Two equatorial groups are never anti-periplanar to each other, and neither is an axial/equatorial pair.

So an E2 reaction on a ring can only proceed through whichever chair places the leaving group axial, even if that chair is the minority conformation. The molecule flips into it, reacts, and is replenished by the equilibrium. Conformational analysis is literally how you predict which product forms — and sometimes whether any product forms at all.

Worked example — the classic menthyl chloride comparison

Neomenthyl chloride eliminates with ethoxide rapidly, giving mostly the more substituted (Zaitsev) alkene. Menthyl chloride, the same compound with the chlorine-bearing carbon flipped over, eliminates roughly 200 times more slowly and gives only the less substituted alkene.

The reason is entirely conformational. In neomenthyl chloride, the favored chair already has the chlorine axial, with axial hydrogens available on both neighboring carbons — fast, and free to give the Zaitsev product.

In menthyl chloride, the favored chair has all three substituents equatorial, including the chlorine. To eliminate at all, the ring must flip into a strained triaxial chair, which is why it is slow. And in that chair only one neighboring carbon has an axial hydrogen — the one that gives the less substituted alkene. Geometry overrides the usual product preference completely.

What carries forward

Conformational reasoning reappears wherever geometry gates a reaction: the anti-periplanar requirement of E2, the backside approach of SN2, the trans-diaxial opening of halonium ions and epoxides in Alkenes & Alkynes and Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry, and the stereochemistry of ring reactions generally. More broadly, this chapter establishes a habit worth carrying: when a reaction's outcome seems arbitrary, check whether the molecule can physically adopt the geometry the mechanism requires.
