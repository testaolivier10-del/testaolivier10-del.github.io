Alkanes & Conformations · Section 27 of 121

Conformational analysis

Practice this — interactive lesson

Methylcyclohexane flips between two chairs. In one chair its methyl group is equatorial, and in the other it is axial. At room temperature about 95% of the molecules are in the equatorial chair at any moment. Working out a split like this for any substituted cyclohexane is called conformational analysis: you decide which chair the molecule prefers, and by how much. This page turns that into a procedure you can run on rings you have never seen.

The procedure uses three earlier ideas: the A-value from Axial/equatorial, the flip between two chairs from Ring flips, and the gauche interaction from Newman projections.

One substituent: equatorial wins

Start with methylcyclohexane and put the methyl on C1. In the axial chair the methyl points straight up. The axial hydrogens on C3 and C5 point up on the same face, right beside it, and crowd it. These are its two 1,3-diaxial interactions. In the equatorial chair the methyl points out from the edge of the ring, where nothing crowds it.

The energy gap between the two chairs is the methyl group's A-value, 1.7 kcal/mol. That gap is what sets the 95:5 split.

Every single substituent behaves the same way. The major chair puts the group equatorial, and the size of the preference is the group's A-value. tert-Butylcyclohexane, with an A-value of 4.9 kcal/mol, spends more than 99.9% of its time with the tert-butyl group equatorial. No lone substituent prefers the axial position because of crowding, since the equatorial position always escapes the 1,3-diaxial interactions that the axial one cannot.

Two substituents: first find out which chairs are possible

Take 1,2-dimethylcyclohexane. It exists as two different compounds. In the trans isomer the two methyls are on opposite faces of the ring, and in the cis isomer they are on the same face. A ring flip never moves a group to the other face, so each isomer keeps its relationship in both of its chairs.

The faces decide which chairs can exist. Axial bonds alternate as you go round the ring: up at C1, down at C2, up at C3, and so on. Each carbon's equatorial bond tilts the opposite way from its axial bond. So if the groups on C1 and C2 are both equatorial, the C1 group tilts down and the C2 group tilts up. They are on opposite faces, which makes the pair trans.

Move one carbon further and the result reverses. C3's axial bond points up, like C1's, so equatorial groups on C1 and C3 both tilt down. They share a face, which makes the pair cis. C4's axial bond points down, like C2's, so a 1,4 pair with both groups equatorial is trans again.

The other isomer in each case cannot put both groups equatorial. A cis-1,2 pair, a trans-1,3 pair and a cis-1,4 pair each have one group axial and one equatorial in both chairs.

"Trans means both equatorial" is only half true. It holds for 1,2 and 1,4 pairs. On a 1,3 ring it is the cis isomer that can put both groups equatorial. Settle this before any arithmetic, because every number after it depends on it. If you are unsure, draw the chair and read the answer off it.

Add up the axial penalties

Worked example — cis-1-tert-butyl-4-methylcyclohexane

The two groups are cis on a 1,4 pair, so one of them is axial in either chair. The only question is which one.

One chair puts the tert-butyl group axial. Its penalty is the tert-butyl A-value, 4.9 kcal/mol. The other chair puts the methyl axial instead, for a penalty of 1.7 kcal/mol.

The methyl-axial chair wins by 4.9 − 1.7 = 3.2 kcal/mol.

That example is the whole method. Draw both chairs and note which groups are axial in each. Add up the A-values of the axial groups to get each chair's penalty. The chair with the smaller penalty wins, and the difference between the two penalties is the size of its preference. When the groups cannot all be equatorial, this arithmetic always gives the equatorial position to the group with the larger A-value.

When the two groups touch each other

An A-value measures how much a group is crowded by hydrogens. When two substituents sit close together, they also crowd each other, and adding A-values misses that. Two such contacts come up often enough to learn, and the next two examples meet one each.

Worked example — trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane, step by step

This example runs the whole procedure, one step at a time. The name alone does not give the answer; the drawing does.

Step 1 — turn the name into faces. Trans means opposite faces, so put the C1 methyl down and the C2 methyl up. If you are given a flat ring drawn with wedges and hashes instead, read the faces straight off it: a wedge is up and a hash is down.

Step 2 — draw one chair and number it. Pick any carbon as C1 and number round to C6 in either direction. Write the numbers on the drawing, not in your head.

Step 3 — put each group on its face. On C1, find the bond that points down and put a methyl on it. On C2, find the bond that points up and put the other methyl on it. Because axial directions alternate, C1-down and C2-up are the same kind of bond: both axial, or both equatorial.

Step 4 — read the labels. In the chair drawn below on the left, both methyls are equatorial. Call this chair ee, for equatorial–equatorial.

Step 5 — flip and read again. The faces stay the same and the labels reverse, so in the other chair both methyls are axial. Call it aa.

Step 6 — add up, then look for contacts. The aa chair has two axial methyls: 1.7 + 1.7 = 3.4 kcal/mol. The ee chair has no axial group, but its two methyls sit on neighboring carbons. Look down the C1–C2 bond and they are 60° apart. That is the gauche interaction of butane, carried onto the ring, and it costs 0.9 kcal/mol. In the aa chair the methyls are 180° apart (anti), so they do not touch.

Result. The ee chair wins by 3.4 − 0.9 = 2.5 kcal/mol, roughly 70:1. The measured value is 2.7. Plain A-value addition would have said 3.4, so the gauche correction is what brings the estimate close to the measurement.

Worked example — cis-1,3-dimethylcyclohexane

Faces. Cis means the same face, so put both methyls up.

Place them. C1 and C3 have the same axial direction, so two groups on the same face are either both axial or both equatorial, never one of each. That gives an ee chair and an aa chair.

The ee chair. Neither methyl is axial, so its penalty is 0. There is no gauche correction either, because the methyls are not on neighboring carbons.

The aa chair. C1, C3 and C5 all have axial bonds pointing up, so both methyls and the axial hydrogen on C5 share the upper face. The two methyls sit side by side, much closer together than a gauche pair, and this contact costs about 3.7 kcal/mol. It is called a syn-pentane interaction, after the five-carbon chain CH₃–C1–C2–C3–CH₃, whose two ends it pushes against each other.

Add it up. Plain A-value addition would charge 1.7 + 1.7 = 3.4 kcal/mol. That sum assumes each axial methyl has two axial hydrogens beside it. Here each methyl has only one, on C5, and the other neighbor is the second methyl. An axial methyl's 1.7 comes from its two hydrogen contacts, so each contact is worth about 0.85. The aa chair's real total is 3.7 + 0.85 + 0.85 ≈ 5.4 kcal/mol.

Result. The ee chair wins by about 5.4 kcal/mol, so cis-1,3-dimethylcyclohexane is almost entirely diequatorial.

Comparing a cis isomer with its trans isomer

Worked example — cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane

Cis on neighboring carbons puts both methyls on the same face, which forces one axial and one equatorial in either chair.

In one chair the C1 methyl is axial and the C2 methyl is equatorial. In the other it is the reverse. Each chair pays 1.7 kcal/mol for its axial methyl. Each also pays 0.9 for a gauche contact, because an axial and an equatorial group on neighboring carbons are 60° apart too. Both chairs total 2.6 kcal/mol.

The two chairs are equal in energy and equally populated. Neither is preferred, and the molecule always carries one axial methyl it cannot get rid of.

That makes the cis isomer less stable than the trans isomer. The trans isomer's best chair carries only its 0.9 gauche contact, so the gap is about 2.6 − 0.9 = 1.7 kcal/mol, and the measured difference is close to 2. The two compounds have the same formula and the same connections, and differ only in which face one methyl is on. The difference still shows up in their heats of combustion (see Cyclohexanes): burned completely, the cis isomer releases more heat.

The same reasoning explains the shape of sugars. Glucose exists mostly as a six-membered ring of five carbons and one oxygen. In its β form (β names which way the OH group on C1 points; Biomolecules explains it), one chair puts all four OH groups and the CH₂OH group equatorial, and leaves only hydrogens axial. β-Glucose is the only common six-carbon sugar that can manage this, a fact often offered as one reason glucose is so widespread in living things. Steroids, which are built from four fused rings, are analyzed with the same chairs and A-values.

What carries forward

Which chair a molecule sits in also decides what it can do, because a reaction can only happen if the molecule can reach the shape its mechanism needs. Looking ahead (nothing on this page depends on it): the E2 reaction in Substitution & Elimination makes a C=C double bond by removing an H and a second group from neighboring carbons. It needs those two to be anti-periplanar, the 180° arrangement from Newman projections. On a cyclohexane ring that means both must be axial. So the chair analysis on this page decides how fast an elimination on a ring goes and which alkene it gives.

The same question returns wherever geometry controls a reaction: the one-step substitution in Substitution & Elimination, where the incoming group must arrive on the side opposite the departing one, and the opening of three-membered rings fused to a cyclohexane in Alkenes & Alkynes and Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry, which puts the two new groups trans and diaxial. When a reaction's outcome seems arbitrary, check whether the molecule can physically reach the geometry its mechanism requires.
