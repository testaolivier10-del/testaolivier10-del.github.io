The definition

Enantiomers are a pair of stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other — exactly the chirality relationship from the first lesson, now applied to a specific pair of molecules. A single stereocenter has exactly two possible configurations, and a molecule with one stereocenter and its mirror image are enantiomers of each other.

Identical physical properties — with one exception

Enantiomers have identical melting points, boiling points, density, solubility, and reactivity with achiral reagents — every ordinary physical measurement gives the same answer for both. The one exception: they rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions by the same magnitude. This property is called optical activity, and it's historically how enantiomers were first discovered and told apart, long before anyone understood molecular structure.

A molecule that rotates polarized light clockwise (viewed facing the oncoming beam) is labeled (+) or d (dextrorotatory); one that rotates it counterclockwise is (−) or l (levorotatory). This (+)/(−) label describes an experimentally measured direction of rotation and has no fixed relationship to the R/S configuration label you'll learn in a later lesson — you cannot predict (+) or (−) just by looking at a structure.

Specific rotation: a quantitative measurement

The exact amount of rotation is reported as specific rotation, [α], measured in a polarimeter and normalized for concentration and path length: [α] = α / (l × c), where α is the observed rotation in degrees, l is the path length in decimeters, and c is concentration in g/mL. Pure (+)-enantiomer and pure (−)-enantiomer of the same compound have specific rotations that are exact opposites (equal magnitude, opposite sign).

Why they behave differently in biology

Even though enantiomers are identical in an achiral environment, they can behave completely differently in a chiral environment — like the active site of an enzyme, which is itself chiral. Only one enantiomer may fit properly, the same way only one of your hands fits comfortably into a right-handed glove. This is the molecular basis for why drug enantiomers can have dramatically different biological effects, even though they'd be indistinguishable by melting point or boiling point.
