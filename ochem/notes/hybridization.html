Hybridization

Practice this — interactive lesson

Hybridization is the idea that makes structural organic chemistry predictable. Once you can look at an atom and name its hybridization in two seconds, you get its geometry, its bond angles, whether it is flat or pyramidal, whether its lone pair is available for donation, and how acidic its hydrogens are — all from one label.

Resolving the carbon paradox

Ground-state carbon is 1s² 2s² 2p², with only two unpaired electrons, which predicts two bonds. Carbon forms four. The resolution has two steps.

First, promotion. It costs a modest amount of energy to move one of the paired 2s electrons up into the empty third 2p orbital, giving 2s¹ 2p³ — four unpaired electrons, one per orbital. That energy cost is repaid about twice over by the two extra bonds carbon can now form: promotion costs roughly 96 kcal/mol, and each C–H bond formed releases roughly 100, so two extra bonds bring in about 200 against a bill of 96. Bonding is so profitable that promotion happens essentially every time carbon bonds.

Second, hybridization. Carbon now has four orbitals available, but they are not equivalent: one spherical s and three dumbbell p orbitals at right angles. If carbon bonded with them as-is, methane would have three bonds at 90° and one pointing in some fourth direction, and one bond would be measurably different from the others. Methane has four identical bonds at 109.5°. So the atom mathematically mixes the four orbitals into four new, identical ones that point as far apart from each other as possible — toward the corners of a tetrahedron. These are sp³ orbitals: one part s, three parts p.

The three hybridizations, and what gets left over

The same mixing scales down, and what does not get mixed matters as much as what does.

  • Mix 1 s with 3 p → four sp³ orbitals, 109.5° apart, tetrahedral. 0 p orbitals left over.
  • Mix 1 s with 2 p → three sp² orbitals, 120° apart, all in one plane (trigonal planar). 1 p orbital left over, standing perpendicular to that plane.
  • Mix 1 s with 1 p → two sp orbitals, 180° apart, linear. 2 p orbitals left over, both perpendicular to the axis and to each other.

Those leftover, unhybridized p orbitals are the whole point. They are what form pi bonds in the next section: a double bond needs one leftover p on each atom (so sp²), and a triple bond needs two (so sp). A leftover p orbital is also where a lone pair sits when it needs to overlap sideways with a neighboring pi bond rather than stay put on its own atom — the mechanism behind resonance, amide planarity, and aromaticity.

4 groups = sp³ (0 leftover p)  ·  3 groups = sp² (1 leftover p)  ·  2 groups = sp (2 leftover p)

The shortcut you will actually use

You do not redo promotion and mixing for every atom. You count electron groups and read off the answer. An electron group is any of: a sigma bond — the ordinary head-on connection between two atoms, which the next section names and explains — or a lone pair. Crucially, a double or triple bond counts as one group, not two or three, because it all points toward a single neighbor and so occupies a single direction in space.

Worked examples — counting groups

Methane, CH₄. The carbon has 4 sigma bonds and 0 lone pairs → 4 groups → sp³, tetrahedral, 109.5°.

Ethene, CH₂=CH₂. Each carbon has 2 sigma bonds to H, plus 1 bond to the other carbon (the double bond counts once) → 3 groups → sp², trigonal planar, 120°. The leftover p orbital on each carbon is what makes the pi bond.

Carbon dioxide, O=C=O. The carbon has two double bonds, each counting once → 2 groups → sp, linear, 180°.

Ammonia, NH₃. Nitrogen has 3 bonds plus 1 lone pair → 4 groups → sp³. The lone pair counts. This is why ammonia is pyramidal rather than flat, and why that lone pair points in a definite direction rather than being smeared over a flat molecule — it is aimed, and a lone pair that is aimed can go and do something.

Water, H₂O. Oxygen has 2 bonds plus 2 lone pairs → 4 groups → sp³, bent. The measured angle is 104.5° — noticeably under the ideal 109.5°, because two lone pairs take more room than two bonds would. Molecular Geometry, two sections on, explains the size of that squeeze.

A carbocation, CH₃⁺. The carbon has 3 sigma bonds and no lone pair — the pair it lost is simply gone → 3 groups → sp², trigonal planar, 120°, with the leftover p orbital empty. That empty p orbital, standing perpendicular to a flat carbon, is what makes a carbocation so reactive and why it can be attacked from either face.

A carbanion, CH₃⁻. Three sigma bonds plus 1 lone pair → 4 groups → sp³, pyramidal, with the lone pair in the fourth corner. (It is the mirror image of the cation: same three bonds, but the pair stayed.)

A radical, CH₃•. Three bonds plus one single electron. A lone electron is much less demanding of space than a pair, so a methyl radical sits close to sp² and nearly flat — the one case where the group count needs a caveat rather than an answer.

Worked example — every atom in one molecule

Exams rarely ask for one center. They give a molecule and ask for the hybridization of every non-hydrogen atom in it. The method does not change; you just run the count several times.

Acetonitrile, CH₃C≡N. The methyl carbon: 3 bonds to H plus 1 to carbon = 4 groups → sp³. The nitrile carbon: 1 bond to the methyl carbon, plus the triple bond counting once = 2 groups → sp. The nitrogen: the triple bond counts once, plus 1 lone pair = 2 groups → sp. So the C–C≡N unit is linear and the methyl end is tetrahedral, in one molecule.

Acetic acid, CH₃COOH. The methyl carbon: 4 groups → sp³. The carbonyl carbon: bond to CH₃, bond to OH, and the C=O counting once = 3 groups → sp², so that carbon and its three neighbors are flat. The OH oxygen: 2 bonds (to the carbonyl carbon and to H) plus 2 lone pairs = 4 groups → nominally sp³. The C=O oxygen: 1 group from the double bond plus 2 lone pairs = 3 groups → sp².

Propenal, CH₂=CH–CHO. Both alkene carbons: 3 groups each → sp². The aldehyde carbon: bond to the neighboring carbon, bond to H, C=O counting once = 3 groups → sp². The oxygen: 3 groups → sp². Every heavy atom in the molecule is sp², which is a different way of saying the whole thing is flat — and that flatness is why its two π systems can talk to each other in Resonance.

A labeled preview: the one place the shortcut fails. Count groups on the nitrogen of an amide (RCO–NH₂) and you get 3 bonds plus 1 lone pair = 4 groups = sp³, pyramidal. The nitrogen is measurably flat, and it is sp². The reason is the sentence earlier in this section about what leftover p orbitals are for: a lone pair sitting next to a π bond does better by moving into a p orbital and overlapping sideways with it than by staying in a hybrid orbital of its own. When it does, the atom holding it has only three hybrid orbitals left and is sp². The same thing happens to the nitrogen of aniline and of pyrrole and to one oxygen of an ester or an enolate. You are not expected to spot this yet — you are expected to know the shortcut has this one exception, so that when Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons explains conjugation you recognize the case rather than trusting the count. Rule of thumb: lone pair next to a π bond → assume sp², not sp³.
The second slip, after forgetting lone pairs: counting a double bond as two groups. It goes to one neighbor, so it occupies one direction and counts once.

What hybridization buys you: s-character

An sp orbital is 50% s in character, sp² is 33%, sp³ is 25%. Since s orbitals hold electrons closer to the nucleus than p orbitals do, more s-character means electrons held tighter and closer in. Three consequences follow, and all three are examinable.

Bond length and strength. More s-character gives shorter, stronger bonds: sp > sp² > sp³. A C–H bond on an sp carbon is about 106 pm; on an sp³ carbon, about 109 pm.

Electronegativity. An sp carbon pulls harder on the electrons in its bonds than an sp³ carbon does — it holds them closer in. Two sections from here that pull gets a name, electronegativity, and is normally treated as a fixed property of each element; this is the exception, where the same element changes its grip depending on how it is hybridized.

Acidity — a labeled preview, because the scale itself belongs to Acids & Bases. Pulling H⁺ off a carbon leaves the bonding pair behind on that carbon, so "how acidic is this C–H?" is really "how comfortably does this carbon hold the leftover pair?" — and the pair sits in the same hybrid orbital that held the bond. More s-character means that orbital is built more from an orbital centered on the nucleus, so it holds the pair closer in and more willingly. Acidity is measured as pKa, where lower means more acidic and each unit is a factor of ten. Three C–H bonds differing in nothing but hybridization: alkyne (sp) ≈ 25, alkene (sp²) ≈ 44, alkane (sp³) ≈ 50 — twenty-five powers of ten from the mixing alone.

The practical payoff, in one line: ordinary reagents will take the hydrogen off a terminal alkyne and nothing reasonable will take one off an alkane. Alkenes & Alkynes turns that into a carbon–carbon bond-forming reaction, and this is the fact it rests on.

Beyond four groups

Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen never exceed four electron groups, for the reason Orbitals gave: the n = 2 shell has only four orbitals to mix, so there is no fifth hybrid to make. Period 3 and beyond sometimes manage five or six — PCl₅, SF₆ — and you will see those labeled "sp³d" and "sp³d²" in older books. That label has been retired, for reasons the Lewis Structures section sets out under the octet-rule exceptions; it is stated once there rather than repeated here. The practical rule is all you need while assigning hybridization: count groups, and if the count exceeds four you are not looking at a carbon, nitrogen or oxygen.

What carries forward

Naming hybridization from an electron-group count is a reflex you will use on nearly every page from here on. It gives you geometry in Molecular Geometry, it explains pi bonding in the next section, it determines whether a lone pair can join a conjugated system in Resonance, it sets alkyne acidity in Acids & Bases, and it is the test for whether a ring can be aromatic in Aromatic Chemistry.
