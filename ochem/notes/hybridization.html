Resolving the carbon paradox

Ground-state carbon (1s² 2s² 2p²) has only 2 unpaired electrons. It takes a small amount of energy to promote one paired 2s electron up into the empty third 2p orbital, giving 2s¹ 2p³ — 4 unpaired electrons, one per orbital. That energy cost is paid back by forming 2 extra bonds, so it happens essentially every time carbon bonds.

What "hybridizing" actually means

Carbon now has 4 orbitals to bond with, but they're not identical (1 s-shaped, 3 dumbbell p's) — that would make lopsided, unequal bonds. Instead the atom mathematically mixes them into 4 new, identical orbitals pointing as far apart as possible (109.5°, toward a tetrahedron's corners): sp³ orbitals (1 s + 3 p).

The same scales down: 1 s + 2 p mixed together gives 3 equivalent sp² orbitals (120° apart, flat) with 1 p orbital left over, unmixed. 1 s + 1 p gives 2 equivalent sp orbitals (180°, linear) with 2 p orbitals left over. Those leftover, unhybridized p orbitals are what go on to form pi bonds (next lesson) — a double bond needs 1 leftover p (sp² carbon), a triple bond needs 2 (sp carbon).

4 groups = sp³ (0 leftover p)  ·  3 groups = sp² (1 leftover p)  ·  2 groups = sp (2 leftover p)

The shortcut

You don't redo the promotion-and-mixing every time — just count an atom's electron groups (sigma bonds + lone pairs; a double/triple bond still counts as one group since it goes to one neighbor) and hybridization follows from the table above. Example: methane's carbon has 4 sigma bonds, 0 lone pairs → 4 groups → sp³. CO₂'s carbon has 2 double bonds → 2 groups → sp. Ammonia's nitrogen has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair → 4 groups → sp³ (lone pairs count as groups too).

More s-character means shorter, stronger bonds: sp > sp² > sp³.

Beyond 4 groups

Carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen never exceed 4 groups — period 2 is simply too small. Period 3+ elements like sulfur and phosphorus sometimes do go further: 5 groups gives the trigonal bipyramidal shape seen in PCl₅, and 6 groups gives the octahedral shape seen in SF₆. Textbooks have long labeled these "sp³d" and "sp³d²" hybridization, as if empty d orbitals mixed in the same way p orbitals do for sp³. Modern computational studies show that is not what happens — real d-orbital involvement here is minimal. The geometry is better explained by two things that have nothing to do with d orbitals: the central atom is simply larger, so there is room for extra neighbors; and the extra bonds carry real ionic character, so the central atom is not truly sharing a full covalent octet's worth of electrons with each one. Either way, the resulting shapes and the "S/P can exceed an octet, C/N/O never can" rule both still hold.
