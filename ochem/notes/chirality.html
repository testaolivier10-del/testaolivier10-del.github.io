The definition

A molecule (or any object) is chiral if it is not superimposable on its mirror image — you can't rotate or slide one onto the other so every atom lines up. If an object can be superimposed on its mirror image, it's achiral.

Hands: the classic example

Your left and right hands are mirror images of each other, but you can't rotate your left hand in space to make it look exactly like your right hand (try it — the thumb always ends up on the wrong side). This is exactly what "chiral" means, and it's where the word comes from (Greek cheir, "hand"). A plain coffee mug, by contrast, is achiral — its mirror image is identical to the original, just rotated.

"Chiral" describes the whole molecule's three-dimensional shape, not any single atom by itself. The next lesson introduces stereocenters, the specific atoms that most commonly cause a molecule to be chiral — but chirality itself is a property of the whole structure.

The plane-of-symmetry shortcut

A fast, reliable test: if a molecule has an internal plane of symmetry (an imaginary flat plane that divides it into two mirror-image halves), it is achiral. If no such plane exists anywhere in the molecule, it's chiral. This is a shortcut for the superimposability test, and it usually matches your intuition once you've practiced it a few times.

Why this matters biologically

Almost all of biology is built from chiral molecules — amino acids, sugars, DNA's helix. A chiral biological receptor can tell two mirror-image molecules apart, which is why one enantiomer of a drug can be therapeutic while its mirror image is inactive or even harmful (thalidomide is the most famous historical example). You'll see exactly how enantiomers differ in their biological interactions in an upcoming lesson.
