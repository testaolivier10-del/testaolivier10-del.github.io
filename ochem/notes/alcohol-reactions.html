Alcohol reactions

Practice this — interactive lesson

Alcohols sit at the center of organic synthesis. They can be made from alkenes, from carbonyls, from halides; they can be turned into halides, alkenes, ethers, esters, aldehydes, ketones and acids. Almost every synthesis route in this course passes through one. The obstacle is that an alcohol cannot react at its C–O bond until something is done about the hydroxyl group.

Recall: the OH has to be activated first

This much you already have from How Reactions Happen: hydroxide is the conjugate base of water, pKa 15.7, so it is a strong base and a hopeless leaving group, and an alcohol will not do SN1, SN2, E1 or E2 at its C–O bond until that is fixed. Two moves fix it — protonate the oxygen, so that what actually departs is neutral water, or replace the hydroxyl with a tosylate, which is made at sulfur and so leaves any stereocenter untouched. Everything in this section is what becomes possible once one of those two has been done, so if you cannot say which one a set of reagents is using, start there.

Tosylation is the method of choice when the rest of the molecule cannot tolerate strong acid, or when you want a clean, single SN2 inversion at a known stereocenter. Protonation is cheaper and simpler, but it exposes everything else in the molecule to acid and it invites carbocation chemistry with all its rearrangements.

Converting alcohols to halides

Three routes, and the choice matters.

HX directly. Tertiary alcohols react readily with HCl or HBr by SN1; primary alcohols need more forcing conditions and go by SN2. Secondary alcohols are in between and prone to rearrangement, which is the main drawback.

SOCl₂ converts ROH to RCl, and PBr₃ converts ROH to RBr. Both work under mild, non-acidic conditions, both invert the carbon, and crucially neither goes through a carbocation, so neither rearranges. When a secondary alcohol has to become a halide with its skeleton intact, these are the reagents.

The reason they behave that way is worth having, because it is the same activation idea in a different costume. The alcohol oxygen is the nucleophile in the first step: its lone pair attacks the sulfur of SOCl₂ (or the phosphorus of PBr₃), and after a chloride is expelled the oxygen is left carrying –SOCl, as an alkyl chlorosulfite, R–O–SOCl. That group is an excellent leaving group — it departs as SO₂ and chloride, two stable neutral-or-weak-base pieces — so the chloride released in the first step can now do an ordinary backside SN2 on the carbon. No positive charge is ever built on carbon, which is exactly why nothing shifts. PBr₃ runs the same play: the oxygen attacks phosphorus to give R–O–PBr₂, and bromide displaces the phosphorus-bearing oxygen from the back.

One caveat on the stereochemistry, because exam questions turn on it: SOCl₂ with pyridine gives clean inversion, and that is the form questions almost always specify. Run without a base, SOCl₂ can instead collapse internally — the chlorine delivered to the same face the leaving group is leaving from (the SNi pathway) — and give retention. Read the conditions before committing to an answer.

Rearrangement is the standard trap. Any acid-mediated route through a carbocation can shift a hydride or methyl group before the nucleophile arrives. 3,3-Dimethylbutan-2-ol treated with HBr gives largely the rearranged tertiary bromide, not the one you would draw by swapping OH for Br. If a question specifies PBr₃ or SOCl₂, the point of the specification is usually that rearrangement has been avoided.
Worked example — the trap, drawn out

Question. 3,3-Dimethylbutan-2-ol with (a) HBr, and (b) PBr₃. Same substrate, two reagents.

Step 1 — what does the reagent do to the OH? HBr protonates it, so the leaving group is water and the carbon is on its own once water goes. PBr₃ converts it to R–O–PBr₂, and bromide then attacks that carbon directly. Already the two routes have parted company: one makes a cation, the other never does.

Step 2 — follow the HBr route. Water leaves C2, giving a secondary cation. Look at its neighbor: C3 is quaternary, carrying three methyls. It has no hydrogen to send across, but it can send a whole methyl group with its bonding pair — a 1,2-methyl shift. Doing so moves the positive charge onto C3, which is now tertiary. Secondary to tertiary is downhill, and the shift is faster than bromide's arrival.

Step 3 — capture. Bromide adds to the tertiary cation, so the product is 2-bromo-2,3-dimethylbutane: the bromine is not on the carbon that carried the OH.

Step 4 — now the PBr₃ route. There is no cation at any point, so there is nothing to rearrange. Bromide attacks C2 from the back, giving 2-bromo-3,3-dimethylbutane with inverted configuration and the skeleton untouched.

The habit to build: whenever an acid route leaves a 1° or 2° cation next to a more substituted carbon, check for a shift before you write the product. If the question hands you PBr₃ or SOCl₂ instead, that check is exactly what it is telling you to skip.

Dehydration: elimination of water

Under strong acid and heat — concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄ — a protonated alcohol ionizes to a carbocation and then loses a beta hydrogen. The mechanism is E1, it follows Zaitsev's rule to give the more substituted alkene, and it carries the same rearrangement risk as any carbocation reaction.

Reactivity follows 3° > 2° > 1°, matching carbocation stability. Primary alcohols are reluctant and, when forced, proceed by a concerted E2-like pathway rather than through a primary cation.

This is the exact reverse of the acid-catalyzed hydration from Alkenes & Alkynes, with the same intermediates in the same order. Which direction runs is a matter of conditions: dilute acid with excess water gives the alcohol, while concentrated acid with heat and the alkene distilled off as it forms gives the alkene. Le Châtelier's principle, used deliberately.

The alkene can rearrange too. The cation is the same cation, so everything from Substitution & Elimination applies: if a hydride or methyl shift gives a more stable cation, it happens first, and Zaitsev is then applied to the rearranged cation. That is how 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol dehydrates to 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene: the methyl shift moves the charge from C2 to C3, and the β-hydrogen is then lost from C2, so the double bond ends up between the carbon that held the OH and the carbon that held the methyl — a tetrasubstituted alkene the unrearranged cation could never have given. Rearrange first, then pick the most substituted alkene.

For a substrate that cannot survive strong acid, POCl₃ in pyridine does the same job under mild, basic conditions. The alcohol oxygen attacks phosphorus, converting OH into a dichlorophosphate ester — a good leaving group installed without any acid — and pyridine then removes the β-hydrogen anti-periplanar to it in one concerted E2 step. Pyridine is doing two jobs: it is the base for that step, and it mops up the HCl released while the ester is formed. Because no cation is ever made, POCl₃/pyridine does not rearrange, and it is the reagent of choice when the acid route would scramble the skeleton.

Oxidation, in one line

Oxidation at the carbinol carbon means trading a C–H bond for a bond to oxygen, so how far an alcohol can climb is settled by counting: a primary alcohol has two C–H bonds there and can go up twice, a secondary one has one, a tertiary one has none and simply does not react. Which reagent stops where — PCC and Swern at the aldehyde, aqueous Cr(VI) all the way to the acid, and why water makes the difference — is the subject of Oxidation & Reduction.

Worked example — one alcohol, four products

Start with butan-2-ol, a secondary alcohol, and let the reagent decide.

PBr₃ → 2-bromobutane. R–O–PBr₂ first, then backside attack by bromide: inversion at C2, and no cation, so the skeleton cannot move.

SOCl₂, pyridine → 2-chlorobutane. The same logic one halogen across: chlorosulfite, backside attack by chloride, inversion, no rearrangement.

Concentrated H₂SO₄, heat → but-2-ene. Protonate, lose water to the secondary cation, then lose a β-hydrogen; Zaitsev picks the internal alkene over but-1-ene because it is disubstituted rather than mono.

TsCl/pyridine, then NaCN → 2-methylbutanenitrile. Tosylation happens at sulfur, so C2 keeps its configuration; cyanide then does one clean SN2 on the tosylate, and the single inversion is entirely that step's doing.

Four transformations of one substrate, each chosen by reagent, and three different answers to the question "does this carbon keep its configuration?" Recognizing which reagent buys which outcome is most of what synthesis planning consists of.

The other reactions

Alcohols are weak acids, pKa around 16, so a strong base such as NaH or sodium metal deprotonates them to alkoxides — better nucleophiles and stronger bases, and the starting material for the Williamson ether synthesis in the next section. Alcohols also react with carboxylic acids and their derivatives to form esters, which is Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives’s chemistry seen from the alcohol's side.

"Around 16" hides a trend worth knowing, because it runs the opposite way to most students' guess: methanol 15.5, water 15.7, ethanol 16, tert-butanol 18. Methanol is more acidic than water, and acidity falls as alkyl groups are added. Two effects point the same way — alkyl groups are weakly electron-donating, which destabilizes the negative charge on the alkoxide, and, more importantly in solution, they wrap the anion in bulk and keep water molecules from solvating it. The bulkier the alkoxide, the worse solvated and the less stable it is, and the weaker its parent acid. So "alcohols are weaker acids than water" is a reasonable rule of thumb for anything from ethanol upwards, and simply false for methanol.

And the alcohols themselves have to come from somewhere. You have already made them three ways without calling it that: acid-catalyzed hydration and hydroboration–oxidation of alkenes in Alkenes & Alkynes, and SN2 of hydroxide on a primary halide in Substitution & Elimination. The fourth and most useful route — adding a carbon nucleophile to a carbonyl, or reducing one — arrives in Carbonyl Chemistry.

What carries forward

Alcohol activation is a reflex you will use in every multi-step synthesis from here on: you cannot do anything at that carbon until the OH becomes a leaving group. Oxidation connects this chapter to Carbonyl Chemistry and Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives — every aldehyde and ketone you meet there can be traced back to an alcohol. And alkoxides made here are the nucleophiles of the next section.
