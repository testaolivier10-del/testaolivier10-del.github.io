Why OH can't just leave

From the Leaving Groups lesson: a good leaving group is the conjugate base of a strong acid — stable once it takes the electrons and leaves. Hydroxide (OH⁻) is the conjugate base of water (pKa ≈ 15.7), a very weak acid, which makes OH⁻ a strong, unstable base and a terrible leaving group. An alcohol cannot undergo SN1, SN2, E1, or E2 directly at the C–O bond until OH is converted into something that leaves more easily.

Two ways to activate the leaving group

Protonation: under acidic conditions (H₂SO₄, HBr, etc.), the alcohol's oxygen is protonated first, turning OH into OH₂⁺ — now the leaving group is neutral water, the conjugate base of H₃O⁺ (pKa ≈ −1.7), an excellent leaving group. This is how alcohols undergo SN1/E1 (via protonation, ionization to a carbocation, then substitution or elimination — often via acid-catalyzed dehydration for E1) or SN2 (as in the classic ROH + HX → RX reaction for primary alcohols). Sulfonate ester formation: reacting the alcohol with tosyl chloride (TsCl) or mesyl chloride (MsCl) converts OH into a tosylate (OTs) or mesylate (OMs) — outstanding leaving groups (conjugate bases of very strong sulfonic acids) — without ever touching the C–O bond's stereochemistry, so a stereocenter's configuration is fully preserved through the tosylation step itself (any inversion happens later, in the substitution).

Tosylation is the go-to method when you need to convert an alcohol to a leaving group under mild, non-acidic conditions — especially useful when the rest of the molecule can't tolerate strong acid, or when you specifically want a clean SN2 inversion at a known stereocenter.

Dehydration: E1 (usually) elimination of water

Under strong acid and heat (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄), a protonated alcohol ionizes to a carbocation and then loses a beta-hydrogen — an E1 mechanism, following Zaitsev's rule (from the E1 lesson) to give the more substituted alkene as the major product, and subject to the same carbocation rearrangement risk. This is the reverse reaction of acid-catalyzed hydration from the previous module.

Oxidation: a ladder, not a single step

Oxidation state increases by removing C–H bonds and/or adding C–O bonds. A primary alcohol (1°) oxidizes first to an aldehyde, then further to a carboxylic acid — strong oxidants (Na₂Cr₂O₇, KMnO₄, or the Jones reagent CrO₃/H₂SO₄) push all the way to the acid, while a milder, selective oxidant (PCC, pyridinium chlorochromate) stops cleanly at the aldehyde. A secondary alcohol (2°) oxidizes once, to a ketone, and cannot go further (no C–H left on that carbon to remove). A tertiary alcohol (3°) has no H on the carbinol carbon at all, so it cannot be oxidized under these conditions.
