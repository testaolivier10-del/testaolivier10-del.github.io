Alkenes & Alkynes · Section 49 of 121

Alkene structure

Practice this — interactive lesson

Substitution & Elimination was about carbons bearing leaving groups. From here the course shifts to functional groups that react because of what they have rather than what they can lose, and the alkene is the first of them. An alkene's pi bond is a pool of exposed, loosely held electron density — which makes the alkene a nucleophile, and reverses the role carbon has been playing for the last chapter.

sp² carbons and the pi bond

Each carbon of a C=C is sp² hybridized: three sp² orbitals form sigma bonds in a trigonal planar arrangement 120° apart, and one unhybridized p orbital is left over, perpendicular to that plane. The two leftover p orbitals overlap side-by-side above and below the sigma framework, forming the pi bond.

Some numbers worth having. A C=C is about 134 pm long against 154 pm for C–C, and worth roughly 147 kcal/mol against 83. The sigma component accounts for about 83 of that, so the pi bond is worth about 65 kcal/mol — strong enough to matter, and weak enough to be the part that breaks first in every reaction in this chapter.

Why the double bond is flat and rigid

A sigma bond rotates freely because its overlap is cylindrically symmetric about the bond axis. A pi bond cannot: rotating one carbon 90° relative to the other would destroy the side-by-side p-orbital overlap entirely. Every atom directly attached to a C=C is therefore locked in the same plane, and the two ends cannot swap.

Rotating about a C=C costs roughly 65 kcal/mol — the entire strength of the pi bond — against essentially zero for a C–C single bond. That is why alkene geometry is configurational, a permanent structural identity, rather than conformational, a freely changing shape. Cis and trans alkenes are different compounds you can put in separate bottles, not two shapes of one compound.

Where the double bond is, and which way it faces

Two things have to be said about a double bond before anything reacts with it: where it sits in the chain, and which way its substituents face. The first is nomenclature from IUPAC Nomenclature: pick the longest chain that contains the C=C, number it from whichever end reaches the double bond first, and cite the lower of the two carbons it spans — so CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃ is pent-2-ene, not pent-3-ene. In a ring the two doubly bonded carbons are C1 and C2 by definition, and the numbering then runs around the ring in whichever direction gives the substituents the lower locants: 1-methylcyclohexene has its methyl on one of the alkene carbons, 3-methylcyclohexene two carbons away.

The second is geometry, and it needs its own notation because the locant cannot carry it.

You already have the notation for it. Cis and trans name the two arrangements when each alkene carbon carries one hydrogen and one other group — same side and opposite sides respectively — and E/Z generalizes them by ranking the two groups on each alkene carbon by CIP priority and comparing only the two winners: same side is Z, opposite sides E. Both, along with the trap that E is not a synonym for trans, are built in the Cis/trans and E/Z section of Stereochemistry. Every stereochemical result in this chapter is reported in those letters, so if the assignment is not yet automatic, that is the section to go back to.

Degrees of unsaturation

Each ring or pi bond removes exactly two hydrogens relative to the saturated formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, which lets you read structural information straight off a molecular formula:

DoU = (2C + 2 + N − H − X) / 2

Halogens count like hydrogens; oxygen is ignored entirely, because inserting an oxygen into a chain changes no hydrogen count.

C₄H₈ gives DoU = (8 + 2 − 8)/2 = 1: one ring or one pi bond, consistent with an alkene or a cyclobutane. C₆H₆ gives 4 — three pi bonds and a ring, which is benzene. This is the first thing to compute when a spectroscopy problem hands you a molecular formula in Spectroscopy, because it tells you immediately whether to look for a ring, a carbonyl or an aromatic system.

Worked example — formulas with nitrogen and halogen in them

Both of the corrections in the formula are easy to state and easy to forget, so run one of each.

C₄H₇Br. A halogen caps a chain exactly the way a hydrogen does, so count it with the hydrogens: DoU = (2×4 + 2 + 0 − 7 − 1)/2 = (10 − 8)/2 = 1. One ring or one pi bond — bromocyclobutane and 4-bromo-1-butene both fit.

C₅H₁₁N. A nitrogen brings a third bond, which lets the chain carry one extra hydrogen, so nitrogen is added to the top: DoU = (2×5 + 2 + 1 − 11)/2 = (13 − 11)/2 = 1. Piperidine (a saturated six-membered ring with one NH) is the obvious candidate; a pentylamine with a double bond in it is another.

The check that catches sign errors. Write the saturated formula first and compare. A saturated C₅ amine is C₅H₁₃N; the compound has C₅H₁₁N, which is two hydrogens short, and two missing hydrogens is one degree of unsaturation. If your arithmetic and that comparison disagree, the arithmetic is wrong.

Alkene stability: more substituted is more stable

Heats of hydrogenation measure alkene stability directly: hydrogenate two different alkenes to the same alkane, and whichever released less heat started out lower in energy. The reaction being used as the measuring stick here — H₂ across the double bond over a metal catalyst — is taught later in this chapter, under Catalytic hydrogenation. All you need of it here is that the metal (Pd, Pt or Ni) does not appear in the equation: it binds H₂ and the alkene on its surface and hands both hydrogens over, lowering the barrier without changing the energies of the starting material or the product. That is what makes the heat released a clean measure of where the alkene started.

Two trends. More alkyl substitution means more stable, by roughly 1–1.5 kcal/mol per substituent, because the alkyl groups donate electron density into the pi system by hyperconjugation — the same effect that stabilizes carbocations, acting on a neutral molecule. And trans beats cis by about 1 kcal/mol, because cis substituents on the same side crowd each other.

This ordering is what Zaitsev's rule is ultimately reporting. When E1 or E2 could give either of two alkenes, the more substituted one is more stable, and under thermodynamic control that is the one you get.

Worked example — ranking by stability

Order 1-pentene, cis-2-pentene, trans-2-pentene and 2-methyl-2-butene.

Count substituents first: 1-pentene is monosubstituted, both 2-pentenes are disubstituted, 2-methyl-2-butene is trisubstituted. Then break the tie between the 2-pentenes on cis versus trans.

Least to most stable: 1-pentene < cis-2-pentene < trans-2-pentene < 2-methyl-2-butene.

Rings: why small ones are always cis

Put a double bond inside a ring and the ring itself becomes one of the two substituents on each alkene carbon — and it has to reach from one carbon to the other. On the same side of the C=C that is a short, comfortable span, which is why cyclohexene, cyclopentene and every other ordinary cycloalkene is cis whether or not anyone writes the word. On the opposite side the chain has to loop the long way around and twist the pi system to do it. Below roughly eight carbons in the ring there is not enough chain to manage it: trans-cyclohexene has never been isolated, trans-cyclooctene is the smallest one that can be put in a bottle, and it is strained enough to be chiral, because the ring cannot flip through the double bond.

Bridgehead alkenes and Bredt's rule

A bridgehead is a carbon shared by two rings of a bicyclic system — the two atoms where the three bridges meet. Putting a double bond there is the same problem as the trans-cycloalkene, run twice at once: the alkene carbon needs its three neighbors and itself coplanar, and a bridgehead carbon is held by three separate chains that all pull in different directions. Bredt's rule is the statement that this cannot be done in a small system.

The useful form of the rule is a size cut-off rather than a prohibition. Count the carbons in the whole bicyclic skeleton: below eight the bridgehead alkene is not a real compound — bicyclo[2.2.1]hept-1-ene, the norbornene with its double bond at the bridgehead, cannot be made — while at eight or more the strain becomes survivable, and bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1-ene is an isolable, if reactive, substance. The longer bridge is what buys the room.

Where this bites is elimination. If the Zaitsev product of an E1 or E2 would be a bridgehead alkene in a small bicyclic, it simply does not form, and the reaction gives the less substituted alkene instead — or nothing at all. Geometric feasibility is checked before stability, every time.

What carries forward

The pi bond's exposed electrons make the alkene a nucleophile, which is the premise of every reaction in the next two sections. Alkene stability is what set the elimination product ratios you already met in Substitution & Elimination, and it will set them again for the dehydrations in Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry. E/Z notation is how the stereochemical outcome of every addition is reported. And degrees of unsaturation is the first calculation you will make in every structure-determination problem in Spectroscopy.
