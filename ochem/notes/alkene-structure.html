sp² carbons and the pi bond

Each carbon of a C=C double bond is sp² hybridized (from the Hybridization lesson): three sp² orbitals form sigma bonds arranged in a trigonal planar shape (120° apart), and one unhybridized p orbital is left over on each carbon, oriented perpendicular to that plane. Those two p orbitals overlap side-by-side above and below the sigma-bond plane, forming a pi (π) bond — a second, weaker bond in addition to the sigma bond joining the two carbons.

Why the double bond is flat and rigid

A sigma bond can rotate freely (the orbital overlap is a symmetric cylinder around the bond axis, from the Newman projections lesson). A pi bond cannot: rotating one carbon 90° relative to the other would break the side-by-side p-orbital overlap entirely. This is why every atom directly attached to a C=C is locked in the same plane, and why cis/trans (or E/Z) isomers of alkenes are stable, isolable, different compounds rather than freely interconverting conformers.

Rotating a C=C double bond costs roughly 65 kcal/mol — the strength of the pi bond itself — compared to essentially 0 for a C–C single bond. That is why alkene geometry is configurational (a structural identity) rather than conformational (a freely-changing shape).

Degrees of unsaturation

Each ring or pi bond in a molecule removes exactly 2 hydrogens compared to the fully saturated formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. This lets you calculate degrees of unsaturation (DoU) directly from a molecular formula: DoU = (2C + 2 + N − H) / 2 (halogens count like H). A molecule with formula C₄H₈ has DoU = (8+2−8)/2 = 1 — exactly one ring or one pi bond, consistent with it being an alkene (or a cycloalkane).

Alkene stability: more substituted is more stable

Heats of hydrogenation (the energy released converting an alkene fully to an alkane with H₂) measure alkene stability directly: 1-butene (monosubstituted) releases about 30.3 kcal/mol, cis-2-butene (disubstituted) about 28.6, trans-2-butene (disubstituted) about 27.6, and 2-methyl-2-butene (trisubstituted) about 26.9. Less heat released means the alkene started out lower in energy — more stable. The trend: more alkyl substitution on the double bond = more stable alkene, and trans is generally more stable than cis (less steric strain between substituents on the same side).
