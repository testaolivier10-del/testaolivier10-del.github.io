Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions · Section 51 of 117

Conjugated systems

Practice this — interactive lesson

Two double bonds in the same molecule can be arranged three ways, and the arrangement changes the chemistry more than the count does. Getting the vocabulary straight first is worth the two minutes, because every reaction in this chapter depends on which of the three you are looking at.

Isolated, conjugated, cumulated

  • Isolated — the double bonds are separated by at least one sp³ carbon, so the run of p orbitals is broken and they never meet. Penta-1,4-diene, CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH=CH₂, is the standard example, and one sp³ carbon is all it takes. Each double bond behaves as though the other were not there.
  • Conjugated — the double bonds alternate with single bonds, so every carbon in the run is sp² and the p orbitals form one continuous overlapping system. Buta-1,3-diene is the simplest case.
  • Cumulated — the double bonds share a carbon, as in propa-1,2-diene (allene). The central carbon is sp, the two π systems are perpendicular, and they cannot conjugate with each other at all.

Conjugation is not limited to double bonds. A lone pair, a carbocation's empty p orbital, or a radical's half-filled one all conjugate with an adjacent π bond in exactly the same way — which is why allylic and benzylic cations, radicals and anions are so much more stable than their ordinary counterparts. That stabilization is the same delocalization you drew as resonance; conjugation is the orbital picture of it.

Three traps in this vocabulary.
  • One sp³ carbon is enough to isolate. The break only has to happen once: a single sp³ center interrupts the chain of parallel p orbitals and the two alkenes stop talking to each other. Reading the rule as "two or more" makes penta-1,4-diene look conjugated, which it is not.
  • "Least stable" does not mean "strained". A cumulated diene sits highest of the three, but nothing in it is bent out of shape — an allene's bond angles are exactly what sp and sp² carbons want. It is simply a molecule that pays for two π bonds and collects no delocalization energy for them. Save the word strained for rings and eclipsed bonds.
  • Conjugation does not stop at carbon. A heteroatom lone pair in a p orbital next to a π bond conjugates with it, which is why an enol ether (CH₂=CH–OR), an enamine (CH₂=CH–NR₂) and an amide are all conjugated systems. Count the p orbitals in the row, not the double bonds.
Worked example — classifying a molecule with three double bonds

Classify the double bonds of hepta-1,3,6-triene, CH₂=CH–CH=CH–CH₂–CH=CH₂.

Number the chain and mark every sp² carbon. The double bonds are C1=C2, C3=C4 and C6=C7. That makes C1, C2, C3, C4, C6 and C7 sp²; C5 is the only sp³ carbon in the molecule.

Walk the chain and look for a break. C1 through C4 is an unbroken run of sp² carbons with the double bonds alternating — C1=C2, single C2–C3, C3=C4. Those two are conjugated with each other. Then comes C5, sp³, and the run stops.

Classify the third. C6=C7 is on the far side of that sp³ carbon, so it is isolated from the other two and behaves like an ordinary terminal alkene.

The answer is not one word. Hepta-1,3,6-triene contains a conjugated diene and an isolated alkene in the same molecule. Questions usually ask "how many π electrons are in conjugation here" rather than "is it conjugated", and the answer here is four — the two π bonds from C1 to C4 — not six.

Conjugation is worth about 15 kJ/mol, and you can measure it

The claim that a conjugated diene is "more stable" is not a hand-wave. Heats of hydrogenation put a number on it. Hydrogenating but-1-ene releases about 127 kJ/mol. A molecule with two isolated double bonds should therefore release about twice that, roughly 254 kJ/mol — and penta-1,4-diene does.

Buta-1,3-diene releases about 239 kJ/mol. It gives out less energy on the way to the same kind of product, which means it started lower down: it was already about 15 kJ/mol more stable than two isolated double bonds would have been. That difference is the delocalization energy, and it is the whole reason conjugated systems behave differently.

A second, independent piece of evidence sits in the bond lengths. The C2–C3 bond of buta-1,3-diene — formally a single bond — is about 1.47 Å, noticeably shorter than the 1.54 Å of an ordinary C–C single bond and on its way toward the 1.34 Å of a double bond. The π system is genuinely spread across all four carbons, so that "single" bond carries partial double-bond character.
Worked example — getting a delocalization energy out of a table

You are given three heats of hydrogenation: but-1-ene −127, penta-1,4-diene −254 and buta-1,3-diene −239 kJ/mol. Find the delocalization energy of buta-1,3-diene.

Build the reference first. The question is always "how much energy would this molecule have released if its double bonds had not been talking to each other?" One ordinary C=C is worth 127 kJ/mol on hydrogenation, so two independent ones should be worth 2 × 127 = 254. Penta-1,4-diene measures exactly that, which is the experimental proof that the reference is a fair one and not just arithmetic.

Subtract the measurement from the reference. 254 − 239 = 15 kJ/mol.

Read the sign the right way round. Both molecules end at the same kind of product, a saturated alkane. Buta-1,3-diene gave out less heat getting there, so it must have started from a lower rung. The 15 kJ/mol is stabilization of the starting material, not of the product.

The trap: comparing buta-1,3-diene against but-1-ene instead of against two but-1-enes. The reference has to contain the same number of π bonds as the compound you are testing, or you are measuring the second double bond rather than the conjugation.

s-cis and s-trans: a conformation, not a configuration

Because the central C2–C3 bond of a conjugated diene is a single bond, the two ends can rotate about it. The two limiting conformations have their own names, and the s stands for single — it is a rotation, not a double-bond geometry, and nothing about it makes two separate compounds.

  • s-trans — the two double bonds point in opposite directions across the single bond. This is the more stable conformation and the one most conjugated dienes sit in most of the time.
  • s-cis — both double bonds point the same way, bringing the two ends of the diene close together. This costs about 12 kJ/mol (roughly 3 kcal/mol), which is nearly as much as the whole delocalization energy, so "slightly" is the wrong word for it. The cost is one specific clash: the hydrogen on C1 and the hydrogen on C4 that point inward in the planar arrangement run into each other. Butadiene relieves some of that by twisting a little way out of plane, so its real minor conformer is skewed rather than exactly planar; "s-cis" names the idealized flat form that reacts.

Keep the distinction from cis and trans across a double bond clear. Those describe configurations and cannot interconvert without breaking a π bond; s-cis and s-trans are conformations and interconvert freely at room temperature by rotation. The prefix s is the only thing marking the difference in writing.

The s-cis conformation is the one that matters practically, because the Diels–Alder reaction requires it. A diene that cannot reach s-cis cannot do the reaction at all — which is why a diene locked s-cis inside a ring, like cyclopentadiene, is unusually reactive, and why one locked s-trans is unreactive no matter how electron-rich it is.

The orbital picture: n p orbitals make n molecular orbitals

"One continuous overlapping system" is worth spelling out, because two later sections lean on it and neither can be understood without it. Start from the smallest case. Bring two parallel p orbitals together and you do not get one orbital, you get two: a low-energy one where the two lobes are in phase and reinforce between the nuclei, and a high-energy one where they are out of phase and cancel there. The first is the bonding π orbital; the second is the antibonding one, written π*. An ordinary C=C is that pair with the two π electrons in the lower orbital and π* empty.

The rule generalizes exactly. Overlap n parallel p orbitals and you get n π molecular orbitals: the lowest has every lobe in phase, and each one above it adds a node. The set does spread a little further as it lengthens — the bottom rung sinks and the top rung rises — but it gains rungs much faster than it gains height, which is why the spacing in the middle closes up. Filling them takes one more rule. In a neutral polyene with an even number of sp² carbons, each carbon contributes one electron, so n electrons fill the lowest n/2 orbitals and the top half stay empty. An odd-numbered or charged system — the allyl case below — has the same ladder but an electron count that does not divide in half, so count its electrons before you fill anything.

Buta-1,3-diene has four p orbitals, so four π orbitals — call them ψ₁ to ψ₄ — and four π electrons, which fill ψ₁ and ψ₂. That makes ψ₂ the highest occupied molecular orbital, the HOMO, and ψ₃ the lowest unoccupied one, the LUMO. These two are the frontier orbitals, and they are the only two that usually matter: the HOMO holds the electrons the molecule can most easily lend out, and the LUMO is the cheapest place to put electrons that arrive.

Two consequences fall straight out of the ladder, and they are the two things the rest of the chapter needs. First, lengthening the conjugation adds orbitals faster than it stretches the ladder, so the top filled one climbs and the bottom empty one drops and the HOMO–LUMO gap narrows — which is what the last section of this chapter measures with a spectrometer. Second, the frontier orbitals of a linear π system are biggest at the two end atoms, so that is where the molecule both donates and accepts, and it is why a Diels–Alder joins C1 and C4 of a diene and nothing in between.

Why the ends of the system are where the chemistry happens

Draw the resonance forms of an allylic cation and you find the positive charge on the two ends of the three-carbon system and never in the middle. The same is true of an allylic anion and an allylic radical. This is a general feature of conjugated systems, and the orbital picture in the previous section says why: in the orbital that does the chemistry, the two ends are where the electron density is concentrated and the middle carbon is where it vanishes.

The allyl system is the cleanest demonstration. Three p orbitals give three orbitals, and the middle one, ψ₂, has its node running straight through the central carbon — that atom contributes nothing to it at all. ψ₂ is the orbital whose occupancy changes when you go from allyl cation (empty) to radical (one electron) to anion (two), so every one of those three species puts its charge or its odd electron on C1 and C3 and none on C2. The resonance drawing and the orbital drawing are the same statement made twice.

It is the reason the next section exists. When a conjugated diene is attacked, the intermediate is an allylic cation with two reactive ends, so the nucleophile has two places to go — and the product you get depends on which one it picks.

What carries forward

Three things from this section are load-bearing later. The stability of conjugated systems explains why allylic bromination works, why dienes are thermodynamically favored products of elimination, and eventually why aromatic rings are in a class of their own. The s-cis requirement is the single commonest reason a Diels–Alder does not happen. And the orbital picture — one continuous π system whose energy gap narrows as it lengthens — is exactly what UV-Vis spectroscopy measures, which is the last section of this chapter.
