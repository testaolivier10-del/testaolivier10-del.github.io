What a ring flip does

A chair flip (or ring flip) converts one chair conformation into the other, alternate chair — every position that was axial becomes equatorial, and every position that was equatorial becomes axial. No bonds break; the ring simply flexes through a sequence of higher-energy shapes and settles into the other chair.

The pathway

Going from one chair to the other passes through a half-chair (a high-energy transition state), then briefly through a twist-boat and/or boat conformation (higher-energy but real, if fleeting, local shapes — the boat has its own eclipsing and steric problems, including a "flagpole" 1,4-interaction), then another half-chair, arriving at the second chair.

The energy barrier for a full ring flip (chair → half-chair → boat/twist-boat → half-chair → chair) is about 10-11 kcal/mol for cyclohexane itself — small enough that ring flipping happens rapidly at room temperature (thousands of times per second), but large enough that it's a real, measurable barrier, unlike the much smaller ~2.9 kcal/mol barrier for simple bond rotation in ethane.

Why substituent identity isn't fixed

Because the ring flips constantly, a substituent isn't permanently "the axial group" or "the equatorial group" — its identity depends on which of the two interconverting chairs you're looking at. What is fixed is the overall equilibrium: the molecule spends more time in whichever chair puts its bulkiest substituent(s) equatorial, exactly as A-values predict.
