Ring flips

Practice this — interactive lesson

A cyclohexane ring is not static. It flexes continuously between two chair forms, around a hundred thousand times a second at room temperature, and every axial position becomes equatorial and vice versa each time. Understanding this motion is what turns the axial/equatorial vocabulary from a labelling exercise into a way of predicting behavior.

What a ring flip does

A chair flip (or ring flip) converts one chair conformation into the other. Every substituent that was axial becomes equatorial, and every substituent that was equatorial becomes axial. No bonds break; the ring simply flexes through a sequence of higher-energy shapes and settles into the alternative chair.

What does not change is which face of the ring a substituent is on. A group pointing up before the flip is still pointing up after it. This is the crucial invariant, and it is what keeps cis/trans relationships fixed while axial/equatorial labels swap.

A ring flip is not a rotation of the drawing. If you redraw a chair rotated on the page, up stays up and axial stays axial — nothing has happened. A genuine flip pushes the "up" end of the chair down and the "down" end up, which is why every axial/equatorial assignment reverses. The reliable test: after a correct flip, every substituent should have swapped axial/equatorial and kept its up/down face. If both changed, or neither did, the drawing is wrong.

The pathway

Going from one chair to the other is not a single motion but a sequence: chair → half-chair → twist-boat → (boat) → twist-boat → half-chair → chair.

The half-chair is the transition state, and it is the high point of the journey at roughly 10–11 kcal/mol above the chair. In it, four adjacent carbons are forced coplanar, which costs both angle strain and torsional strain at once.

The twist-boat sits about 5.5 kcal/mol above the chair and is a genuine, if shallow, energy minimum — a real conformation the molecule briefly occupies, not merely a point on a slope. The boat at about 6.5 kcal/mol is a transition state between two twist-boats, suffering from eclipsed bonds along two of its edges and from a "flagpole" clash between the two hydrogens on its raised ends.

The full barrier of about 10–11 kcal/mol is worth comparing with the 2.9 kcal/mol for rotating ethane. Because rate falls off exponentially with barrier height, that extra 8 kcal/mol costs nearly six orders of magnitude: ring flipping runs at roughly 10⁵ times per second against ethane's 10¹⁰–10¹¹, so it is about a million times slower than a simple bond rotation — and still, by any human standard, extremely fast. Cool cyclohexane to about −90 °C and the flip becomes slow enough that NMR — an instrument that reports hydrogens in different surroundings as separate signals, and the subject of Spectroscopy — sees the axial and equatorial hydrogens separately, which is the direct experimental proof that this whole picture is correct.

Why substituent identity is not fixed

Because the ring is flipping constantly, a substituent is never permanently "the axial group." Its label depends on which of the two interconverting chairs you are looking at, and it holds each label roughly in proportion to how long the molecule spends in that chair.

What is fixed is the equilibrium. The molecule spends more time in whichever chair puts its bulkier substituents equatorial, in exactly the ratio the A-values predict. So when a chemist says "the methyl is equatorial," the precise meaning is "about 95% of the molecules are in the chair that puts the methyl equatorial at any instant."

Worked example — drawing the other chair

You are given a chair carrying a bromine axial and pointing up on C1, and a methyl equatorial and pointing up on C2. Draw the flipped chair and read the new labels off it.

Step 1 — record the faces before you touch the drawing. Br: up. CH₃: up. Write that down. It is the only information that has to survive the flip, and it is the thing people lose.

Step 2 — redraw the ring with the ends reversed. Whichever vertex of your chair was the raised end becomes the lowered one and vice versa; the four carbons in between stay where they are. Do not rotate the page — that changes nothing. Keep the carbons numbered in the same order going round, so C1 is still C1.

Step 3 — put each group back on its own face. On C1, draw the bond that points up from that carbon in the new ring, and hang the Br on it. On C2, draw the bond that points up from that carbon, and hang the methyl on it. You are placing by face, never by label.

Step 4 — now read the labels. C1's up bond in the new chair is the outward-angled one, so the Br is equatorial, still up. C2's up bond is now the vertical one, so the methyl is axial, still up. Both labels flipped; neither face did.

Step 5 — check. Every substituent swapped axial for equatorial and kept its face. If a group came out axial in both drawings you rotated the page; if one changed face you drew a different compound. And because both groups were up to begin with they are cis, and they are still cis — the relationship a flip can never touch.

Why the methyl had to go on C2. Axial directions alternate as you walk round the ring: up, down, up, down, up, down. So C1 and C2 have opposite axial directions — C1 axial up means C2 axial down, and therefore C2's equatorial bond points up, which is what makes this problem drawable in the first place. C1 and C3, two apart, share an axial direction: if C1's axial points up, so does C3's, and C3's equatorial bond points down. “Equatorial and pointing up on C3” is not a hard molecule to draw; it is not a molecule at all. That is also why a cis-1,3 pair is diaxial in one chair and diequatorial in the other, never one axial and one equatorial.

When the ring cannot flip

Two situations lock a ring, and both are used deliberately.

A very large substituent such as tert-butyl does not stop the flip in principle, but makes one chair so overwhelmingly favored that the other is never meaningfully populated. This is the conformational anchor trick from the previous section.

A fused ring system can stop it outright. In trans-decalin, two cyclohexane rings share an edge with the ring-fusion hydrogens on opposite faces, which means each ring joins the other through two equatorial bonds. Flip one ring and those two bonds both become axial — and axial bonds on adjacent carbons point in opposite directions, 180° apart, so the second ring would have to bridge them. No six-membered ring can close across that gap, so the flip cannot happen and the system is genuinely rigid. This is why steroids — four fused rings, mostly trans-fused — have a fixed three-dimensional shape, and why that shape can be so precisely complementary to a receptor.

What carries forward

The ring flip is the mechanism behind everything in the next section: comparing two chairs and picking the favored one is only meaningful because the molecule can actually get from one to the other. And it is what makes the E2 story work — a substrate whose favored chair has the leaving group equatorial can still eliminate, because it can flip into the minority chair where the leaving group is axial and the reaction is geometrically allowed.
