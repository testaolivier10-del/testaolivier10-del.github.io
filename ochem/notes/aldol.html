Enolate Chemistry · Section 73 of 117

Aldol reactions

Practice this — interactive lesson

The aldol reaction joins two carbonyl molecules together at the alpha carbon of one and the carbonyl carbon of the other. It is the most important carbon–carbon bond-forming reaction in biology — glycolysis runs it backwards, and photosynthesis runs it forwards — and it is the reason the previous section's enolate chemistry matters.

The enolate as a carbon nucleophile

The carbon-centered contributor of an enolate gives the alpha carbon genuine nucleophilic character, even though most of the formal charge sits on oxygen. Given an electrophile, that alpha carbon behaves like any other carbon nucleophile in this course — an acetylide, a Grignard — and attacks.

What makes the aldol special is that the electrophile is another carbonyl molecule. The same compound is both nucleophile and electrophile, in different molecules, at the same time.

The aldol reaction, step by step

Step 1. Base removes an alpha hydrogen from one carbonyl molecule, forming its enolate. Note that the base is deliberately weak — hydroxide or an alkoxide — so that only a small fraction is deprotonated and plenty of un-ionized carbonyl remains available as the electrophile.

Step 2. The enolate's alpha carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule, in ordinary nucleophilic addition: pi electrons move to oxygen, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide.

Step 3. That alkoxide is protonated, by water or by the solvent, giving the product: a beta-hydroxy carbonyl, with a new OH exactly two carbons from the retained carbonyl.

Every step is reversible. The reverse — a retro-aldol — is a real reaction and a real strategy; the aldolase enzyme in glycolysis uses it to cleave a six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon fragments.

Which way the addition sits depends on the carbonyl. For aldehydes it is downhill, and the beta-hydroxy aldehyde can be isolated in good yield. For ketones it is uphill — acetone with catalytic hydroxide is only a few percent diacetone alcohol at equilibrium — because the product is crowded and a ketone C=O was already the more stable carbonyl to begin with. That is why almost every ketone aldol you will be asked about is run hot, all the way to the condensation: the dehydration does not come back, and it drags the unfavorable addition along behind it.

The name describes the product: ALD(ehyde) + (alcoh)OL, a molecule containing both. The reaction keeps the name with ketone starting materials too, even when no aldehyde is present anywhere.

Aldol condensation: heat pushes further

The beta-hydroxy carbonyl still has an alpha hydrogen, now sitting between the carbonyl and the new hydroxyl — a setup primed for elimination. The elimination is neither E1 nor E2. Base removes the alpha hydrogen first, giving an enolate; that enolate then pushes its electrons down and expels hydroxide from the beta carbon. Deprotonation first, leaving group second, through a carbanion in the middle — the pattern is called E1cb (elimination, unimolecular, conjugate base), and it is open here only because the carbanion in the middle is an enolate and therefore stabilized. Hydroxide is a hopeless leaving group for E1 or E2; it is tolerable here because the enolate is doing the pushing.

The product is an enone: a C=C conjugated with a C=O. The word is used loosely in this chapter for any α,β-unsaturated carbonyl, aldehyde as well as ketone.

The driving force is conjugation. The enone's extended pi system is substantially more stable than the isolated aldol product, which is what pulls an otherwise unfavorable dehydration forward. The two-stage sequence — aldol addition, then dehydration — is the aldol condensation, and "condensation" here means a reaction that joins two molecules and expels a small one, in this case water.

Whether you stop at the beta-hydroxy carbonyl or continue to the enone is a matter of conditions: mild temperature and a short reaction time give the addition product, while heat gives the condensation product.

Worked example — acetaldehyde with itself

Step 1: hydroxide removes an alpha hydrogen from CH₃CHO, giving ⁻CH₂CHO.

Step 2: that carbon attacks the carbonyl of a second acetaldehyde. The tetrahedral alkoxide forms.

Step 3: protonation gives 3-hydroxybutanal — the aldol, four carbons from two molecules of two.

With heat: dehydration gives (E)-but-2-enal, crotonaldehyde — an α,β-unsaturated aldehyde, not a ketone. The geometry is worth noting: the condensation gives the E product almost exclusively, because the transition state leading to it is the less crowded one.

Notice that both new bonds came from the carbonyls themselves. Nothing was added except a catalytic base.

Worked example — reading an enone backwards

Target: PhCH=CH–CO–CH₃, 4-phenylbut-3-en-2-one.

Step 1 — find the C=C conjugated to the C=O, and put the water back. That double bond came from the dehydration, so add OH to the carbon further from the carbonyl and H to the nearer one: PhCH(OH)–CH₂–CO–CH₃.

Step 2 — cut the alpha–beta bond. It is the only bond the aldol made. The alpha side keeps the carbonyl and takes its hydrogen back; on the beta side the OH becomes a C=O again.

Step 3 — read off the roles. Alpha side: CH₃COCH₃, acetone, which supplied the alpha carbon and so was the nucleophile. Beta side: PhCHO, benzaldehyde, whose carbonyl carbon was attacked and so was the electrophile.

Check that the reaction makes sense: benzaldehyde has no alpha hydrogens, so it could only ever be the electrophile. That is a clean crossed aldol. Answer: acetone and benzaldehyde, NaOH, heat.

The acid-catalyzed aldol

Acid reaches the same product by the mirror-image route. There is no enolate anywhere in it — under acid you never generate a strong base — so the nucleophile is the neutral enol, and the electrophile is the protonated carbonyl, which is far hungrier than a neutral one. The enol attacks the protonated carbonyl, and losing the enol's OH proton leaves the neutral beta-hydroxy carbonyl.

Acid also dehydrates readily, and by its own route: tautomerize to the enol, protonate the beta hydroxyl so it can leave as water, and let the enol's C=C push in as it goes; losing the OH proton at the end gives the enone. In practice acidic conditions run almost always straight through to the condensation product, while basic conditions let you stop at the addition product if you keep it cold and brief.

Crossed aldols and why they need control

Mix two different carbonyl compounds, each with alpha hydrogens, and in principle four products result: either compound's enolate can attack either compound's carbonyl. That is a statistical mess, and a crossed aldol run naively is not a useful reaction.

Two strategies give control.

Use one partner with no alpha hydrogens. Benzaldehyde, formaldehyde and benzophenone cannot form enolates, so they can only be the electrophile. A crossed aldol between benzaldehyde and a ketone gives one product cleanly, and because the resulting enone is conjugated with the ring it dehydrates readily. This special case is called a Claisen–Schmidt condensation — confusingly named after the same Claisen as the next section, although it is an aldol and not a Claisen.

An aldehyde is also the better electrophile on its own merits: one alkyl group instead of two leaves the carbonyl carbon less hindered and less electron-rich. So where a crossed aldol pairs an aldehyde with a ketone, the aldehyde takes the electrophile's role even when both partners could enolize.

Use LDA at low temperature. Convert one partner completely and irreversibly to its enolate at −78 °C, then add the second partner. Since the first compound is entirely deprotonated, it cannot act as an electrophile, and since the second is added afterwards, it never sees the base. This is the general solution and the modern one.

Match the base to the intent. A classic aldol needs a weak base, because the reaction requires both the enolate and the un-ionized carbonyl in the same flask. A crossed aldol needs a strong one, because the whole point is to deprotonate one partner completely before the other arrives. Using hydroxide for a crossed aldol gives a mixture; using LDA for a self-aldol gives nothing to attack.

Intramolecular aldol: the same reaction, closing a ring

If one molecule carries two carbonyls, the enolate of one can reach the other, and the aldol closes a ring instead of joining two molecules. Nothing about the mechanism changes. The only new question is which enolate attacks which carbonyl.

The answer is ring size, and the preference is the one from Alkanes & Conformations: five- and six-membered rings close readily, three, four and seven-plus do not compete. So work out every enolate the substrate can form, count the ring each one would close, and keep the five or the six.

Worked example — hexanedial

Substrate: OHC–CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂–CHO. Number it C1 to C6, with the two CHO carbons as C1 and C6.

Step 1: the alpha carbons are C2 and C5. Take the C2 enolate.

Step 2: C2 attacks C6. Count the ring that closes — C2, C3, C4, C5, C6 — five atoms. That works.

The alternative: C2 attacking C1 is no ring at all, since they are already bonded; on a longer chain the other enolate would have to close three or four atoms, and does not.

Step 3: protonation gives 2-hydroxycyclopentane-1-carbaldehyde — a five-membered ring carrying an OH, with a CHO hanging off the carbon next to it.

With heat: E1cb dehydration puts the new C=C inside the ring and in conjugation with the CHO, giving cyclopent-1-ene-1-carbaldehyde. Recognizing a ring with an enone in it as “an intramolecular aldol condensation did this” is the single most useful pattern in synthesis questions.

Conjugate addition: the other place a nucleophile can attack

The enone produced by an aldol condensation is itself electrophilic — and at two positions. A nucleophile can attack the carbonyl carbon directly (1,2-addition) or the beta carbon (1,4- or conjugate addition), which is electrophilic because the enone has a resonance contributor with the positive charge on the beta carbon: push the C=C electrons into the carbonyl, and the beta carbon is the atom left electron-poor. Draw that contributor once and both electrophilic sites are obvious.

Which one happens depends on the nucleophile. Hard, reactive nucleophiles — organolithiums, hydride — tend to attack the carbonyl directly. Softer, more stabilized ones — cuprates (R₂CuLi), enolates, amines, thiolates — add conjugately. A conjugate addition using an enolate as the nucleophile is the Michael reaction, and combining a Michael addition with a subsequent intramolecular aldol condensation is the Robinson annulation, the standard way to build a new six-membered ring.

What carries forward

The aldol pattern — make an enolate, attack a carbonyl, join two fragments — is the template for the Claisen condensation in the next section and for a great deal of biological chemistry. Retro-aldol cleavage is how sugars are broken down. And the 1,2 versus 1,4 choice is the last of this course's recurring competitions between two sites on one molecule.
