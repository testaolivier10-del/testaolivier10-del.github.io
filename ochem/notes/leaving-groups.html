How Reactions Happen · Section 40 of 121

Leaving groups

Practice this — interactive lesson

A nucleophile can be perfect and an electrophile can be beautifully polarized, and if nothing is willing to leave, no reaction happens. The leaving group is the third member of the set, and it is the one that most often decides whether a proposed reaction actually works. It is also the single most common reason a student's proposed mechanism is wrong: something departed that had no business departing.

Why spreading charge out matters

This is the one idea underneath the whole section. A negative charge is a concentration of electrons, and like charges repel — so a charge crammed into a small space is crowded, and that repulsion is stored energy. High energy means unstable. Give the charge more room, whether by putting it on a bigger atom or by spreading it over several atoms (delocalization), and the repulsion eases: lower energy, more stable, more willing to exist on its own, better leaving group. Why I⁻ beats Cl⁻, why tosylate is excellent, and why HO⁻ is hopeless are all this one principle applied to three different structures.

What makes a good one

When a leaving group departs, it takes the bonding pair with it, so it leaves as an anion (or as a neutral molecule, if it was positively charged to begin with). A good leaving group is therefore one that is stable on its own once holding that pair — which is the same thing as saying it is a weak base, which is the same thing as saying it is the conjugate base of a strong acid.

Those three statements are equivalent, and being able to move freely between them is most of the skill. Poor leaving groups — HO⁻, ⁻NH₂, ⁻CH₃ — are strong bases that do not want the negative charge, because it stays concentrated on a small atom or, worse, on one that is not electronegative at all.

Quantifying it: pKaH

The cleanest way to rank leaving groups is by the pKa of their conjugate acid — the neutral species you get by handing the proton back to the leaving group — written pKaH. This is the Acids & Bases scale read for a new purpose: lower pKaH means a weaker base, and a weaker base is a better leaving group. The table below is that scale restricted to the groups that actually sit on carbon.

Read the table as a guide rather than a ruler. It gets the big gaps exactly right — the four orders of magnitude between a halide and hydroxide are real, and nothing with a pKaH above about 10 leaves carbon unassisted. It is less reliable at the top, where the numbers are crowded and other effects take over: the table gives HI at −10 against TsOH at −2.8 and so predicts I⁻ > TsO⁻, while measured substitution rates put tosylate marginally ahead of iodide. The honest statement is that sulfonates and iodide are in the same top tier and either order may be keyed; what the table is for is telling that tier apart from chloride, and chloride apart from hydroxide.

There is a second, smaller factor the table cannot show: the strength of the bond being broken. Leaving-group ability is about the anion's stability once it has gone, but something still has to break the C–X bond on the way, and that bond gets weaker and longer down the halogen column. For the halides the two effects push the same direction, which is why the halide order is so clean; where they disagree — as they do for fluoride, whose anion is a middling base but whose bond is the strongest of the four — the bond strength is what decides.

Ranking the halides

Among the halides the order is I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻. Iodide is the largest and most polarizable, so it disperses the charge over the biggest volume and is the weakest base of the four. Two reinforcing effects push the same way: the C–I bond is also the weakest and longest of the four, so it is easiest to break.

Fluoride is a genuinely poor leaving group despite fluorine being the most electronegative element, which catches people out. Electronegativity would suggest fluorine is happy to take the electrons — but fluoride is small and charge-dense, the C–F bond is the strongest carbon–halogen bond at roughly 116 kcal/mol, and HF is a weak acid, meaning F⁻ is a reasonably strong base. Alkyl fluorides are essentially inert to substitution, which is exactly why fluorine is used in pharmaceuticals to block metabolism at a particular position.

Leaving as a neutral molecule

Everything above assumed the leaving group departs as an anion, and that is the usual case. But a group attached through an atom that already carries a positive charge leaves neutral, and neutral is a far easier thing to be. R–OH₂⁺ losing water is the case the rest of this section is built around, and the nitrogen version works the same way: R–NH₂ will never leave, but protonate it to R–NH₃⁺ and the group that departs is neutral ammonia, or make it a quaternary ammonium R–NR₃⁺ and the group that departs is a neutral trialkylamine. Both are feasible, where the amide anion is not.

This is one idea, not three: adding a positive charge to the leaving atom means the electron pair it takes with it merely restores neutrality instead of creating an anion, and an anion is the expensive part. Any time a poor leaving group is sitting on an atom that could be protonated or alkylated, that is the activation step a question is testing.

Good leaving groups and good nucleophiles are near-opposites, and the reason is one sentence: a leaving group has to be stable holding the electron pair, and a nucleophile has to be unstable enough to want to give it away. So a reaction that runs well forward tends not to run backward — bromide leaves a carbon easily and attacks one only sluggishly by comparison, while an alkoxide attacks eagerly and never leaves. The exception that proves it is iodide, which is unusually good at both because polarizability helps a large atom carry charge and reach out with it, which is why iodide catalyzes substitutions that would otherwise be slow.
A mechanism that kicks out HO⁻ is wrong. Hydroxide is the conjugate base of water, a weak acid, so hydroxide is a strong base and will not leave carbon on its own. Nor will ⁻NH₂ or ⁻CH₃. If your proposed arrow pushes a hydroxide off a carbon, the step does not happen — go back and find the activation step you skipped.

An alcohol has to be activated before it can leave

Alcohols are everywhere in synthesis, and HO⁻ never leaves, so the one operation that unlocks all of their chemistry is turning that OH into something that will. The principle is the one this whole section has been making, and there are only two shapes it can take.

Put a positive charge on the leaving atom. Protonate the oxygen and the group that departs is neutral water rather than hydroxide — pKaH −1.7 instead of 15.7, about seventeen units of improvement from one proton, and the electron pair it takes away merely restores neutrality instead of creating an anion.

Or replace the oxygen's hydrogen with something that spreads the charge. A sulfonate ester, R–OTs, leaves as tosylate, whose negative charge is shared over the three oxygens of the sulfonate group — roughly −⅓ on each, pKaH −2.8. Nothing about the carbon has changed; the group that has to walk away has been swapped for one that is eighteen orders of magnitude happier to do it. That is why tosylate sits at the top of the table beside iodide.

Which reagents perform these conversions — TsCl, MsCl, SOCl₂, PBr₃, hot H₂SO₄ — what each one does to the configuration at that carbon, and which to reach for when, is the opening business of Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry. What this section owes you is the reason any of them is needed at all, and the worked example below is that reason in one flask.

Worked example — why R–OH + NaBr gives nothing and R–OH + HBr gives R–Br

Put 1-butanol in a flask with sodium bromide. Bromide is an excellent nucleophile and the carbon bearing the OH is δ+, so the ingredients of a substitution are all present. Nothing happens. Work out why, one step at a time.

Step 1 — what would have to leave? For bromide to bond to that carbon, the carbon must give up a bond, and the only candidate is the C–O bond. That would push HO⁻ off as hydroxide.

Step 2 — check the leaving group. Hydroxide's conjugate acid is water, pKaH 15.7. Hydroxide is a strong base and will not depart on its own. The step fails here, and no amount of nucleophile fixes it, because the problem is not on the nucleophile's side of the reaction.

Now run the same reaction with HBr instead of NaBr. HBr supplies the same bromide, but it also supplies a proton, and that changes everything.

Step 1 — protonate. An oxygen lone pair takes the proton from HBr (two arrows: lone pair to H, H–Br bond to Br). The alcohol becomes R–OH₂⁺, and bromide is released into solution.

Step 2 — now the leaving group is water. Water's conjugate acid is H₃O⁺, pKaH −1.7. That is roughly seventeen pKa units better than hydroxide, and water leaves readily.

Step 3 — bromide attacks. The bromide already in solution bonds to the carbon as water departs, giving 1-bromobutane.

The lesson is not that acid is magic. It is that the reaction was never blocked by a weak nucleophile or an insufficiently positive carbon; it was blocked by one step being impossible, and the fix was to change the thing that leaves. When a mechanism you have written refuses to work, that is the first place to look.

Worked example — ranking four substrates with nothing but the pKaH table

Rank CH₃–I, CH₃–OTs, CH₃–OH and CH₃–NH₂ for how fast a nucleophile displaces the group on carbon. The carbon is identical in all four, so the only variable is what leaves.

CH₃–NH₂ leaves as ⁻NH₂, whose conjugate acid is ammonia at pKaH 38. That is off the bottom of the table: an amide anion is one of the strongest bases in the course and never departs from carbon. Last, and not close.

CH₃–OH leaves as HO⁻, pKaH 15.7. Also a strong base, also refuses — third, and in practice as unreactive as the amine until it is activated.

CH₃–I leaves as I⁻, pKaH −10, and CH₃–OTs leaves as TsO⁻, pKaH −2.8. Both are conjugate bases of very strong acids, both are very weak bases, and both leave easily. The table would separate them in iodide's favor; measured rates put tosylate a little ahead. Treat them as tied at the top unless a question tells you otherwise.

Answer: CH₃–OTs ≈ CH₃–I ≫ CH₃–OH > CH₃–NH₂. The gap between the top pair and the bottom pair is enormous — many orders of magnitude — and the gap within each pair is small. Getting the tiers right is worth more marks than getting the order inside a tier right.

The best leaving group in common use is N₂, from a diazonium salt. Nitrogen gas is so stable, so unreactive and so entropically favored on leaving that the reaction is essentially irreversible — which is what makes diazonium chemistry such a powerful tool for installing substituents on aromatic rings in Aromatic Chemistry.

Reading the names SN1, SN2, E1, E2

The letter is the reaction type: S for substitution, E for elimination, with the N in SN meaning nucleophilic — the incoming group is an electron-rich attacker. The number is how many species take part in the slowest, rate-determining step: 1 means the molecule ionizes on its own first, with the leaving group departing unassisted, and the nucleophile or base arrives afterwards; 2 means the leaving group departs at the same instant the nucleophile or base attacks, in one concerted motion. All four require a leaving group to depart at some point — no leaving group, no reaction. Substitution & Elimination covers each in full.

What carries forward

Leaving-group quality is one of the four variables (along with substrate, nucleophile and solvent) that decide which of SN1, SN2, E1 and E2 you get in Substitution & Elimination. Alcohol activation is a recurring move throughout Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry and Amines. And in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives, the entire reactivity ordering of the carboxylic acid derivatives — acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide — turns out to be a leaving-group ranking in disguise.
