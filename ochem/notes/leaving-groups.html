The acid/base words, in one line each

An acid gives away an H⁺; a base takes one with a lone pair. Strong/weak = how eagerly. Whatever is left after an acid loses its H⁺ is its conjugate base (HCl → Cl⁻, H₂O → OH⁻), and the two see-saw: strong acid ⇒ weak (stable, content) conjugate base; weak acid ⇒ strong (grabby) conjugate base. pKa puts a number on it — lower pKa = stronger acid. Substitution and elimination, in one line each

Substitution swaps one group on a carbon for another — something new attaches, the old group departs. Elimination strips a group off one carbon and an H off the neighbouring carbon, and the leftover electrons become a C=C double bond. Both need a group to break off carbon carrying the bonding electrons, which is why leaving groups gate them. Module 6 covers both in full.

Why spreading charge out matters

The one idea under the whole lesson. A negative charge is a pile of electrons, and like charges repel — so a charge crammed into a small space is crowded, and that repulsion is stored energy. High energy = unstable. Give the charge more room (a bigger atom, or several atoms sharing it — delocalization) and the repulsion eases: lower energy, more stable, more willing to exist alone, better leaving group. Why I⁻ beats Cl⁻, why tosylate is excellent, and why HO⁻ is hopeless are all this one principle applied to different structures.

What makes a good one

When a leaving group departs, it takes a bonding pair of electrons with it. A good leaving group is one that's stable on its own once it has those electrons — a weak base (the conjugate base of a strong acid). Poor leaving groups (OH⁻, NH₂⁻, CH₃⁻) are strong bases that don't want the negative charge and resist leaving, because the charge stays concentrated on a small atom (O, N) or on one that isn't electronegative at all (C) — unlike I⁻, which dilutes it across a big, polarizable atom.

Activating an alcohol

Because HO⁻ won't leave, an alcohol has to be activated — the OH converted into a better leaving group before the reaction is run. Protonate it with strong acid (R–OH → R–OH₂⁺) so the leaving group is neutral water, whose conjugate acid H₃O⁺ has pKa ≈ −1.7 instead of 15.7. Or tosylate it with TsCl (R–OH → R–OTs), giving the resonance-stabilized TsO⁻ — mild, neutral, and it leaves any stereocenter on that carbon untouched because the C–O bond is never broken. A mechanism that kicks out HO⁻ is wrong.

Ranking the halides

Among F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, I⁻ is the best leaving group — the weakest base of the four (largest, most polarizable, most stable as an anion). Cl⁻ beats OH⁻ as a leaving group because Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of a strong acid (HCl) and is very stable on its own, while OH⁻ is the conjugate base of water (a much weaker acid), making OH⁻ a much stronger, more reluctant base.

Tosylate (OTs⁻) is an excellent leaving group because its negative charge is delocalized (resonance-stabilized) across the sulfonate group, making it a very weak base — exactly why chemists convert poor leaving groups like OH⁻ into tosylates before a substitution.

Reading the names SN1, SN2, E1, E2

The letter is the reaction: S = substitution, E = elimination, and the N in SN means nucleophilic (the incoming group is an electron-rich attacker). The number is how many species take part in the slowest, rate-setting step: 1 = the molecule ionizes alone first and the leaving group departs unassisted, then the nucleophile or base comes in; 2 = the leaving group departs at the same moment the nucleophile or base attacks, in one motion. All four need a leaving group to depart at some point — no leaving group, no reaction. Module 6 covers each.

Quantifying it: pKaH

Lower pKa of the leaving group's conjugate acid (pKaH) = weaker base = better leaving group. HI ≈ −10, HBr ≈ −9, HCl ≈ −7 (all strong acids → I⁻/Br⁻/Cl⁻ are weak bases, good leaving groups, in that ranked order). Water ≈ 15.7 (weak acid → OH⁻ is a strong base, poor leaving group).
