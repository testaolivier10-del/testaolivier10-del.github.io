Polymers · Section 118 of 121

Addition polymers

Practice this — interactive lesson

An addition polymer comes from an alkene, and the mechanism is the electrophilic or radical addition you already know, repeated. Nothing is lost, so the repeat unit has exactly the same formula as the monomer — which is the quickest way to recognize one.

The chain reaction, in three stages

Radical polymerization is the commonest, and it is a chain reaction with the three stages any chain reaction has:

  • Initiation. A peroxide, RO–OR with its weak O–O bond, or an azo initiator such as AIBN, which breaks to two carbon radicals and N2, splits homolytically to give two radicals, one of which adds to a monomer.
  • Propagation. The radical adds to the next alkene, generating a new radical at the end of a longer chain. This repeats thousands of times.
  • Termination. Two radical chain ends meet, and there are two ways it can end. Combination: the two radicals couple, and you get one chain of the combined length. Disproportionation: one radical takes a β-hydrogen from the carbon next to the other radical center, so one chain is left with a C=C at its end and the other ends saturated — two dead chains instead of one long one. Either way two radicals are consumed and both chains stop.

Because termination needs two radicals to find each other in a solution where they are very dilute, propagation runs a long way first. That is why chains are long.

This is the same initiation–propagation–termination structure as radical halogenation, and it is worth seeing the two as one mechanism. The difference is only what the radical adds to: a C–H bond there, a C=C bond here.

Chain transfer: a fourth thing that can happen

There is a step that sits between propagation and termination. A growing radical can abstract a hydrogen from something else — solvent, monomer, an added thiol, or its own backbone — which kills that chain but makes a new radical. The number of radicals in the flask does not change, so the polymerization carries on at the same rate; what changes is that one chain stopped early. That is chain transfer, and it is how molecular weight is controlled in practice: a little of a thiol, RSH, whose S–H bond is weak enough to give up its hydrogen easily, is added as a chain-transfer agent, and every chain stops shorter.

Compare the three fates by what each does to the radical count, because that is what exam questions turn on. Propagation makes one chain longer and keeps one radical. Chain transfer stops one chain and starts another, and still keeps one radical. Termination stops two chains and destroys two radicals. Only the last of the three lowers the radical concentration, which is why adding a transfer agent shortens the chains without slowing the reaction down.

Head-to-tail, and why

Take a monosubstituted alkene, CH2=CHX. The growing radical can add to either carbon, and it adds to the CH2 end — because that leaves the radical on the substituted carbon, which is the more stable of the two. Sterics push the same way, which is why the selectivity is so high: the CH2 end is also the less hindered carbon to attack. Head-to-head defects do happen — the selectivity is high rather than perfect — and a chain carrying a few of them is still described as head-to-tail.

Do that repeatedly and every substituent ends up on alternate carbons, which is called head-to-tail linking. It is the same regiochemical argument as Markovnikov's rule, reached by asking which intermediate is more stable rather than by quoting a rule.

Worked example — draw three repeat units of PVC and show that it is head-to-tail

Step 1. The monomer is CH2=CHCl. Its two ends are different: one CH2, unsubstituted, and one CHCl, substituted.

Step 2. A chain radical R· attacks the CH2 carbon, giving R–CH2–ĊHCl. The radical sits on the carbon bearing the chlorine, which is secondary and has the chlorine lone pairs next to it — the better of the two radicals available.

Step 3. That radical attacks the CH2 end of the next monomer: R–CH2–CHCl–CH2–ĊHCl.

Step 4. Once more: R–CH2–CHCl–CH2–CHCl–CH2–ĊHCl.

Step 5 — read the backbone. C1 is CH2, C2 carries Cl, C3 is CH2, C4 carries Cl, C5 is CH2, C6 carries Cl. Every chlorine is on an alternate carbon, 1,3 to the next one and never 1,2. That is what head-to-tail means.

The contrast. Had one monomer added the other way round you would find two chlorines on adjacent carbons — a head-to-head defect — and then two CH2 groups in a row, which is the tail-to-tail join that has to follow it. Exams draw a chain and ask you to find that defect: look for any pair of adjacent substituted carbons.

Every one of these is drawn the same way. Take the monomer, break the π bond, and write the repeat unit in brackets with a bond out of each side: –[CH2–CHX]–n.

Dienes, and the polymer that keeps its double bond

The last row of that table is a diene, and it behaves differently for a reason the conjugation chapter already gave you. Polymerize a conjugated diene and 1,4-addition takes over: the radical adds at C1, which puts the radical on C2; that radical is allylic over C2–C3–C4, so it is delocalized out to C4, and the next monomer adds there — which leaves C2=C3 as a double bond in the middle of every repeat unit. Isoprene, 2-methyl-1,3-butadiene, polymerized 1,4 gives polyisoprene: natural rubber when those double bonds are cis, and the hard, inelastic gutta-percha when they are trans. The retained double bonds are not a curiosity. They are the sites sulfur attacks in vulcanization, two sections from now, and a saturated chain has nothing equivalent to offer.

Two monomers can also be polymerized together, giving a copolymer. In a random copolymer the two units are interspersed, which spoils the regularity of the chain and so lowers crystallinity; the Tg is not lowered but lands somewhere between the two homopolymers’ values, and there is only one of it (styrene–acrylonitrile, SAN, has a higher Tg than polystyrene). In a block copolymer each chain is a long run of one monomer followed by a long run of the other, and the two blocks separate into their own domains, so the material shows the properties of both at once. SBR tire rubber is styrene with butadiene; ABS is acrylonitrile, butadiene and styrene, and it is tough because rubbery polybutadiene domains sit dispersed inside a rigid glassy matrix.

Tacticity: the same polymer, three materials

In a monosubstituted polymer such as polypropylene, every substituted carbon in the chain has a definite orientation — the substituent points to one side or the other — so the arrangement along the chain matters. (These are not stereocenters in the CIP sense: the two chain directions are near-identical, which is exactly why tacticity gets its own vocabulary instead of R and S. Polyethylene and PTFE have no such centers at all, and no tacticity.) Three patterns have names:

  • Isotactic — all substituents on the same side. Packs well, crystalline, strong.
  • Syndiotactic — substituents alternating regularly. Also packs well.
  • Atactic — random. Cannot pack, so the material is amorphous and soft.
Radical polymerization gives atactic polymer, because a planar radical has no preference about which face the next monomer arrives on. Getting isotactic polypropylene — the useful kind — needs a Ziegler–Natta catalyst, which holds the growing chain on a metal center and forces each addition to happen the same way. Atactic polypropylene is a sticky goo; isotactic polypropylene is rope and car bumpers. Same repeat unit.

Concretely, a Ziegler–Natta catalyst is TiCl4 with a trialkylaluminum such as Al(C2H5)3. The aluminum alkylates the titanium, which leaves a Ti–C bond with an empty coordination site beside it; the alkene binds into that empty site and then inserts into the Ti–C bond, lengthening the chain by one monomer and regenerating the empty site. That is the same coordinate-then-insert pattern as the palladium cycle in Organometallics, and because the insertion has to happen at one defined, crowded site on the metal, every monomer arrives the same way round. Stereocontrol and the absence of branching both come out of that single fact: the chain end is held, so it can neither flip nor curl back onto itself.

Branching, and the two polyethylenes

Chain transfer to the growing chain's own backbone is the special case with a name of its own: backbiting. The radical end curls round into a six-membered arrangement and takes the hydrogen five carbons back, and the chain then continues from that new position — leaving a four-carbon butyl branch behind. Radical polymerization of ethylene at high pressure does this constantly, giving low-density polyethylene: branched, poorly packed, flexible, the stuff of plastic bags.

A Ziegler–Natta catalyst suppresses it, giving high-density polyethylene: linear, well packed, rigid, the stuff of milk bottles and pipe. Identical repeat unit, identical formula, different architecture, and genuinely different materials.

What carries forward

Initiation, propagation, termination; head-to-tail regiochemistry from the more stable radical; and two structural variables — tacticity and branching — that change the material without changing the chemistry. When a question gives you an alkene and asks for the polymer, break the π bond and bracket the repeat unit; when it gives you two materials with the same formula, the answer is architecture.
