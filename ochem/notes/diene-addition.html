1,2- vs 1,4-addition

Practice this — interactive lesson

Add HBr to buta-1,3-diene, CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂, using one molecule of HBr for each molecule of diene, so that only one of the two double bonds reacts. You get two products, 3-bromobut-1-ene and 1-bromobut-2-ene, and both come from the same carbocation. Which one you isolate depends on the temperature.

This page follows that reaction one step at a time. It builds on two earlier ideas: the allylic cation from Conjugated systems, whose charge sits on both ends of a three-carbon system, and the rule from Markovnikov / anti-Markovnikov that the proton adds where it leaves the more stable cation.

Where the proton goes

Number the diene C1 to C4 from one end. The first step is the one you know from any HBr addition to an alkene: a π bond takes the proton from HBr, and bromide leaves. The proton can bond to an end carbon, C1, or to an inner carbon, C2. The molecule is symmetric, so C4 and C3 give the same results as C1 and C2.

Put the proton on C1 and the positive charge lands on C2. C2 sits right beside the C3=C4 double bond, so its empty p orbital overlaps with that π bond. A cation whose charged carbon is next to a double bond is called allylic, and its charge is shared with the far end of that double bond.

Put the proton on C2 instead and the charge lands on C1. C2 now has four single bonds, so it is sp³, and it has no p orbital to pass the overlap along. That cuts C1 off from the C3=C4 double bond and leaves a primary cation with no resonance at all, about the least stable cation this reaction could make.

The barriers to the two cations differ by tens of kJ/mol, so essentially all of the reaction goes through C1. This is the Markovnikov reasoning applied to a diene: the proton goes where it leaves the more stable cation, and here that cation is allylic.

One cation, two ends

The cation from protonating C1 has two resonance forms. In the first, the charge is on C2 and the double bond is still C3=C4. In the second, the π electrons of C3=C4 have shifted toward C2: the double bond is now C2=C3 and the charge is on C4. These are two drawings of one cation, not two cations that turn into each other. Its charge is shared between C2 and C4 and never sits on C3.

So bromide has two positive carbons it can bond to. It bonds with one of its lone pairs, either to C2 or to C4, and each choice gives a different compound.

The two products

Bromide at C2 gives 3-bromobut-1-ene. The H and the Br sit on neighboring carbons, C1 and C2, and the C3=C4 double bond survives at the end of the chain. This is 1,2-addition.

Bromide at C4 gives 1-bromobut-2-ene. The H is on C1 and the Br is on C4, at opposite ends of the original diene, and the double bond now sits in the middle of the chain, between C2 and C3. This is 1,4-addition, also called conjugate addition.

The 1, 2 and 4 in these names count along the original diene. They are not the locants in the product's IUPAC name: 1,4-addition gives 1-bromobut-2-ene, whose name numbers the chain from the Br end. The new position of the double bond is not a separate rearrangement step either. The second resonance form of the cation already had its double bond there.

Other electrophiles split the same way

Nothing in this argument depends on HBr. Any electrophile that adds to C1 of a conjugated diene leaves an allylic cation behind, and the nucleophile then has the same two ends to choose from.

Br₂ is one example. Addition reactions showed that Br₂ adds to an alkene through a bromonium ion, a three-membered ring that holds a positive bromine. With buta-1,3-diene, the ring forms across C1 and C2. It then opens at the C2–Br bond, because breaking that bond puts the charge on C2, next to C3=C4, where it is allylic. Breaking the C1–Br bond instead would leave a primary cation with no resonance.

Bromide then bonds to C2 or to C4. 1,2-addition gives 3,4-dibromobut-1-ene, and 1,4-addition gives 1,4-dibromobut-2-ene. As with HBr, cold conditions favor the 1,2-product and warm conditions favor the 1,4-product, for the reasons given below.

The same allylic cation can also form from the other direction, by ionization. Put 3-bromobut-1-ene, the 1,2-product from HBr, in water. Its C–Br bond breaks, bromide leaves, and the allylic cation forms again. Water then bonds to C2 or to C4 and loses a proton, giving but-3-en-2-ol or but-2-en-1-ol. SN1 named this allylic rearrangement: the new group can end up on a different carbon from the one the leaving group left, and the double bond shifts with it.

The temperature decides the ratio

Run the HBr addition at about −80 °C and the mixture is roughly 80% 1,2-product and 20% 1,4-product. Run it at about 40 °C and the ratio flips to roughly 15:85 in favor of the 1,4-product. The diene, the reagent and the cation are the same at both temperatures.

A second experiment shows what changed. Take the pure 1,2-product, warm it to 40 °C with a trace of HBr, and it turns into the same 15:85 mixture. At that temperature the two products interconvert: bromide leaves again by the ionization shown above, the allylic cation re-forms, and bromide bonds to either end. So the product that forms first is not necessarily the one you end up with.

Two separate comparisons are at work. One asks which product forms faster, and the other asks which product is more stable. For buta-1,3-diene they give different answers, and the next two sections take them one at a time.

Why the 1,2-product forms faster

Compare the two resonance forms of the cation as carbocations. When the charge is on C2, the charged carbon is bonded to two other carbons, C1 and C3, so that form is a secondary cation. When the charge is on C4, the charged carbon is bonded only to C3, so that form is primary.

The secondary form is the more stable of the two, so it is the major contributor: it describes more of the real cation. The real cation therefore carries more of its positive charge on C2 than on C4. Bromide bonds faster to the carbon with more positive charge, so the 1,2-product forms faster. At −80 °C bromide cannot leave again, so whatever forms first stays, and the 1,2-product dominates.

A second effect points the same way. Right after the proton lands on C1, the bromide ion that gave up the proton is still close by. C2 is next to C1, while C4 is three carbons away, so bromide often bonds to C2 before it has time to reach C4. This is called the proximity effect. The charge argument above is the one most courses expect, and the proximity effect adds to it rather than replacing it.

Why the 1,4-product is more stable

Now compare the products themselves. In 3-bromobut-1-ene, the 1,2-product, the double bond is at the end of the chain, and only one carbon is bonded to it: it is monosubstituted. In 1-bromobut-2-ene, the 1,4-product, the double bond is inside the chain and has a carbon bonded to each end: it is disubstituted. Alkene structure showed that a more substituted alkene is more stable, so the 1,4-product is lower in energy.

At 40 °C, bromide can leave either product and re-form the allylic cation, as the warming experiment showed. Each molecule goes back and forth many times, and the mixture settles at the ratio that the stabilities set. That ratio favors the more stable 1,4-product.

Naming the two outcomes

The product that forms faster is called the kinetic product; here it is the 1,2-product. The product that is more stable is called the thermodynamic product; here it is the 1,4-product. At −80 °C the products cannot turn back into the cation, so the kinetic product dominates, and the reaction is said to be under kinetic control. At 40 °C they can, so the thermodynamic product dominates, and the reaction is under thermodynamic control.

The next page, Kinetic vs thermodynamic control, states this idea in general and draws it on an energy diagram. This page needs only the diene case.

Unsymmetrical dienes: choose the end first

Buta-1,3-diene is symmetric, so protonating C1 and protonating C4 give the same cation. A diene with a substituent has two different ends, and you must decide which one takes the proton before you can predict any products. Isoprene shows how.

Worked example — HBr and isoprene

Add HBr to 2-methylbuta-1,3-diene (isoprene), CH₂=C(CH₃)–CH=CH₂. Number the diene so that the methyl sits on C2.

Try C1. The charge lands on C2. C2 is bonded to three carbons: the new CH₃ at C1, its own methyl, and C3. So this resonance form is a tertiary cation. The other form puts the charge on C4, which is primary. Best form: tertiary.

Try C4. The charge lands on C3, which is bonded to C2 and C4: a secondary cation. The other form puts the charge on C1, which is primary. Best form: secondary.

Pick the end. Tertiary beats secondary, so the proton goes to C1, and the reaction runs through the cation shared between C2 and C4.

Bond bromide to each end. Bromide at C2 gives the 1,2-product, 3-bromo-3-methylbut-1-ene, which keeps a monosubstituted double bond at the end of the chain. Bromide at C4 gives the 1,4-product, 1-bromo-3-methylbut-2-ene. Its double bond, C2=C3, carries three carbons, so it is trisubstituted.

Name the kinetic and thermodynamic products. The tertiary form is the major contributor, so C2 carries far more of the charge. Bromide bonds there faster, which makes the 1,2-product the kinetic product and the main product cold. The 1,4-product has the more substituted double bond by a wide margin, so it is the thermodynamic product and wins when the mixture is warmed.

The general rule, then: try both ends of the diene, find the best resonance form of each cation, and put the proton on the end whose cation has the better best form. That cation is the one both products come from.

Pick a test diene with care. Penta-1,3-diene, CH₂=CH–CH=CH–CH₃, looks like a good example and is not. Protonating C1 gives a cation with a methyl group at each charged end, so bromide at C2 and bromide at C4 give the same compound, 4-bromopent-2-ene. There is no pair of products to compare.
Worked example — a diene with two substituted ends

Add HBr to 2-methylpenta-1,3-diene, CH₂=C(CH₃)–CH=CH–CH₃. Both C1 and C4 are ends of the conjugated system, so the first decision is which one takes the proton.

Try C1. The charge lands on C2, which carries the new CH₃, its own methyl and C3, so that form is tertiary. The other form puts the charge on C4, which is bonded to C3 and C5: secondary. Best form: tertiary.

Try C4. The charge lands on C3, which is bonded to C2 and C4: secondary. The other form puts it on C1, which is primary. Best form: secondary.

Pick the end. Tertiary beats secondary, so the proton goes to C1, and the cation shares its charge between the tertiary C2 and the secondary C4, with more of it on C2.

Bond bromide to each end. Bromide at C2 gives the 1,2-product, 4-bromo-4-methylpent-2-ene, whose double bond is inside the chain and disubstituted. Bromide at C4 gives the 1,4-product, 4-bromo-2-methylpent-2-ene, whose double bond is trisubstituted.

Name the kinetic and thermodynamic products. Bromide bonds to the tertiary carbon faster, so the 1,2-product is the kinetic product and the one you isolate cold. On stability the order reverses, because a trisubstituted double bond beats a disubstituted one, so warming drains the mixture into the 1,4-product, the thermodynamic product.

What carries forward

The pattern on this page is one delocalized intermediate, two places to capture it, and a product ratio set by the conditions rather than by a change of mechanism. It is not limited to dienes. In Enolate Chemistry a ketone can lose a proton from either of the two carbons next to its C=O, giving two different enolates (the anions left behind). It gives one enolate at low temperature with a bulky base and another under conditions that let the two interconvert. The name conjugate addition also returns there, for nucleophiles that add to the far end of a C=C–C=O system.
