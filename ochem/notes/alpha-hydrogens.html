Enolate Chemistry · Section 82 of 119

Alpha hydrogens & enolates

Practice this — interactive lesson

So far the carbonyl group has been something nucleophiles attack. This chapter turns it around: a carbonyl makes the hydrogens next to it acidic enough to remove, and the anion that results is a carbon nucleophile. That single fact is what allows carbonyl compounds to react with each other, and it is the basis of most carbon–carbon bond formation in biology.

The alpha carbon and its unusually acidic hydrogens

The carbon directly attached to a carbonyl carbon is the alpha carbon, and hydrogens on it are alpha hydrogens. A ketone's alpha C–H has a pKa around 20 — dramatically more acidic than a plain alkane C–H at 50, and more acidic even than a terminal alkyne at 25.

Why: delocalization into the carbonyl

Removing an alpha hydrogen leaves a carbanion immediately adjacent to a carbonyl, and that lone pair delocalizes directly into the carbonyl pi system. The resulting anion is an enolate, and it has two resonance contributors: one with the charge on carbon, and one with the charge on oxygen and a C=C where the C–C was. The oxygen-centered form is the major contributor, since oxygen holds negative charge far better than carbon.

This is the single largest acidifying effect in the course: about 30 pKa units, a factor of 10³⁰. A plain carbanion is so unstable it is essentially never formed with common bases; put that same carbanion next to a carbonyl and an ordinary alkoxide can generate it. One resonance interaction is the difference between impossible and routine.

Two carbonyls do it again

If an alpha hydrogen sits between two carbonyls, the resulting anion delocalizes into both, and the acidity increases enormously.

Write the contributors out and the size of the effect explains itself. Deprotonate the central CH₂ of pentane-2,4-dione and the charge can sit on carbon, on the left-hand oxygen, or on the right-hand oxygen — three contributors where a simple enolate has two, and two of the three put it on an electronegative atom. The anion is also planar and conjugated the whole way across O–C–C–C–O, so the two carbonyls share the load instead of competing for it. Eleven pKa units, bought with one extra C=O.

A 1,3-diketone at pKa 9 is about as acidic as phenol and more acidic than hydrogen cyanide — from a hydrogen on carbon. This is why 1,3-dicarbonyls are the workhorse nucleophiles of synthesis: an ordinary alkoxide deprotonates them completely, with no special reagents required.

Note also that an ester's alpha hydrogen (pKa 25) is markedly less acidic than a ketone's (20), because the ester's alkoxy oxygen is already donating into the carbonyl and there is less demand left for the alpha carbon to satisfy.

Worked example — find the alpha hydrogens

The rule: an alpha carbon is bonded directly to a carbonyl carbon. A hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon itself — an aldehyde's CHO — does not count, and neither does anything one bond further out.

(a) Butan-2-one, CH₃COCH₂CH₃. The carbonyl is C2, so C1 (a CH₃, three H) and C3 (a CH₂, two H) are both alpha. Five alpha hydrogens in two non-equivalent sets — which is exactly why this ketone can give two different enolates.

(b) 2,2-Dimethylpropanal, (CH₃)₃CCHO. The carbonyl carbon carries an H, but it sits on the carbonyl carbon rather than next to it. The one alpha carbon is the quaternary carbon, which has no hydrogen at all: zero alpha hydrogens, so no enolate and no enol, under any conditions.

(c) Ethyl acetate, CH₃CO₂CH₂CH₃. Only the CH₃ on the acyl side is alpha (three H, pKa 25). The OCH₂ hydrogens sit on a carbon bonded to oxygen, not to the carbonyl carbon, so they are neither alpha nor acidic — a distinction worth being deliberate about, because the two groups look alike written out.

(d) Pentane-2,4-dione, CH₃COCH₂COCH₃. Three alpha carbons: the two methyls, at the usual 20, and the central CH₂, which is alpha to both carbonyls at 9. A base takes the central one every time.

The habit: mark every carbon touching a C=O, then count hydrogens on those carbons and nowhere else.

Choosing a base: two regimes

With a base whose pKaH is below the substrate's — hydroxide or ethoxide, around 16, against a ketone at 20 — deprotonation is incomplete, and you get a small equilibrium concentration of enolate coexisting with a large excess of unreacted ketone. That is exactly what an aldol reaction needs, since the ketone must be available as the electrophile.

With a base whose pKaH is well above the substrate's — LDA at about 36 — deprotonation is complete and irreversible. LDA is chosen because it is strong enough to do this and bulky enough not to attack the carbonyl carbon itself, which a small strong base would.

The choice of base also decides which enolate forms when a ketone has two different alpha carbons — the subject of Kinetic & thermodynamic enolates, the last section of this chapter.

The enol tautomer

Because the enolate's charge is shared between carbon and oxygen, it can be reprotonated at either. Protonating at carbon regenerates the ketone; protonating at oxygen gives the enol — a C=C bearing an OH, the same species that appeared fleetingly in alkyne hydration in Alkenes & Alkynes.

Keto and enol are tautomers: constitutional isomers that interconvert rapidly, with a hydrogen genuinely in a different place. They are not resonance structures, and the distinction matters — draw them with ⇌, never ↔.

Both catalysts reach the enol in two steps, in opposite order. Under acid: protonate the carbonyl oxygen first, giving the resonance-stabilized protonated ketone, and then let a weak base — a second molecule of solvent — take the alpha hydrogen, the C=C forming as it leaves. Under base: take the alpha hydrogen first, giving the enolate, and then protonate the enolate on oxygen. Neither route makes a naked carbocation or a naked carbanion. Notice the ordering rule underneath, because it governs every acid- and base-catalyzed mechanism from here on: under acid you never generate a strong base, so protonation comes first; under base you never generate a strong acid, so deprotonation comes first.

There is a direct experimental test that this is happening. Stir a ketone in D₂O with a trace of NaOD and every alpha hydrogen — and only the alpha hydrogens — is replaced by deuterium, because each turn of the enolate cycle picks up D rather than H. Acetone exchanges all six; butan-2-one exchanges five; 2,2-dimethylpropanal exchanges none. Counting the exchangeable hydrogens is the cleanest way to count alpha carbons, and it is a standard exam question.

For a simple ketone the keto form dominates by roughly 10⁶ to one, because a C=O is much stronger than a C=C. But for a 1,3-diketone the enol can dominate: pentane-2,4-dione is about 80% enol in the pure liquid, because its enol is conjugated with the second carbonyl and internally hydrogen-bonded. Phenol is the extreme case — its "enol" form is aromatic, so it exists entirely as the enol and never as a ketone.

Do not draw keto and enol as resonance forms. A hydrogen moves between them, so they are separate compounds in equilibrium. Getting this wrong is a conceptual error rather than a notational one, and it will make every enolate mechanism you write afterwards ambiguous about what actually moved.

What enolates do

Once you have an enolate, the alpha carbon behaves like any carbon nucleophile. It does SN2 on an alkyl halide (alkylation, best with the doubly stabilized 1,3-dicarbonyls, since an ordinary enolate tends to do other things). It attacks aldehydes and ketones (the aldol reaction, next section). It attacks esters by acyl substitution (the Claisen condensation, the section after). And it reacts with halogens at the alpha position.

One consequence is worth noticing on its own. If the alpha carbon is a stereocenter, forming the enolate destroys it: that carbon rehybridizes to sp² and the anion is flat, so the proton that comes back arrives on either face with equal probability and the product is racemic. (R)-3-methylcyclohexanone is safe, because its stereocenter is the beta carbon. (R)-3-phenylbutan-2-one, whose stereocenter is its alpha carbon, is fully racemized by a catalytic amount of acid or base within minutes. Note what does not happen: a planar intermediate gives racemization, not inversion. This is why some chiral drugs lose their optical purity on the shelf, and why amino acids racemize in the hot base used to hydrolyze a peptide.

What carries forward

The enolate is the nucleophile for the rest of this chapter. Kinetic versus thermodynamic control returns wherever two products compete under different conditions. And the fact that a 1,3-dicarbonyl is acidic enough for an ordinary base makes the acetoacetic ester and malonic ester syntheses possible — the classical routes to substituted ketones and carboxylic acids.
