Bond dipoles

A bond is polar when its two atoms have different electronegativities — the shared electrons sit closer to one side, creating a small dipole that points toward the more electronegative atom. In C–O, the dipole points toward oxygen.

Molecular polarity depends on geometry too

A whole molecule's polarity depends on both bond polarity and shape — if the individual bond dipoles point in directions that cancel out by symmetry (like vectors of equal size pointing opposite ways), the molecule can be nonpolar even with polar bonds.

Worked examples

CO₂ (linear, two C=O dipoles pointing in exactly opposite directions): the dipoles cancel — nonpolar overall. Water (bent, two O–H dipoles): the bent shape means the dipoles add up instead of canceling — polar overall. CCl₄ (tetrahedral, four identical polar C–Cl bonds): the symmetric arrangement makes all four dipoles cancel — nonpolar, even though every individual bond is polar.

"Like dissolves like": polar water mixes with polar substances but not nonpolar ones like CO₂ or oil. This is the entire basis of solubility rules you'll use constantly once alcohols and ethers show up later in the course.

Dipole moment: a measurable quantity

Polarity is measured as the dipole moment (μ), in debyes (D). Water: μ ≈ 1.85 D (bent shape, dipoles add). CO₂: μ = 0 D exactly (linear, dipoles cancel). It's the vector sum of every bond dipole, combining electronegativity difference (Electronegativity) with molecular shape (Molecular Geometry).
