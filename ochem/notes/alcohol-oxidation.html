Oxidizing alcohols

Practice this — interactive lesson

A primary alcohol can be oxidized to an aldehyde or straight past it to a carboxylic acid, and the aldehyde is very often the thing you actually want. Learning this section is learning which reagent stops where, and the reason turns out to be a single mechanistic detail: whether there is water in the flask.

The secondary column is the reassuring one: a ketone has no hydrogen left on the carbonyl carbon, so it cannot be oxidized further under these conditions and both reagents give the same thing. The choice of reagent only matters for primary alcohols.

Why water decides where a chromium oxidation stops

Chromium(VI) reagents oxidize an alcohol by way of a chromate ester, which then loses the carbinol C–H to give the carbonyl. For a primary alcohol that gives an aldehyde — and an aldehyde in water is in equilibrium with its hydrate, the gem-diol formed by water adding across the C=O.

That hydrate is, structurally, an alcohol again: it has an OH and a hydrogen on the same carbon. So the chromium reagent oxidizes it a second time, and the product is the carboxylic acid. Take the water away and the hydrate never forms, so the oxidation has nothing left to grip and stops at the aldehyde.

This is the whole explanation for the reagent list below. Anhydrous chromium reagent → aldehyde. Aqueous chromium reagent → carboxylic acid. It is not about strength, and adding less oxidant or shortening the reaction time does not reliably stop an aqueous oxidation at the aldehyde.

The reagents worth knowing

  • PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate), in anhydrous dichloromethane. Cr(VI) with no water present, so a primary alcohol stops at the aldehyde and a secondary gives the ketone. The standard answer when a question wants an aldehyde and offers a chromium reagent.
  • Jones reagent (CrO₃ in aqueous sulfuric acid, usually in acetone). Cr(VI) with water, so a primary alcohol goes all the way to the carboxylic acid. Secondary alcohols give ketones. Also written as CrO₃/H₂SO₄ or as Na₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄, which behave the same way.
  • Swern oxidation (oxalyl chloride and DMSO at −78 °C, then triethylamine). No metal at all, and stops cleanly at the aldehyde. Mild enough for sensitive substrates, which is why it is common in real synthesis.
  • Dess–Martin periodinane (DMP), a hypervalent iodine reagent at room temperature. Also stops at the aldehyde, also metal-free, and easier to handle than the Swern because it needs no cryogenic step.
  • KMnO₄, hot and often basic then acidified. Mn(VII) in water, so it behaves like the aqueous chromium case: a primary alcohol goes through to the carboxylic acid and a secondary gives the ketone. It is the reagent you meet as an answer option when the question wants "all the way up" without naming chromium. Purple Mn(VII) drops out as brown MnO₂, which is the color change the test relies on.
  • Two more worth recognizing. PDC (pyridinium dichromate) is PCC’s near-twin — anhydrous Cr(VI), stops at the aldehyde. MnO₂ is the selective one: it oxidizes allylic and benzylic alcohols to the carbonyl and leaves ordinary saturated alcohols alone, which is the rare case where reagent choice really can distinguish two alcohols in one molecule.

Group them by outcome rather than by chemistry and the list is short: PCC, PDC, Swern and DMP give aldehydes; Jones, other aqueous Cr(VI) and hot KMnO₄ give carboxylic acids; all of them give ketones from secondary alcohols and nothing from tertiary ones. MnO₂ is the single exception to "no oxidant tells two alcohols apart", and it tells them apart by position (allylic/benzylic) rather than by class.

Chromium reagents are toxic and environmentally nasty, which is why the Swern and DMP exist and why modern papers usually avoid chromium. Exams still ask about PCC and Jones constantly, so you need both — but it is worth knowing the metal-free options are not merely alternatives, they are the ones a working lab reaches for first.
Three traps. (1) These oxidants are blind to C=C. PCC on hex-5-en-1-ol gives hex-5-enal — the alkene is untouched, because none of these reagents is a hydrogenation catalyst or an electrophile for the π bond. Students who have just met "oxidation" often reach for the alkene as well. (2) A diol oxidizes at every carbinol carbon it has. Hexane-1,6-diol plus Jones gives the diacid, not the hydroxy-acid; there is nothing site-selective about these reagents. (3) The Jones color change is the test. Orange Cr(VI) going green Cr(III) means an oxidizable alcohol was present, so a question describing that color change is telling you the substrate was primary or secondary. No color change means tertiary.
Worked example — choosing a reagent by what must survive

Target: convert the primary alcohol of 4-hydroxypentan-1-ol, HO–CH₂CH₂CH₂–CH(OH)–CH₃, into an aldehyde, leaving the secondary alcohol alone.

Step 1 — how far up? Primary alcohol to aldehyde is one rung, not two. So the reagent must be one that stops: anhydrous, not aqueous. That rules out Jones, CrO₃/H₂SO₄ and hot KMnO₄ immediately, before any other consideration.

Step 2 — what else is in the molecule? A secondary alcohol. Look down the list: PCC, PDC, Swern and DMP all oxidize secondary alcohols to ketones just as readily as they oxidize primary ones to aldehydes. None of them distinguishes by class.

Step 3 — is there a selective reagent? MnO₂ is the only one on the list that discriminates, and it discriminates by position: allylic and benzylic only. Neither alcohol here is allylic or benzylic, so MnO₂ does nothing useful.

Step 4 — so reagent choice cannot solve it. Treating this molecule with PCC gives the keto-aldehyde: both alcohols are oxidized. The answer is to take the secondary alcohol out of play first — mask it, oxidize, unmask it. That operation is a protecting group, and the standard one for an alcohol is a silyl ether: TBSCl with imidazole to install it, oxidize, then TBAF to take it off. Those reagents are taught in Synthesis & Retrosynthesis; quote them here only as a preview.

The transferable move is step 4, not the reagent names: when two identical functional groups sit in one molecule and only one may react, no oxidant will save you. Recognizing that a selectivity problem has become a protection problem is what is actually being tested.

Worked example — four substrates, one reagent each

Give the product of each. Name the substrate class first, then count the carbinol hydrogens, then read the reagent for water.

(a) 2-methylbutan-1-ol + PCC, CH₂Cl₂. Primary (two carbinol H). PCC is anhydrous, so it stops one rung up: 2-methylbutanal, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CHO.

(b) 2-methylbutan-1-ol + CrO₃, H₂SO₄, H₂O. Same substrate, aqueous reagent. The aldehyde hydrates and is taken a second rung: 2-methylbutanoic acid, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CO₂H.

(c) cyclohexanol + Jones. Secondary (one carbinol H), so one rung and it stops whatever the reagent: cyclohexanone. Note that "aqueous" bought you nothing here — the aqueous/anhydrous split only ever changes the answer for a primary alcohol.

(d) 2-methylbutan-2-ol + Jones, in excess, overnight. Tertiary: no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon. No reaction, and neither excess nor time changes that. The reagent has nothing to remove.

The pattern: two questions, asked in this order — how many hydrogens on the carbon bearing the OH, and is there water in the flask. The first sets the ceiling, the second says whether you hit it.

Oxidation levels, again

Read the whole section against the ladder from the last one and it compresses. An alcohol sits at one rung, an aldehyde or ketone one rung up, a carboxylic acid one rung above that. A primary alcohol has two rungs available because it has two hydrogens on the carbinol carbon; a secondary alcohol has one; a tertiary alcohol has none and therefore cannot climb at all.

What carries forward

Every one of these reactions runs backwards in the next section, where hydride reagents take carbonyls back down to alcohols. Holding both directions at once is what makes synthesis planning possible — a primary alcohol and an aldehyde are one oxidation apart in either direction, so a route that needs one from the other is a single step, and knowing which reagent gets you there without overshooting is the content.
