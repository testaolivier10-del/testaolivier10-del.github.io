First, the word: regiochemistry

Regiochemistry is the question of which atom in a molecule a reagent ends up attached to, when more than one position was available. It is not about 3D shape (that is stereochemistry) — it is purely about position. When an alkene adds H–Br, both carbons of the double bond could in principle take the Br; asking which one actually does is a regiochemistry question, and this whole lesson is the answer.

Markovnikov's rule, restated mechanistically

The classic textbook statement — "H adds to the carbon that already has more H's" — is really just a consequence of a single deeper fact: carbocations form at whichever carbon gives the more stable carbocation (from the Alkanes/Carbocation-stability trend: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl, driven by hyperconjugation and inductive donation from alkyl groups). Protonating the carbon with fewer substituents leaves the cation on the carbon with more substituents — the more stable, more substituted carbocation. The nucleophile (X⁻, H₂O, etc.) then attacks that more-substituted carbon.

Hydroboration-oxidation: a genuinely different, concerted mechanism

BH₃ (or its complexes) reacts with an alkene in a single, concerted step: boron and hydrogen add to the double bond simultaneously, from the same face (syn addition), with no carbocation intermediate at all. Regiochemistry is controlled by sterics, not carbocation stability: the large boron atom preferentially bonds to the less hindered, less substituted carbon. A subsequent oxidation step (H₂O₂/NaOH) replaces boron with OH with retention of position and configuration. Net result: OH ends up on the less substituted carbon — the opposite regiochemistry from acid-catalyzed hydration, hence "anti-Markovnikov."

Because hydroboration-oxidation has no carbocation intermediate, it never rearranges — a genuinely useful, predictable alternative whenever you specifically need the anti-Markovnikov alcohol or need to avoid carbocation rearrangement entirely.

Radical HBr addition: anti-Markovnikov by a totally different route

In the presence of peroxides (ROOR), HBr addition switches from the ionic (carbocation) mechanism to a radical chain mechanism: a bromine radical (not H⁺) adds first, to whichever carbon gives the more stable radical (3° > 2° > 1°, the same substitution-stability trend but for radicals instead of cations) — which is the less substituted carbon. This flips the regiochemistry: Br ends up on the less substituted carbon, H on the more substituted one — the reverse of normal (ionic) HBr addition. This "peroxide effect" applies specifically to HBr; HCl and HI do not undergo an efficient radical chain and still add via the normal, Markovnikov, ionic pathway.
