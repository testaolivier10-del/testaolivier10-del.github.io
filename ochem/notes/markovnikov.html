Alkenes & Alkynes · Section 47 of 117

Markovnikov / anti-Markovnikov

Practice this — interactive lesson

When HBr adds to propene, the bromine could land on either carbon of the double bond. It lands overwhelmingly on one of them. Explaining why — and then learning how to make it land on the other one instead — is one of the most practically useful things in this chapter, because it turns a reaction that gives a product into a pair of reactions that give whichever product you want.

First, the word: regiochemistry

Regiochemistry is the question of which atom a reagent ends up attached to when more than one position was available. It is not about three-dimensional arrangement — that is stereochemistry — but purely about position. Asking which carbon of an alkene takes the bromine is a regiochemistry question.

Keeping the two words separate matters, because a reaction can be highly regioselective and not stereoselective at all, or the reverse. Hydrohalogenation is the first; bromination is the second.

Markovnikov's rule, restated mechanistically

The classic statement is "the hydrogen adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens." That is a correct summary and a poor explanation, and it fails on the variants below.

The real content is one deeper fact: the carbocation forms wherever it is most stable. Protonating the less substituted carbon leaves the positive charge on the more substituted one, which is the better cation — 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl, by hyperconjugation and inductive donation from the alkyl groups. The nucleophile then attacks there, so the halogen or hydroxyl ends up on the more substituted carbon.

Once you hold it this way, the rule generalizes. A cation stabilized by resonance beats one stabilized by alkyl groups, so adding HBr to styrene, PhCH=CH₂, puts the bromine on the carbon next to the ring even though that carbon is formally secondary — the cation there is spread over the whole ring instead of sitting on one atom. (That is a benzylic cation, and why the ring is so good at stabilizing it is the business of Aromatic Chemistry; the resonance picture from Organic Structure & Electron Movement is enough to see that it works.) "More hydrogens" would not have told you that; "more stable cation" does.

Markovnikov selectivity is a statement about the transition state leading to the cation, not about the product. Because the rate-determining step is protonation, the pathway with the lower barrier wins — and the barrier is lower for the route to the more stable cation. This is Hammond's postulate in action: for an endothermic step, the transition state resembles the product of that step, so anything stabilizing the cation also stabilizes the transition state leading to it.

Hydroboration–oxidation: a genuinely different mechanism

BH₃, usually as its THF or dimethyl sulfide complex, reacts with an alkene in a single concerted step: boron and hydrogen add across the double bond simultaneously, from the same face, with no intermediate at all.

Because there is no carbocation, regiochemistry cannot be set by cation stability. Two reasons are usually given — sterics, and the polarity of the B–H bond — and they are not two reasons at all. They are one picture: the four-center transition state.

Boron has an empty orbital, so the pi bond starts donating into it before anything has finished moving. That donation is uneven. Electron density flows from the alkene toward boron, which leaves a partial positive charge developing on the alkene carbon further from boron — and that charge, like any positive charge, prefers the more substituted carbon. Boron therefore ends up on the less substituted carbon, which is also the less hindered one, so sterics and electronics push the same way instead of competing. Meanwhile the hydrogen is already on its way from boron to the carbon carrying the partial positive charge, through the same four-membered arrangement of B, H and the two carbons.

Because all four atoms are joined in one ring at the moment of reaction, the boron and the hydrogen have no choice about which face they come from: whichever face the boron approaches is the face the hydrogen is delivered to. That is where syn addition comes from — not a preference, a geometric consequence of the transition state having a closed ring.

A subsequent oxidation with H₂O₂ and NaOH replaces the boron with OH, with complete retention of both position and configuration. The net result is OH on the less substituted carbon, added syn with the hydrogen — the opposite regiochemistry from acid-catalyzed hydration, hence "anti-Markovnikov."

Because hydroboration–oxidation has no carbocation, it never rearranges. That makes it valuable twice over: when you need the anti-Markovnikov alcohol, and when you need any alcohol from a substrate whose cation would shift. Its syn stereochemistry is a third benefit — both new groups arrive on the same face, so on a ring the product is a single, predictable diastereomer.
Worked example — one alkene, two alcohols

Start with 1-methylcyclohexene and ask for each hydration route.

H₃O⁺: protonation gives the tertiary cation at C1; water attacks there. Product: 1-methylcyclohexan-1-ol, the Markovnikov alcohol, with OH on the more substituted carbon.

BH₃ then H₂O₂/NaOH: boron adds to the less substituted C2 and hydrogen to C1, both from the same face. Oxidation swaps the boron for OH without disturbing it, so the OH sits where the boron was.

Now read the stereochemistry carefully, because this is where students lose the mark. "Syn" describes the two groups that were added: the new OH on C2 and the new H on C1 are cis to each other. The methyl on C1 was already there and is not part of that relationship — and since C1 now carries the new hydrogen on the front face, its methyl must be on the back face. So the OH (front, C2) and the methyl (back, C1) are on opposite sides: the product is trans-2-methylcyclohexan-1-ol.

Syn addition does not mean a cis product. It means the two new groups are cis, and what that does to everything else depends on what was already on the ring.

Same starting material, two different regiochemistries and two different stereochemical outcomes, chosen by reagent.

Radical HBr addition: anti-Markovnikov by another route

In the presence of peroxides (ROOR), HBr addition abandons the ionic pathway for a radical chain, and it is worth writing out in the same three-part form used for radical halogenation in Alkanes & Conformations, because that form is what an exam asks you to reproduce. Every arrow here is a fishhook — single-barbed, moving one electron.

StageStepWhat it does
InitiationRO–OR → 2 RO·The weak O–O bond breaks on gentle heat or light. This happens once.
InitiationRO· + H–Br → RO–H + Br·Makes the chain carrier. Now the cycle can start.
Propagation 1Br· + C=C → ·C–C–BrBromine adds first, to the less substituted carbon, leaving the more stable radical.
Propagation 2·C–C–Br + H–Br → H–C–C–Br + Br·The radical takes a hydrogen and hands back a Br·, which starts the cycle again.
TerminationBr· + Br·, or two carbon radicals combiningTwo carriers meet and the chain stops. Rare, because carriers are dilute.

The two propagation steps are the reaction. Notice that they consume one alkene and one HBr and regenerate the bromine radical, so a single initiation event can turn over thousands of molecules — and notice which species adds first: it is the bromine radical, not H⁺. That one difference is the whole regiochemical story.

The bromine radical adds to whichever carbon leaves the more stable radical behind, and radical stability follows the same 3° > 2° > 1° ordering as carbocations, for the same hyperconjugation reason. So bromine adds to the less substituted carbon, leaving the radical on the more substituted one. That radical then abstracts H from another HBr, propagating the chain.

Net result: Br on the less substituted carbon, H on the more substituted one — precisely the reverse of ionic HBr addition. The product is racemic, since the radical is close to planar.

The peroxide effect works for HBr only — and the reason is which step fails. A chain runs only if both propagation steps are downhill or nearly so; one badly uphill step stalls it. Estimate each from bond strengths, as heat in minus heat out.
  • HBr works. Br· adding to the pi bond is about −5 kcal/mol, and the radical taking H from H–Br is about −11. Both favorable, neither so exothermic as to be indiscriminate.
  • HCl fails at the hydrogen abstraction. Cl· adds to the alkene readily enough (about −19 kcal/mol, since a C–Cl bond at 84 kcal/mol comfortably repays the 65 kcal/mol pi bond). It is the second step that stalls: pulling H off H–Cl costs 103 kcal/mol and returns a C–H worth about 98, so it is roughly +5 and uphill.
  • HI fails at the addition. The abstraction step is easy for HI (H–I is only 71 kcal/mol, so that step is about −27). The first step is the problem: a C–I bond is worth only about 56 kcal/mol against the 65 kcal/mol pi bond it has to break, roughly +9 and uphill.
So the two failures are at opposite ends of the cycle: HCl cannot abstract, HI cannot add. Both therefore ignore peroxides entirely and add by the ordinary ionic Markovnikov route. Getting these the wrong way round is a favorite exam trap, and the way to keep them straight is to remember that the C–X bond formed gets weaker down the group while H–X gets weaker too — so the halogen that is bad at holding onto carbon is the one that cannot start, and the halogen that holds its own hydrogen too tightly is the one that cannot finish.

What carries forward

Read the table as two pairs. Two routes run through an electron-poor intermediate and put the oxygen or halogen on the more substituted carbon; two avoid one and put it on the less substituted carbon. Choosing a reagent is choosing which column you want to be in — and, if the substrate has a cation that would shift, choosing whether a cation is allowed to exist at all.

The pattern established here — that changing the mechanism changes the regiochemistry — recurs throughout the rest of the course. It is why kinetic and thermodynamic enolates give different products in Enolate Chemistry, why 1,2- and 1,4-addition compete in conjugate addition, and why ortho/para and meta directors exist in Aromatic Chemistry. Whenever two reagents give opposite products from the same substrate, the explanation is that they are running different mechanisms.
