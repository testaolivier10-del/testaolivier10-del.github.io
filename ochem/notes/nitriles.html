Nitriles

Practice this — interactive lesson

A nitrile has no carbonyl, no leaving group and nothing that looks like the RCO–X skeleton the rest of this chapter is built on. It belongs here anyway, for a bookkeeping reason that turns out to be the practical one.

Structure first

The nitrile carbon is sp hybridized, exactly like an alkyne carbon: two sigma bonds, two pi bonds, and a bond angle of 180°. R–C≡N is linear along those three atoms, the C≡N bond is 116 pm, and the nitrogen keeps one lone pair in an sp orbital pointing straight out from the carbon — which is why a nitrile is a weak Lewis base and will coordinate a metal.

The triple bond is strongly polarized toward nitrogen, so nitriles carry large dipole moments and boil high for their size: acetonitrile is 41 g/mol and boils at 82 °C, against propyne at 40 g/mol boiling at −23 °C. That large dipole with no O–H or N–H to donate is exactly the definition of a polar aprotic solvent, which is why acetonitrile — miscible with water, and so easy to run reactions and chromatography in — is one of the standard ones met in Substitution & Elimination. One further consequence of that polarization: a hydrogen on the carbon next to a C≡N is weakly acidic, pKa about 25 for CH3CN, for the same delocalization reason an alpha hydrogen next to a carbonyl is — the anion puts its charge on the nitrogen.

Naming: count the nitrile carbon as C1 and add -nitrile to the parent alkane, so CH3CH2CH2C≡N is butanenitrile — four carbons, and the one bearing the nitrogen is one of them.

Same rung as a carboxylic acid

Count the bonds from the nitrile carbon to a heteroatom: three, all of them to the same nitrogen. That is the same count a carboxylic acid carbon carries to oxygen, so the two sit on the same rung of the oxidation ladder — the +3 level shared by the acid, the ester, the amide and the acid chloride. Converting a nitrile into any of them is substitution, not oxidation, and needs no oxidant. Getting off the rung, down to an aldehyde or an amine, needs a reducing agent, exactly as it does for the others.

Where nitriles come from

  • SN2 on a primary alkyl halide. RCH2X + NaCN → RCH2CN. Cyanide is a strong nucleophile and only a weak base — HCN has a pKa near 9 — which is the exact combination SN2 wants. The limits are the usual ones: secondary substrates react sluggishly and give elimination alongside, and tertiary substrates give no nitrile at all. What replaces it is decided by the solvent rather than by cyanide, which is too weak a base (HCN pKa 9.2) to force a brisk E2: in a protic solvent such as aqueous ethanol the halide mostly solvolyzes to the alcohol, while in a polar aprotic solvent such as DMSO, with heat and nothing else on offer, elimination takes over and you get the Zaitsev alkene. The fact worth carrying is the negative one — no nitrile, so plan the chain extension around a primary substrate.
  • Dehydration of a primary amide with SOCl2 or P2O5. RCONH2 → RCN. A sideways move along the rung, as promised.
  • From a cyanohydrin, made by adding cyanide to an aldehyde or ketone. That route puts an OH on the carbon next door, and the carbonyl chapter covers it.
  • From an aryl diazonium salt with CuCN, the Sandmeyer reaction. Electrophilic substitution cannot install a CN group at all — there is no electrophilic cyanide reagent — so an aryl nitrile is always made some other way. Sandmeyer is the usual one; dehydrating an aryl amide is another, and an activated aryl halide will take cyanide by SNAr. Preview: diazonium salts and SNAr are taught in Aromatic Follow-Through. All that is needed here is the negative fact — an aromatic ring will not accept a CN group by the electrophilic route, because no electrophilic cyanide reagent exists.

What a nitrile does

ReagentProductThrough
H3O+ or HO−, heatRCOOHthe amide
LiAlH4, then H2ORCH2NH2full reduction
DIBAL-H, 1 equiv, −78 °C, then H3O+RCHOan imine anion
R′MgX or R′Li, then H3O+RCOR′an imine

Hydrolysis runs under aqueous acid or base with heat, and it goes through the amide on the way. That intermediate is real enough to catch: milder conditions stop the reaction at RCONH2.

LiAlH4 delivers two hydrides and gives a primary amine. Watch the carbon count: the nitrile carbon is the one that becomes the CH2, so RCN gives RCH2NH2 with no carbon gained or lost. This is the row in the reduction table that sits beside the amide row, and for the same reason — nitrogen stays attached.

DIBAL-H, one equivalent at low temperature, stops one hydride earlier. The product of that single addition is a metalated imine anion, and an imine anion is not an electrophile, so it simply sits there until water is added. Hydrolysis on workup gives the aldehyde. The contrast with LiAlH4 is the same one the reduction section draws for esters: the reagent is crippled, not rationed.

A Grignard or organolithium adds once and cannot add twice, because the intermediate here is an imine salt rather than a carbonyl. Aqueous workup hydrolyzes it to a ketone. That is the whole contrast with an ester, which expels alkoxide to a ketone that is hungrier than the ester was and gets attacked again; a nitrile never generates a carbonyl until the Grignard is gone.

One carbon, four destinations

Put the four together and the nitrile is a one-carbon extension that can be cashed out as an acid, an amine, an aldehyde or a ketone. Combined with the SN2 route, RX → RCN → RCOOH is the standard way to lengthen a chain by one carbon and finish with an acid.

Worked example — 1-bromopropane to pentan-2-one

You have 1-bromopropane, CH3CH2CH2Br, and need pentan-2-one, CH3CH2CH2COCH3.

Count first. Three carbons in, five out, so two must be added — and they arrive from different places. One is the nitrile carbon, which ends up as the carbonyl carbon; the other is the methyl the organometallic brings, which ends up on the far side of it.

Step 1. NaCN. SN2 on a primary bromide gives butanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2C≡N — four carbons, the new one being the nitrile carbon.

Step 2. CH3MgBr, then H3O+. The Grignard adds once to the nitrile carbon, giving a magnesium imine salt. That salt is an anion, not an electrophile, so no second addition is possible however much reagent is present. Aqueous workup hydrolyzes the imine to the ketone: CH3CH2CH2–CO–CH3, pentan-2-one.

Check the alternative. Hydrolyzing the nitrile instead gives butanoic acid, and adding a methyl Grignard to that fails — the acidic O–H destroys the reagent, and the carboxylate left behind is inert. Nitrile-to-ketone works precisely because there is no acidic proton and no carbonyl until after the organometallic has been consumed.

Count carbons before you pick a reagent. The commonest nitrile mistake is losing track of the CN carbon. RCN → RCH2NH2 with LiAlH4 keeps every carbon — the nitrile carbon becomes the CH2. RCN → RCHO with DIBAL-H keeps every carbon — the nitrile carbon becomes the CHO. RCN → RCOR′ with a Grignard gains one, from the organometallic. And RX → RCN gained one already, at the very first step. Write the carbon count over each arrow and these four stop blurring together.
The Grignard route — RX → RMgX, then CO2, then H3O+ — reaches the same product with the same one added carbon, and the two are complements rather than duplicates. The cyanide route demands a primary, unhindered substrate but tolerates acidic protons — an O–H or an N–H does not touch it. It is not a general tolerance: cyanide is a nucleophile, so a ketone elsewhere in the molecule becomes a cyanohydrin, which is one of the ways to make a nitrile listed three bullets above. The Grignard route tolerates a hindered or tertiary substrate but is destroyed by any O–H, N–H or carbonyl in the molecule. Look at the substrate, not at the target, to choose.

Spotting one

The C≡N stretch appears near 2250 cm−1, sharp and medium. The 2500–2000 window is otherwise close to empty, which is what makes the peak diagnostic.
