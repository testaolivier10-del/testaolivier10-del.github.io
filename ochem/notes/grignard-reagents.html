Grignard reagents

Practice this — interactive lesson

A Grignard reagent is made by stirring an alkyl, vinyl or aryl halide with magnesium turnings in dry ether. The metal inserts into the carbon–halogen bond:

R–Br + Mg → R–MgBr

Not every halide goes in equally readily. Iodides and bromides insert easily; chlorides are sluggish and usually need THF and heat; fluorides do not react at all, because the C–F bond is far too strong. Aryl and vinyl halides work — which is worth noticing on its own, since the same halides refuse SN2 and SN1 completely — but they normally need THF rather than diethyl ether. And the halide has to be otherwise inert: a bromide carrying an O–H, an N–H, a carboxyl or a carbonyl of its own cannot be turned into a Grignard, because the first molecules of reagent are destroyed by the next molecules of starting material.

Nothing is added and nothing leaves. The same carbon that was δ+ in the halide comes out δ− in the product, which is the polarity reversal the previous section described.

Why the solvent has to be ether

Diethyl ether or THF is not incidental. The magnesium of a Grignard is electron-poor, and the two lone pairs on the ether oxygen coordinate to it, which is what keeps the reagent in solution and stable enough to use. Try the same reaction in a hydrocarbon and it does not work; try it in anything with an O–H and the reagent destroys the solvent.

Dry means dry, and two different things go wrong when it is not. With wet glassware the magnesium surface stays coated in oxide and hydroxide, so the insertion never initiates: the turnings sit there unreacted and the gray cloudiness that signals a working Grignard never appears. And any reagent that does form is protonated to R–H before it can meet the electrophile, so that much of the halide is wasted as the alkane. Either way the symptom is not a mess — it is nothing happening.

What a Grignard attacks

The mechanism is always the same: the nucleophilic carbon attacks an electrophilic carbon, and an acidic workup protonates whatever anion is left. What differs is the electrophile, and the products are worth learning as a set, because the carbon count changes differently in each case.

Two of these add exactly one carbon, and they are the pair to memorize together because they end in different places: formaldehyde gives a primary alcohol and CO2 gives a carboxylic acid. A nitrile is the row people miscount. R′C≡N hands over its own carbon and the R′ group attached to it, so R–MgX plus CH3C≡N gives the ketone R–CO–CH3 — two carbons longer, not one. It would be a one-carbon step only for HCN, which is not used this way and would give an aldehyde rather than a ketone. Asked to lengthen a chain by exactly one carbon, choose between formaldehyde and CO2 by what functional group you want at the end.

The ester problem

An ester looks like a way to make a ketone, and it is not. The Grignard adds once, the tetrahedral intermediate collapses by expelling the alkoxide, and what is left is a ketone that is more electrophilic than the ester was. The second equivalent of Grignard attacks it immediately.

So the reaction takes two equivalents of Grignard (in practice an excess), the product is a tertiary alcohol carrying two identical R groups from the reagent, and stopping at the ketone by using one equivalent does not work — the ketone is consumed faster than it forms. An acyl chloride ends up in the same place by a different route. It is the most electrophilic acyl derivative, so the ester argument does not transfer — the ketone it gives is less reactive than the acyl chloride was. It is still reactive enough to be attacked as fast as it forms, which is enough to spoil the selectivity.

If you actually want the ketone, you need something that cannot add twice. A Gilman reagent is one answer — it stops after a single addition to an acyl chloride, and the next section but one explains why. The other answer changes the substrate instead of the reagent.

A Weinreb amide, R–CO–N(CH3)OCH3, is an ordinary amide whose nitrogen carries an extra methoxy group, and that methoxy is the whole trick. The Grignard adds once to give the usual tetrahedral alkoxide — but here the magnesium is held between the new O− and the oxygen of that OCH3, in a stable five-membered ring. A metal gripped by two donor atoms of the same molecule like this is said to be chelated. Because the intermediate is locked, it cannot collapse to a ketone while reagent is still in the flask, and no second addition is possible. The ketone appears only when water breaks the ring open on workup — exactly the argument the carboxylate dianion makes in the next section, reached by a different route.

Grignards as bases, which is usually the problem

Every one of the reactions above assumes the substrate has no acidic hydrogen. Put an alcohol, a carboxylic acid, an amine, a thiol or a terminal alkyne anywhere in the molecule and the Grignard removes that proton instead, giving R–H and a magnesium salt. The reagent is consumed and the carbonyl is untouched.

Three routes around it, in the order you should consider them:

  • Reorder. Do the Grignard step before the acidic group is installed. Free, and usually possible.
  • Use extra reagent. One equivalent to kill the acidic proton and a second to do the chemistry. Wasteful but sometimes simplest.
  • Protect. A silyl ether on the alcohol — an R₃Si group capping the oxygen, so that there is no O–H left to quench the reagent — removed with fluoride afterwards. Two extra steps, so it is the last resort rather than the first; protecting groups are collected in Synthesis & Retrosynthesis.
Worked example — pentan-1-ol from 1-bromopropane

Step 1 — count carbons. The halide has three, the target has five. Two carbons have to arrive from the electrophile.

Step 2 — decide what the new functional group must be. The target is a primary alcohol and the OH sits at the far end of the two new carbons. That is the epoxide row of the table and nothing else: ethylene oxide adds two carbons and leaves the oxygen on the terminal one.

Step 3 — check the alternatives and say why they fail. Formaldehyde would add one carbon, giving butan-1-ol. Acetaldehyde adds two but puts the OH on a carbon that already carries a methyl, so you get pentan-2-ol, a secondary alcohol. CO2 adds one carbon at the acid level. Only the epoxide lands in the right place.

Step 4 — write the conditions in order. (i) Mg, dry diethyl ether; (ii) ethylene oxide; (iii) H3O+. The third step is not optional bookkeeping — what the second step leaves is a magnesium alkoxide, and the alcohol only exists after it is protonated.

Step 5 — check the regiochemistry of the opening. There is no acid present when the epoxide is attacked, so no carbocation character develops and the reaction is a plain SN2: backside attack at the less hindered carbon. Ethylene oxide is symmetrical, so it does not matter here — but with 2-methyloxirane it decides the answer, and the nucleophile still takes the CH2 end.

Common trap. Reversing steps (ii) and (iii), or writing them in the same flask. Add water at any point before the epoxide and there is no reagent left to do anything with.

What carries forward

A Grignard is nucleophilic carbon that adds once to an aldehyde or ketone and twice to an ester, gives a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol depending on what it hit, and is destroyed by any acidic proton in the flask. When a route needs a C–C bond next to an oxygen, this is almost always the reaction — and when a route on paper does not work in practice, an acidic hydrogen is almost always the reason.
