Grignard reagents

Practice this — interactive lesson

Stir bromoethane with magnesium turnings in dry diethyl ether, and the magnesium slowly dissolves. The product is ethylmagnesium bromide, CH3CH2MgBr. The metal has inserted itself into the carbon–bromine bond.

A compound R–MgX made this way from a halide R–X is a Grignard reagent. R is the carbon group and X is the halogen. In general:

R–Br + Mg → R–MgBr

Nothing is added and nothing leaves. The magnesium simply ends up between the carbon and the halogen. The carbon that was δ+ in the halide is δ− in the Grignard reagent, so it now behaves as a carbon nucleophile. This is the polarity flip that Why C–metal means nucleophilic carbon explained.

Which halides work

The halogen matters. Iodides and bromides react with magnesium easily. Chlorides are sluggish and usually need heat and THF (tetrahydrofuran, a ring-shaped ether). Fluorides do not react at all, because the C–F bond is far too strong.

The kind of carbon matters much less. Bromobenzene, a bromine on a benzene ring, gives phenylmagnesium bromide, PhMgBr (Ph is short for phenyl, a benzene ring used as a group). A halogen on a benzene ring makes an aryl halide, and a halogen on a C=C carbon makes a vinyl halide. Both refuse SN2 and SN1 completely, yet both form Grignard reagents. Forming a Grignard reagent is not a substitution at carbon, so the rules that block SN2 and SN1 do not apply. Vinyl halides and aryl chlorides usually need THF rather than diethyl ether.

Nothing else in the halide may react with the reagent. A bromide that also carries an O–H, an N–H, a carboxyl group or a C=O cannot be turned into a Grignard reagent. The first molecules of reagent to form react with that group on the starting material still in the flask. Why C–metal means nucleophilic carbon set out this test, and the section on Grignards as bases, below, shows how to get around it.

Why the solvent has to be ether

Look at the magnesium in CH3CH2MgBr. It forms only two bonds, one to carbon and one to bromine, so it has just four valence electrons around it. That makes it electron-poor. An ether oxygen has two lone pairs and can share one of them with the magnesium.

Two ether molecules do this, each giving one lone pair, and that brings the magnesium to eight electrons. This coordination keeps the reagent dissolved and stable enough to use.

Diethyl ether and THF both work, and THF binds the magnesium more strongly, as Ether chemistry noted. A hydrocarbon solvent has no oxygen to offer, and in it the reaction does not work. A solvent with an O–H, such as ethanol or water, protonates the reagent as fast as it forms.

Dry means dry, and water spoils a Grignard reaction in two separate ways. With wet glassware the magnesium surface stays coated in oxide and hydroxide, so the insertion never starts. The turnings sit there unchanged, and the gray cloudiness that signals a working Grignard never appears. Any reagent that does form is protonated to R–H before it meets the electrophile, and that much of the halide is wasted as the alkane. Either way, the symptom is not a mess. Nothing seems to happen at all.

What a Grignard attacks

Methylmagnesium bromide adds to propanal, and acidic workup gives butan-2-ol. Nucleophilic addition drew this mechanism. The nucleophilic carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon, the C=O π bond moves onto oxygen, and H3O+, added afterward, protonates the alkoxide.

Every reaction in this section follows that pattern. What changes is the electrophile, and with it the product and the number of carbons the chain gains. The table lists the set.

The first three rows follow the counting rule from Nucleophilic addition. Count the carbon groups on the carbonyl carbon, then add one for the Grignard reagent. The total is the number of carbon groups on the carbinol carbon, the carbon that carries the OH, and that number is the class of the alcohol.

Two rows add exactly one carbon, and they are worth learning as a pair because they end in different places. Formaldehyde gives a primary alcohol. CO2 gives a carboxylic acid, a reaction drawn in Carboxylic acids. When a question asks you to lengthen a chain by exactly one carbon, choose between these two by the functional group you want at the end.

The nitrile row is the one people miscount. A nitrile R′C≡N hands over its own carbon and the R′ group attached to it. So R–MgX plus CH3C≡N gives the ketone R–CO–CH3, which is two carbons longer than R, not one. Nitriles shows why this reaction stops at the ketone. HCN cannot serve as a one-carbon nitrile here. It is an acid (pKa about 9), so it simply protonates the Grignard reagent.

The ester problem

Ethyl propanoate looks like a route to a ketone. Add CH3MgBr once, lose ethoxide, and butan-2-one should be left. It is not. The butan-2-one reacts with more CH3MgBr, and the product after workup is 2-methylbutan-2-ol. Nucleophilic addition met this case briefly, and this section gives the full argument.

The Grignard reagent adds once, and the tetrahedral intermediate collapses by expelling ethoxide. What is left is a ketone that is more electrophilic than the ester was. The ester’s OEt group donated electron density into its C=O, and that group has now left. So the next molecule of Grignard reagent attacks the ketone at once.

The reaction therefore takes two equivalents of Grignard reagent, and in practice an excess. The product is a tertiary alcohol carrying two identical R groups from the reagent. Using only one equivalent does not stop it at the ketone, because the ketone is consumed faster than it forms.

An acyl chloride ends up in the same place for a different reason. It is the most electrophilic acyl compound, so the ketone it forms is less reactive than the acyl chloride was. That ketone is still reactive enough to be attacked as fast as it forms, though. So an acyl chloride and a Grignard reagent also give the tertiary alcohol, not the ketone.

To make a ketone, you need a reaction that cannot add twice. A nitrile is one: it gives the ketone only on workup, after the reagent is gone. A Gilman reagent is another. It is a copper-based carbon nucleophile, taught later in this chapter in Cuprates and conjugate addition, and it adds only once to an acyl chloride. The third answer, below, changes the substrate instead of the reagent.

The Weinreb amide of propanoic acid is CH3CH2–CO–N(CH3)OCH3. It is an amide whose nitrogen carries a methyl group and a methoxy group, OCH3. CH3MgBr adds to its C=O once, giving the usual tetrahedral intermediate with an oxygen bonded to magnesium.

Now the methoxy oxygen does its job. It shares a lone pair with the same magnesium, so the magnesium is gripped by two oxygens of one molecule. Together they close a five-membered ring of carbon, oxygen, magnesium, oxygen and nitrogen. A metal held by two donor atoms of the same molecule like this is chelated.

The chelate holds together while the reaction runs. It cannot collapse to a ketone while reagent is still in the flask, so no second addition is possible. Water breaks the ring open during workup, and only then does butan-2-one form.

Grignards as bases, which is usually the problem

Try to add CH3MgBr to the ketone of 4-hydroxybutan-2-one. The reagent never reaches the ketone. It takes the proton from the O–H instead and leaves as methane. Why C–metal means nucleophilic carbon explained why the proton wins.

Every reaction above assumes the substrate has no acidic hydrogen. An alcohol, a carboxylic acid, an amine, a thiol (R–SH) or a terminal alkyne anywhere in the molecule gives up its proton to the Grignard reagent. The products are R–H and a magnesium salt. The reagent is used up and the carbonyl is untouched.

Two routes get around the problem. Consider them in this order:

  • Reorder. Run the Grignard step before the acidic group is put in or unmasked. It costs no extra steps and is usually possible. For an N–H, an S–H, a carboxylic acid or a terminal alkyne, reordering is the answer, because this course has no mask for those groups that survives a Grignard reagent.
  • Protect. When the alcohol has to be present during the Grignard step, cap it as a silyl ether. That is an R3Si group on the oxygen in place of the hydrogen, so no O–H is left to quench the reagent. Fluoride removes it afterward. The cost is two extra steps, one on and one off. Protecting groups, later in the course, teaches the method. A C=O in the halide can be masked in the same spirit, as the acetal from Acetals & hemiacetals.
A second equivalent of Grignard reagent, one to use up the proton and one to react, sometimes works. It wastes reagent, though, and the yields are often poor and hard to repeat. A question that puts a Grignard reagent next to an acidic proton expects you to reorder or protect.
Worked example — pentan-1-ol from 1-bromopropane

Step 1 — count carbons. The halide has three and the target has five. Two carbons have to arrive with the electrophile.

Step 2 — decide where the new OH must go. The target is a primary alcohol, and its OH sits at the far end of the two new carbons. Only the epoxide row of the table does that. Ethylene oxide adds two carbons and leaves the oxygen on the end one.

Step 3 — check the alternatives and say why they fail. Formaldehyde adds one carbon and gives butan-1-ol. Acetaldehyde adds two, but it puts the OH on a carbon that already carries a methyl, so it gives pentan-2-ol, a secondary alcohol. CO2 adds one carbon and gives a carboxylic acid. Only the epoxide puts the OH in the right place.

Step 4 — write the conditions in order. (i) Mg, dry diethyl ether; (ii) ethylene oxide; (iii) H3O+. Step (iii) matters. Step (ii) leaves a magnesium alkoxide, and the alcohol exists only once that is protonated.

Step 5 — check where the ring opens. No acid is present when the reagent meets the epoxide, so the opening is an SN2 at the less hindered carbon, as in Epoxides. Ethylene oxide is symmetrical, so here it makes no difference. With 2-methyloxirane it decides the answer: the reagent takes the CH2 end, and the product is hexan-2-ol.

Common trap. Swapping steps (ii) and (iii), or writing them as one step. If acid or water goes in before the epoxide, no reagent is left to open it. Mixing the halide, the magnesium and the electrophile together from the start, with no acid, is a real method called the Barbier reaction. The usual order is still to make the reagent first and then add the electrophile to it, slowly and cold.

What carries forward

A Grignard reagent is a carbon nucleophile. It adds once to an aldehyde or ketone and twice to an ester, and gives a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol depending on what it hit. Any acidic proton in the flask destroys it. When a route needs a new C–C bond next to an oxygen, this is usually the reaction to reach for. When a route that works on paper fails in the flask, an acidic hydrogen is usually the reason. Organolithiums and acetylides comes next, with a more reactive relative.
