The Lewis definition

A Lewis acid is an electron pair acceptor. A Lewis base is an electron pair donor. This is broader than Brønsted-Lowry: every Brønsted base is also a Lewis base (it donates its lone pair to a proton), but Lewis acids don't need a proton at all — BF₃, AlCl₃, ZnCl₂, and carbocations are all Lewis acids because they have an empty (or accessible) orbital that can accept an electron pair.

Worked example: BF₃ + NH₃

Boron in BF₃ has only 6 valence electrons around it (an incomplete octet, from the Lewis Structures lesson's exceptions) — it's electron-deficient and has an empty p orbital. NH₃'s nitrogen lone pair donates into that empty orbital, forming a new covalent bond (a dative or coordinate bond) without breaking any existing bond. NH₃ is the Lewis base; BF₃ is the Lewis acid.

This is exactly the nucleophile/electrophile language from Module 2, applied to acid-base chemistry: a Lewis base is a nucleophile, and a Lewis acid is an electrophile. Every nucleophilic attack you'll draw all semester — SN2, carbonyl addition, enolate alkylation — is technically a Lewis acid-base reaction.

Why this matters for mechanisms

Carbocations (Module 6) are Lewis acids — empty p orbital, ready to accept a pair of electrons from a nucleophile. Metal catalysts like AlCl₃ (Friedel-Crafts, Module 13) are Lewis acids that activate an otherwise-unreactive electrophile by accepting electron density from it. The Lewis framework is what lets "acid-base chemistry" describe reactions that never involve a proton.
