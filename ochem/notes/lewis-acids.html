Acids & Bases · Section 22 of 118

Lewis acids/bases

Practice this — interactive lesson

The Brønsted definition explains everything that involves a proton. The Lewis definition explains everything else — and in doing so it reveals that most of the reactions in this course, including ones that look nothing like acid–base chemistry, are acid–base reactions in disguise.

The Lewis definition

A Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor. A Lewis base is an electron-pair donor.

Notice that the proton has disappeared from the definition entirely. That is the point. Every Brønsted base is also a Lewis base — it donates its lone pair to a proton — but a Lewis acid needs no proton at all, only somewhere for an incoming pair to go. BF₃, AlCl₃, FeBr₃, ZnCl₂, TiCl₄ and every carbocation qualify.

"Somewhere to go" covers two different situations, and it is worth separating them, because only the first is the empty-orbital picture. BF₃, AlCl₃ and a carbocation have a genuine hole: six valence electrons and an empty p orbital waiting to be filled. FeBr₃, ZnCl₂, TiCl₄ and SnCl₄ do not — these are metal centers, and a metal accepts a pair by expanding its coordination number instead, taking on one more ligand than it started with. Titanium in TiCl₄ has four bonds and can comfortably hold five or six; accepting a carbonyl oxygen's lone pair simply makes it a five-coordinate complex. The result is identical from the substrate's point of view, which is why both kinds sit in the same list.

The smallest Lewis acid of all is H⁺ itself. A bare proton has no electrons and an empty 1s orbital, so every bond it ever forms is made entirely from a base's donated pair. That is the honest way to state how the two definitions fit together: a Brønsted base is always a Lewis base, and a Brønsted acid works by supplying the Lewis acid H⁺ rather than by being one itself. Textbooks differ on whether to call HCl a Lewis acid; nothing chemical hangs on the answer, and the arrows you draw are the same either way.

The relationship between the two definitions is one of nesting, not of competition. Lewis is the broader category; Brønsted chemistry is the special case in which the pair is donated to H⁺. Both are correct, and chemists use whichever is more convenient for the reaction at hand.

Worked example — BF₃ + NH₃

Boron in BF₃ has only 6 valence electrons — an incomplete octet, one of the exceptions from Foundations — and therefore a genuinely empty p orbital perpendicular to its trigonal planar framework.

One arrow: tail on nitrogen's lone pair, head on boron. The lone pair becomes a new B–N bond, and no existing bond breaks.

Formal charges after: nitrogen has 4 bonds, so +1; boron has 4 bonds and 3 valence electrons, so 3 − 0 − 4 = −1. The product, F₃B–NH₃, is a neutral adduct with an internal charge separation. Boron has gone from trigonal planar to tetrahedral, and now has a full octet.

A bond formed from a pair supplied entirely by one atom is sometimes called a dative or coordinate bond. Once formed it is an ordinary covalent bond in every respect; the name records only its origin.

This is the nucleophile/electrophile vocabulary from How Reactions Happen wearing different clothes: a Lewis base is a nucleophile and a Lewis acid is an electrophile. Every nucleophilic attack you will draw all semester — SN2, carbonyl addition, enolate alkylation, aromatic substitution — is technically a Lewis acid–base reaction. The two vocabularies exist because they grew up in different subfields, not because they describe different physics. And a Lewis base does not need a lone pair: a π bond is a pair of electrons held loosely above and below the internuclear axis, and an alkene donates that pair to an electrophile exactly as ammonia donates a lone pair to boron. Ethene has no lone pairs anywhere and is still a Lewis base, which is the whole of alkene reactivity in Alkenes & Alkynes.

Why this matters for mechanisms

The Lewis framework earns its keep in three places you will meet repeatedly.

Carbocations (Substitution & Elimination) are textbook Lewis acids: empty p orbital, full positive charge, ready to accept a pair from anything nucleophilic within reach. Describing SN1's second step as a Lewis acid–base reaction is not a stretch — it is the most accurate description available.

Worked example — a carbocation meets water

The tert-butyl cation, (CH₃)₃C⁺, is generated in the first step of an SN1 reaction. Water is the solvent. What happens, and who is the acid?

The Lewis acid is the cation. Its central carbon has three bonds and six valence electrons, is trigonal planar and sp², and has a completely empty p orbital perpendicular to that plane. Nothing about it involves a proton.

The Lewis base is water, through either of oxygen's two lone pairs.

One arrow: tail on a water lone pair, head on the cationic carbon. No bond breaks. The new C–O bond is made entirely from oxygen's pair, so it is a dative bond in origin and an ordinary one thereafter.

The bookkeeping. Before: carbon +1, oxygen 0. After: carbon has four bonds and is neutral; oxygen has three bonds and one lone pair, so it is +1. The charge did not vanish — it moved onto the atom that supplied the electrons. Students routinely draw this product neutral on both atoms, and the formal-charge count is what catches it.

What follows is a Brønsted step: a second water molecule removes a proton from that oxonium ion to give the neutral alcohol. So the two definitions run consecutively in one mechanism, one step each, which is the ordinary state of affairs rather than a curiosity.

Lewis acid catalysts activate otherwise unreactive electrophiles. In Friedel–Crafts acylation (Aromatic Chemistry), AlCl₃ accepts a lone pair from the chlorine of an acyl chloride, which weakens the C–Cl bond enough to generate a genuinely free acylium ion — an electrophile powerful enough to attack benzene, which the acyl chloride alone is not. The catalyst did not supply the electrophile; it manufactured one.

Carbonyl activation without acid. Coordinating a Lewis acid such as ZnCl₂ or TiCl₄ to a carbonyl oxygen pulls electron density off the carbon, making it far more electrophilic — the same result protonation achieves, but under neutral conditions that an acid-sensitive substrate can survive.

"Lewis acid" does not mean "acidic" in the everyday sense. BF₃ contains no hydrogen at all, cannot donate a proton, and will not turn litmus paper red. It is an acid strictly in the electron-pair-accepting sense. Conversely, something can be a strong Brønsted acid and an unremarkable Lewis acid. Keep the two scales separate; a species can rank very differently on each.

Hard and soft, briefly

Lewis acids and bases are often sorted as hard (small, charge-dense, not very polarizable — H⁺, Li⁺, BF₃, F⁻, RO⁻) or soft (large, diffuse, polarizable — I⁻, RS⁻, Ag⁺, Cu⁺). The useful generalization is that hard prefers hard and soft prefers soft.

You do not need the full theory. Its immediate use is that it resolves something How Reactions Happen left slightly unsatisfying: thiolate (RS⁻) is a better nucleophile than alkoxide (RO⁻) while being the much weaker base of the two. Size and polarizability were the explanation given there, and hard/soft is the same observation generalized — a large, diffuse, polarizable nucleophile pairs well with a large, diffuse electrophilic carbon, whatever their basicities happen to be.

What carries forward

Lewis acid catalysis appears in Friedel–Crafts chemistry (Aromatic Chemistry), in epoxide opening (Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry), and throughout carbonyl chemistry wherever a substrate cannot tolerate strong protic acid. More broadly, recognizing that nucleophilic attack is Lewis acid–base chemistry means the whole course has fewer independent ideas in it than the list of named reactions suggests.
