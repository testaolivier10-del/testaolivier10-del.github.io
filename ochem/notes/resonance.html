Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons · Section 13 of 121

Resonance

Practice this — interactive lesson

Foundations gave you tools for drawing a molecule as a single fixed structure. This module is about what happens when a single structure is not good enough — when the electrons in a molecule are genuinely spread over more atoms than any one Lewis drawing can show. That spreading out is delocalization, and it is the most important stabilizing effect in organic chemistry. Resonance is how we draw it.

What resonance is

Some molecules and ions cannot be captured by a single Lewis structure. When you can draw two or more valid structures that differ only in where the electrons are — never in where the atoms are — those are resonance structures, conventionally linked by a double-headed arrow (↔).

The real molecule is a single resonance hybrid: a blend of every valid structure, existing at all times. It is not flipping back and forth between them. This is the point people most often get wrong, so it is worth stating in the strongest terms.

Resonance is not equilibrium. Acetate does not spend half its time with the charge on one oxygen and half on the other. It exists permanently in one state in which the charge is spread over both, and neither drawn structure is that state — each is a partial description, in the same way that a front view and a side view are both partial descriptions of one building. The double-headed arrow ↔ means "these are two views of one thing"; the equilibrium arrows ⇌ mean "these are two things interconverting." Using the wrong arrow is a real error, not a notational nicety.

The requirement: conjugation

Resonance is not available everywhere. It requires that the electrons in question have somewhere adjacent to go — specifically, an available p orbital next door that theirs can overlap with.

In practice this means one of three arrangements: a lone pair next to a pi bond; a pi bond next to another pi bond; or a lone pair or pi bond next to an empty p orbital — which in organic chemistry almost always means a carbocation, a carbon with three bonds, a full positive charge and an empty p orbital where its fourth bond would be. A system connected this way is conjugated, and delocalization runs the length of the conjugated system and stops where the conjugation stops.

The word "adjacent" is doing real work. An sp³ carbon between two pi bonds breaks the conjugation entirely, because it has no available p orbital to pass the electrons through. A lone pair with no neighboring pi bond or empty orbital has nowhere to delocalize into, and there is simply no resonance structure to draw.

Work the negative case, because it is the one exams ask about. Take the nitrogen of an ordinary amine, CH₃CH₂–NH₂: the lone pair sits on an sp³ nitrogen whose neighbors are a carbon and two hydrogens, none of which offers a p orbital. No arrow can be drawn, no second structure exists, and the lone pair stays exactly where it is — which is why an amine nitrogen is fully available as a base and a nucleophile. Now move that same nitrogen next to a carbonyl, CH₃–CO–NH₂: its lone pair is adjacent to the C=O pi bond, it delocalizes, and the nitrogen loses most of its availability. Same element, same lone pair, same charge; the only difference is what sits next door.

The same test catches the sp³ break. In CH₂=CH–CH₂–OH the oxygen lone pairs are two bonds from the alkene, with an sp³ CH₂ in between, so they are stranded: no resonance. Move the OH one carbon closer, to CH₂=CH–OH, and the lone pair is adjacent to the pi bond and delocalizes onto the far carbon. When you are asked "is this conjugated?", you are being asked to look for exactly one thing — an unbroken run of p orbitals — and an sp³ atom anywhere in the run ends it.

Worked example — acetate, CH₃COO⁻

The carboxylate carbon is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to another that carries the negative charge and three lone pairs. Push one of that oxygen's lone pairs into a new C–O pi bond, and simultaneously push the existing C=O pi bond up onto the other oxygen as a lone pair. ("Push" here means a curved arrow, the notation the next section teaches in full; for now read it as "these two electrons move from here to there", and check the result against the rules below rather than against the arrows.)

You now have a second structure, identical to the first except that the double bond and the charge have swapped oxygens. Because the two oxygens are equivalent by symmetry, both structures contribute equally — there is no major contributor here.

The experimental payoff: both C–O bonds in acetate are measured at 126 pm, exactly between a C–O single bond (143 pm) and a C=O double bond (123 pm), and both oxygens carry exactly −½ charge. Neither drawn structure says that. The hybrid does.

Legal first, then ranked

Before asking which structure contributes most, ask whether it is a structure at all. Three tests reject almost every invalid drawing, and each one is worth running deliberately until it becomes reflex.

  • No second-row atom may exceed an octet, in any contributor. Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine have four orbitals in the valence shell and therefore a hard ceiling of eight electrons — four bonds for carbon, and a nitrogen or oxygen that already carries lone pairs has fewer bonds available than that. A drawn structure with five bonds to carbon or four to neutral nitrogen is not a poor contributor; it does not exist. This is the single most common way a generated structure is wrong.
  • The electron count is the same in every structure. Add up bonding pairs and lone pairs in one contributor, then in the next; if the totals differ you have created or destroyed electrons, which means you drew an arrow with no source or lost one on the way. Since the atoms are also unchanged, the net charge must match too.
  • The number of unpaired electrons is the same in every structure. Resonance moves pairs; it never splits one. A closed-shell species cannot have a contributor with two unpaired electrons, and a radical has exactly one unpaired electron in every one of its contributors — the allyl radical moves its single electron between the two ends and never gains a second.

Counting contributors is also a legitimate question, and the answers are smaller than students expect. Benzene has five, of which only two matter (the two Kekulé structures; the other three are the high-energy Dewar structures with a long bond across the ring, and they are normally ignored); the allyl anion, allyl cation and allyl radical have two each; naphthalene has three. The point of the count is never the number itself — it is that a molecule with several equivalent contributors has its charge or its pi system genuinely spread over all of them.

Ranking contributors

When resonance structures are not equivalent, they do not contribute equally, and the more stable structure dominates the hybrid. Rank them with these criteria, in roughly this order of importance:

  • Complete octets win. A structure where every second-row atom that can have a full octet has one beats a structure that leaves an atom electron-deficient. This criterion outranks the charge rules below.
  • Fewer and smaller formal charges win. No charges beats +1/−1, which beats +2/−2.
  • Negative charge belongs on the more electronegative atom, and positive charge on the less electronegative one.
  • Like charges should not sit on adjacent atoms. Two adjacent negatives, or two adjacent positives, is electrostatically expensive.
  • More bonds and less charge separation generally wins when everything else is close.

None of the valid structures is "wrong" to draw — each contributes something. These rules only say which contributes more.

Worked example — ranking the two forms of an enolate

An enolate is the anion left when a proton is removed from the carbon next to a carbonyl; take the simplest one, CH₂=CH–O⁻, and rank its two contributors.

Identify the move. A lone pair on the negatively charged oxygen is adjacent to the C=C pi bond. That is the first of the three moves below: lone pair into an adjacent pi bond, with the old pi bond collapsing onto the far atom.

Draw the second structure. The O lone pair becomes a new C=O pi bond, and the C=C electrons move onto the terminal carbon as a lone pair. You now have ⁻CH₂–CH=O.

Recompute the charges. In the first structure oxygen has one bond and three lone pairs: 6 − 6 − 1 = −1, and every carbon is neutral. In the second, oxygen has two bonds and two lone pairs: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0, and the terminal carbon has three bonds and one lone pair: 4 − 2 − 3 = −1. One negative charge in each, as required.

Apply the rules in order. Octets: both structures give every atom a full octet, so rule 1 does not separate them. Number of charges: one each, so rule 2 does not either. Rule 3 decides it — the negative charge is on oxygen in the first structure and on carbon in the second, and oxygen is much the more electronegative. The O⁻ structure is the major contributor.

And then the useful twist: the minor, carbon-centered structure is the one that predicts the reaction. The hybrid carries real electron density on that terminal carbon, which is why an enolate attacks through carbon even though its charge mostly sits on oxygen. Ranking tells you where the electrons are; it does not by itself tell you which end reacts.

Worked example — a protonated carbonyl, where the octet rule outranks the charge rules

Protonate acetone's oxygen and you get (CH₃)₂C=OH⁺. Rank its two contributors.

Identify the move. The C=O pi bond can collapse onto oxygen — the third move below, a pi bond broken onto one atom.

Draw the second structure. The pi electrons become a third lone pair on oxygen, which leaves oxygen with two bonds (to carbon and to hydrogen) and two lone pairs, and leaves carbon with three bonds and six electrons. The charge moves from oxygen to carbon: (CH₃)₂C⁺–OH.

Recompute. Structure one: oxygen 6 − 2 − 3 = +1, carbon neutral. Structure two: oxygen 6 − 4 − 2 = 0, carbon 4 − 0 − 3 = +1. One positive charge each time.

Apply the rules. Rule 3 would prefer the second structure, because positive charge is better placed on the less electronegative atom. But rule 1 comes first, and the second structure leaves carbon with only six electrons. The oxygen-centered structure, with its full octets, is the major contributor, and this is exactly the case the ranking order was written for.

Now say why the minor one matters anyway. It fails only rule 1, and by only one atom; that is a real but not fatal penalty, so it contributes a genuine share of the hybrid. Its share is where the carbonyl carbon's δ+ comes from, and it is why protonating a carbonyl makes its carbon so much more electrophilic: the positive charge was on oxygen in the drawing you started from, and resonance has put some of it on carbon, which is the atom nucleophiles can actually reach.

This ranking explains one of the largest acidity differences in the course. A carboxylic acid (pKa ≈ 4.8) is about 10¹¹ times more acidic than an alcohol (pKa ≈ 16), even though both give up an O–H proton. The difference is entirely in the anion: acetate spreads its negative charge over two equivalent oxygens, while ethoxide has to keep the whole charge on one. Delocalization is stabilization, and the more stable the anion left behind, the more willingly the acid lets its proton go. Acids & Bases names that anion — the conjugate base — and turns this into an exact comparison.

How much stabilization are we talking about?

Resonance stabilization is not a small correction. The allyl cation is roughly 15 kcal/mol more stable than a comparable non-delocalized cation, which is why a carbocation next to a double bond forms as readily as one carrying three carbon neighbors, despite carrying only two — Substitution & Elimination explains why the neighbor count matters in the first place. Benzene is about 36 kcal/mol more stable than the hypothetical non-delocalized "cyclohexatriene," which is the entire reason aromatic compounds behave as a separate class. An amide's C–N bond has roughly 18 kcal/mol of rotational barrier from resonance, enough to lock it flat — which is why peptide bonds are planar and why protein secondary structure exists at all.

The three moves

Nearly every resonance structure you will ever draw comes from one of three electron pushes, and recognizing them turns resonance from guesswork into pattern matching.

Lone pair into an adjacent pi bond. The lone pair becomes a new pi bond, and the old pi bond's electrons move onto the far atom as a lone pair. This is acetate, and it is every amide, ester and enolate.

Pi bond into an adjacent empty orbital. The pi electrons slide over to form a new bond with a carbocation next door, moving the positive charge to the far end. This is the allyl cation, and it is the mechanism behind allylic rearrangement in Substitution & Elimination.

Pi bond broken onto one atom. The pi electrons collapse onto the more electronegative atom, generating a charge-separated structure. For a neutral carbonyl this gives the C⁺/O⁻ form, which fails two rules at once — it separates charge where the first structure had none, and it leaves carbon six electrons short — and is therefore genuinely minor. It is also the whole explanation of carbonyl chemistry, because a minor contributor is not a rare one: it is a permanent fraction of the single real structure, and that fraction is the δ+ on the carbonyl carbon.

Never move atoms. If two structures differ in where a hydrogen is, they are not resonance structures — they are tautomers, genuinely different compounds in equilibrium with each other. Keto and enol forms are the classic pair, and they get their own treatment in Enolate Chemistry. Draw them with ⇌, never with ↔.

What carries forward

Resonance is the single most reusable idea in the rest of the course. It sets the acidity of carboxylic acids, phenols and alpha hydrogens in Acids & Bases and Enolate Chemistry; it stabilizes the carbocations of Substitution & Elimination and Alkenes & Alkynes; it is what makes an amide unreactive and an acid chloride ferocious in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives; it defines aromaticity in Aromatic Chemistry; and the directing effects of Aromatic Chemistry are resonance arguments almost in their entirety.
