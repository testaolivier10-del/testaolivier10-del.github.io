What resonance is

Some molecules and ions can't be captured by a single Lewis structure. When you can draw two or more valid structures that differ only in where the electrons are — never where the atoms are — those are resonance structures. The real molecule is a single hybrid: a blend of every valid resonance structure, not a molecule flipping back and forth between them.

The requirement: conjugation

Resonance only works when a lone pair, negative charge, or pi bond sits directly next to another pi bond or an adjacent p orbital, so their p orbitals can line up and overlap. A system connected this way — alternating single/multiple bonds, or a lone pair next to a pi bond — is conjugated. A lone pair with no neighboring pi bond has nowhere to delocalize into — no resonance to draw.

Worked example: acetate

Acetate (CH₃COO⁻) has two resonance structures, each putting the negative charge on a different one of its two (identical) oxygens. Because the two oxygens are equivalent by symmetry, both structures contribute equally to the hybrid — there's no "major" one here. Experimentally, both C–O bonds are the same length, in between a single and double bond.

When resonance structures aren't equivalent, the more stable one dominates. Between a structure with negative charge on carbon vs. one with it on oxygen, the oxygen version is the major contributor — a more electronegative atom stabilizes negative charge better. This is why carboxylic acids are far more acidic than alcohols: their anion is resonance-stabilized this way.

Ranking contributors: the actual rules

When structures aren't equivalent, rank them with these criteria, roughly in order of importance:

  • Complete octets win. A structure where every atom (that can) has a full octet is more stable than one that leaves an atom electron-deficient.
  • Fewer and smaller formal charges win. A structure with formal charges of 0 beats one with +1/−1, which beats one with +2/−2.
  • Negative charge belongs on the more electronegative atom (and positive charge on the less electronegative one) — this is the acetate-vs-carbon comparison above.
  • Like charges shouldn't sit on adjacent atoms. Two negative (or two positive) formal charges next to each other is highly destabilizing.
  • More bonds (less charge separation) generally wins when everything else is close, since more bonds means more shared, stabilized electron density.

None of these structures is "wrong" to draw — every valid one contributes something to the hybrid. These rules only tell you which one contributes more.
