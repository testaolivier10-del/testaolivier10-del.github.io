How Reactions Happen · Section 43 of 121

Radical halogenation

Practice this — interactive lesson

Alkanes are the one family in this course defined by what they will not do. Every bond is C–C or C–H, both close to nonpolar; there is no lone pair to donate, no empty orbital to accept, no partial charge for a nucleophile or an electrophile to aim at. Nothing you have met so far attacks an alkane, and that is the point of them — they are the inert skeleton everything else hangs off.

There is one reaction. Given a halogen and either heat or ultraviolet light, an alkane will swap a hydrogen for a halogen. It runs by a mechanism unlike anything else here: no charges, no nucleophiles, no arrows pushing pairs of electrons. It moves single electrons instead, and it runs as a chain.

The condition is not decoration. Br₂ and an alkane in a dark flask at room temperature do nothing at all. The light or the heat is what starts the chain, and without it there is no reaction to have. (Looking ahead: an alkene decolorizes bromine water in the dark, with no light needed at all, which is why bromine water is the classic test for a double bond — the reaction it undergoes is taught in Alkenes & Alkynes.)

Homolysis, and the arrow that moves one electron

Every bond you have broken so far broke heterolytically: both electrons left with one atom, producing a cation and an anion, drawn with the ordinary double-barbed curved arrow. A halogen molecule under UV light does the opposite. The bond breaks homolytically — one electron to each atom — and the products are two neutral species each carrying an unpaired electron. Those are radicals.

Because the arrow now carries one electron rather than two, it is drawn with a single barb, and is called a fishhook arrow. Two fishhooks are needed to break one bond, one for each electron. Mixing the two arrow types in a single step is the most common way a radical mechanism is drawn wrongly: if a step produces a charge, it was not a radical step.

Three stages, and only one of them repeats

Initiation makes radicals out of a molecule that had none. Light or heat splits X–X into two X·. This happens rarely — it is the slow, expensive step — and it does not need to happen often, which is the whole trick of a chain reaction. A halogen–halogen bond is weak enough (Br–Br is 46 kcal/mol) to break this way; where a starting material has no such bond, chemists add a dedicated initiator such as a peroxide or AIBN, molecules built around a deliberately weak O–O or C–N bond that falls apart into radicals on gentle warming.

Propagation is two steps, and together they are the reaction. A halogen radical abstracts a hydrogen from the alkane, giving H–X and a carbon radical. That carbon radical then attacks X₂, taking one halogen and releasing the other as a fresh X·. Add the two together and the radicals cancel out: R–H + X₂ → R–X + H–X. Look at what each step does to the radical count — it consumes one and produces one. The chain feeds itself, and a single initiation event can turn thousands of alkane molecules over before it stops.

Termination is any step where two radicals find each other and pair up: X· + X·, R· + X·, or R· + R·. These consume two radicals and make none, which is what ends the chain. They are rare while the reaction runs, because the radical concentration is always tiny — two radicals have to collide, and there are very few of them in the flask at any moment.

Common slip: labelling the second propagation step as termination because it "makes the product." Sort the steps by what happens to the radical count, not by whether a product appears. Up from zero is initiation, unchanged is propagation, down to zero is termination.

Which hydrogen gets taken

An alkane with more than one kind of hydrogen can give more than one product, and which one dominates is decided by the stability of the carbon radical formed when that hydrogen is abstracted. A radical has only seven electrons at that carbon, so anything that shares electron density with it helps — and the neighboring C–H sigma bonds do exactly that, leaking density into the half-filled orbital. This is hyperconjugation, the same donation between a filled sigma orbital and an empty or half-empty one that explained the staggered preference in the Newman section and that ranks carbocations in the Carbocations section of this chapter. More alkyl groups on the carbon means more C–H bonds positioned to donate, so:

3° > 2° > 1° > methyl

That is the carbocation order, for the same reason and in the same words, which is worth noticing: a radical is one electron short of a carbanion and one electron better off than a cation, and both of the electron-deficient species are stabilized by the alkyl groups around them. The difference is one of size. Hyperconjugation into an empty orbital is worth far more than hyperconjugation into a half-filled one, so the spread from methyl to tertiary is tens of kcal/mol for cations and about 5 kcal/mol for radicals. Same ranking, much flatter.

What is genuinely surprising is how much that ordering matters, and that the answer depends on which halogen you picked.

Bromine is fussy and chlorine is not, and to see why you need one principle and three numbers.

The principle is the Hammond postulate from the Energy diagrams section: a downhill step has an early, reactant-like transition state and an uphill step has a late, product-like one.

The numbers are bond dissociation energies — the energy needed to break a bond homolytically, which is also the direct measure of how stable the radical left behind is: the weaker the C–H bond, the more stable the radical. A secondary C–H runs about 98 kcal/mol and a tertiary about 96, against 101 for a primary. The abstraction step breaks a C–H and makes an H–X, so its ΔH is simply the difference:

AbstractionBond brokenBond madeΔH
Cl· + R–HC–H, ~98H–Cl, 103about −5 — downhill
Br· + R–HC–H, ~98H–Br, 87.5about +11 — uphill

Put the two together. Chlorine’s abstraction is exothermic, so its transition state is early and reactant-like: the C–H has barely stretched, the radical has barely formed, and a difference in radical stability of a couple of kcal/mol has almost nothing to act on. Bromine’s abstraction is endothermic, so its transition state is late and product-like: the radical is essentially fully formed by the time the barrier is reached, and every bit of its stability is already showing up in the barrier height. The gap between a 3° and a 1° radical is the same 5 kcal/mol either way — 101 for a primary C–H against 96 for a tertiary — and bromine collects nearly all of it while chlorine collects almost none.

Why only chlorine and bromine

The same ΔH arithmetic decides which halogens are usable at all. H–F is 136 kcal/mol, so fluorine’s abstraction is exothermic by nearly 40 — the chain runs so hot that it is unselective to the point of uselessness and frequently explosive. H–I is only 71, so iodine’s abstraction is endothermic by about 27; that barrier is simply too high to cross at any reasonable temperature, and the overall reaction is uphill besides. Chlorine and bromine are the two that land in the workable window, one at each end of it.

One more practical limit: the product R–X still has C–H bonds, so it can be halogenated again. With chlorine, the halogen already installed pulls electron density out of the C–H bonds nearest it and makes them slightly harder to abstract, so the second chlorine tends to land somewhere else on the chain and the mixture spreads across the molecule. With bromine the carbon that already carries one is the easier one to attack next, because a bromine lone pair helps stabilize the radical beside it, so a second bromine tends to arrive at the same carbon. Either way the product is a mixture of mono- and polyhalogenated material. Laboratory radical halogenations are therefore run with the alkane in large excess, so that a halogen radical is far more likely to meet starting material than product.

Practically: if a synthesis needs one clean product from a substrate with a tertiary C–H, use bromine. Chlorination of the same substrate gives a mixture you would have to separate. "Bromine is selective, chlorine is indiscriminate" is worth memorizing in exactly those terms, because exam questions are usually asking which halogen to choose rather than asking you to draw anything.

Two corrections to the arithmetic

First — count the hydrogens. The table is per hydrogen, so predicting a product ratio means multiplying each rate by how many equivalent hydrogens there are. Statistics and selectivity pull against each other, and a real prediction needs both.

Worked example — predicting the ratio, twice

Chlorination of propane. CH₃CH₂CH₃ has two kinds of hydrogen: six primary (the two end methyls) and two secondary (the middle CH₂).

Multiply count by per-hydrogen rate. Primary: 6 × 1 = 6. Secondary: 2 × 4 = 8. Total 14, so the split is 6/14 and 8/14 — about 43% 1-chloropropane and 57% 2-chloropropane. Four-to-one selectivity turns into a nearly even mixture, because there are three times as many primary hydrogens to be found.

Bromination of 2-methylpropane. (CH₃)₃CH has nine primary hydrogens and one tertiary.

Primary: 9 × 1 = 9. Tertiary: 1 × 1600 = 1600. Total 1609, so the tertiary bromide is 1600/1609, about 99%. A nine-to-one head start counts for nothing against a sixteen-hundred-fold preference.

The same substrate with chlorine. Primary: 9 × 1 = 9. Tertiary: 1 × 5 = 5. Total 14, so the primary chloride is 9/14, about 64% — the major product is now the one from the less stable radical. That is the single cleanest demonstration of the difference: on one molecule, bromine gives 99% of one isomer and chlorine gives a two-thirds/one-third mixture of the other.

Counting the products in the first place is half the marks in this kind of question. Two hydrogens are equivalent, and so give the same monohalogenation product, when swapping them leaves an identical molecule. Propane has two sets and so two monochlorides; 2-methylpropane has two sets and so two; butane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃) has two sets — the six ends and the four middles — and therefore exactly two monochlorides, not four. Find the sets first, then apply the rates.

Second — the intermediate is flat. The carbon radical is essentially planar, with its unpaired electron in a p orbital, so a halogen can arrive on either face with equal ease. If the reaction creates a new stereocenter, the product is racemic. A radical mechanism cannot deliver a single enantiomer, and that is a structural fact about the intermediate rather than a limitation of the conditions. This is the same argument Stereochemistry made about any planar intermediate: attack on two equivalent faces gives the two enantiomers in equal amounts, and a 50:50 mixture is racemic and optically inactive.

Looking ahead: allylic bromination

This subsection is a preview. It needs alkenes, which arrive in Alkenes & Alkynes, and it is placed here because the mechanism is the chain you have just learned rather than anything new. Read it for the logic and come back to it when you have met a double bond properly.

One position is far easier to halogenate than any ordinary alkane carbon: the one next door to a double bond. (The carbon next to a benzene ring behaves the same way for the same reason, and Aromatic Chemistry calls that position benzylic.) Abstracting a hydrogen there gives a radical whose unpaired electron is delocalized by resonance across the pi system, and that extra stabilization makes the abstraction much easier than the substitution pattern alone would suggest.

The difficulty is practical. Adding Br₂ to an alkene does not give allylic substitution — it gives addition across the double bond, which is faster. The solution is NBS (N-bromosuccinimide), a reagent that releases bromine slowly and keeps its concentration permanently low. Addition needs a reasonable concentration of Br₂; the radical chain does not. Starve the reaction of bromine and substitution wins.

Worth noticing

Because the allylic radical is a resonance hybrid, the bromine can end up at either end of that delocalized system, and allylic bromination frequently gives two products with the double bond in two different places. Drawing both resonance forms of the radical is how you predict the second one, and forgetting it is how the second one gets missed.

What carries forward

Radical chemistry reappears twice in this course. The anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr in the presence of peroxides, in the Markovnikov section, is the same chain logic applied to an alkene — initiation, two propagation steps, termination — which is why the regiochemistry flips: a bromine radical adds first, not a proton. And the fragmentation patterns in mass spectrometry are radical chemistry too, since the molecular ion is itself a radical cation that breaks apart to leave the most stable radical it can.

The transferable idea is smaller than the mechanism: a reaction's selectivity tracks how reactive its attacking species is. A very reactive species is unselective because it commits early; a sluggish one is selective because it commits late and gets to see what it is making. That is the same Hammond reasoning that will explain the difference between kinetic and thermodynamic enolates in Enolate Chemistry.
