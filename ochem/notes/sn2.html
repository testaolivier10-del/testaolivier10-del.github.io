The setup

A nucleophile (electron-rich — a lone pair or negative charge to donate) attacks an electrophile (electron-poor — has an atom that can accept that pair). In SN2, the nucleophile attacks an electrophilic carbon and kicks out a leaving group, all in a single step.

Finding the electrophilic carbon

The leaving group (like Br or Cl) pulls electron density toward itself, leaving the carbon it's attached to short on electrons — that carbon is the electrophile, and it's where the nucleophile attacks.

The two arrows

Arrow 1: tail on the nucleophile's lone pair, head on the electrophilic carbon — the new bond forming. Arrow 2: tail on the C–leaving-group bond (starting at the carbon), head on the leaving group — the old bond breaking, with the leaving group keeping both electrons (a heterolytic bond break). Both arrows fire together, in one concerted step — that's the "2" in SN2 (bimolecular: rate depends on both nucleophile and substrate).

Backside attack: the nucleophile attacks from the side of the carbon exactly opposite the leaving group, since that's the only approach that doesn't collide with it. As the new bond forms on one side and the old one breaks on the other, the carbon's three other substituents flip through it — like an umbrella turning inside out in the wind. This inversion of configuration is a direct, testable signature of SN2, and it's why SN2 at a stereocenter always inverts R to S (or vice versa), using the R/S labels from Module 5.

Worked examples on this page

HO⁻ + CH₃Br → CH₃OH + Br⁻. NC⁻ (cyanide) + CH₃Cl → CH₃CN + Cl⁻. In both, the nucleophile's lone pair attacks from the back side of the C–halogen bond, the halogen leaves with the bonding electrons, and the carbon inverts.

Rate law and solvent effects

SN2 is bimolecular: rate = k[Nu][substrate] (both concentrations matter, unlike SN1). Favored by polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMSO, DMF) — these leave the nucleophile's lone pair "naked" and reactive. Polar protic solvents (water, alcohols) hydrogen-bond to and cage the nucleophile, slowing SN2 — the same caging effect from the Nucleophiles lesson.
