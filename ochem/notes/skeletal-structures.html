Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons · Section 11 of 121

Skeletal structures

Practice this — interactive lesson

Almost everything from here to the end of the course is drawn in a notation nobody has explained yet. Lewis structures, where every atom and every bond is written out, are honest and unreadable past about six carbons — cholesterol as a Lewis structure is a page of Cs and Hs that no chemist could scan. So organic chemists draw skeletal structures instead, also called line-angle structures, and they are the language the rest of this book is written in.

The notation is not a shorthand for experts. It is a deliberate decision about what a chemist needs to see, and it rewards exactly the thing that matters: the carbon skeleton and the functional groups hanging off it, with the uninformative hydrogens hidden.

Four rules, and the third is the one people get wrong

1. A line is a bond, and every vertex or end of a line is a carbon. No C is written. A zigzag of five line segments is a six-carbon chain, because the two ends count too — the commonest miscount in the first week is forgetting the ends.

2. Hydrogens on carbon are not drawn. They are inferred: carbon makes four bonds, so whatever is left over after the lines you can see is hydrogen. A vertex with two lines meeting it has two hydrogens; a chain end with one line has three.

Count bonds, not lines on the page, and multiple bonds behave themselves. A double bond is two of carbon's four; a triple bond is three. So on a C=C, a carbon drawn with the double bond and one other bond has one hydrogen, and a carbon drawn with the double bond and nothing else is the CH₂ at the end of a terminal alkene. On a C≡C, an internal alkyne carbon has the triple bond plus one single bond and therefore no hydrogen, while a terminal alkyne carbon has the triple bond and one hydrogen — the ≡C–H whose acidity turns up much later. This is the sub-case people skip, and the next section is nothing but C=C and C=O.

3. Every atom that is not carbon IS drawn, and so are the hydrogens attached to it. O, N, S, Cl and the rest are always written, and an O–H is written OH rather than left implied. This is the asymmetry that trips people: hydrogen on carbon is invisible, hydrogen on anything else is visible. It exists because O–H and N–H hydrogens are the ones that do chemistry — they are acidic, they hydrogen bond, they get removed by a base (a species that takes H⁺ away — Acids & Bases makes this exact) — and a notation that hid them would hide the reactive part of the molecule.

4. Lone pairs are usually left off, and you are expected to supply them. An oxygen drawn with two bonds has two lone pairs whether or not anyone drew them, and when you are pushing arrows you will need them.

Common slip: counting a vertex as a carbon and then also writing in its hydrogens when you redraw the molecule, giving carbon five bonds. Count the lines meeting the vertex first, subtract from four, and what remains is the hydrogen count. Two lines meeting means CH₂, not CH₄.

Charged atoms: what a + or a − does to the hidden half

A drawn charge is not decoration. It changes the bond count and the lone-pair count of the atom it sits on, and since both of those are the things skeletal notation hides, a charge is an instruction about the invisible part of the drawing. Two sections from now you will be pushing arrows out of lone pairs nobody drew, so it is worth working out once.

The arithmetic is the formal-charge arithmetic of Foundations, read backwards. An atom's valence electrons minus its bonds minus its unshared electrons gives the charge; fix the charge and the bond count, and the lone pairs fall out. In practice four cases cover almost everything:

  • Carbon with a + and three lines — a carbocation. Three bonds is all it gets, so no hydrogen and no lone pair; the fourth orbital is empty. If it is drawn with two lines it has one hydrogen, still no lone pair.
  • Carbon with a − and three lines — a carbanion. Three bonds plus one lone pair, and therefore no hydrogen. The lone pair is the whole point of the species and it is never drawn.
  • Oxygen with a − and one line — an alkoxide or carboxylate oxygen. One bond, no hydrogen, three lone pairs. Neutral oxygen with one bond would carry an H and two lone pairs, so the minus sign has traded a hydrogen for a lone pair.
  • Nitrogen with a + and four lines — an ammonium. Four bonds, no hydrogen beyond whatever is written, and no lone pair. Nitrogen's lone pair is exactly what it spent to make the fourth bond.
Worked example — every atom of skeletal acetate

Acetate is drawn as a two-carbon skeleton whose right-hand carbon carries a double-bonded O above it and a single-bonded O⁻ to the right. Go atom by atom.

Left carbon: one line, no charge → 4 − 1 = three hydrogens, CH₃.

Right carbon: one line to the methyl, two to the double-bonded oxygen, one to the charged oxygen — four bonds already, so no hydrogen and no lone pair.

Double-bonded oxygen: two bonds, neutral → no hydrogen, two lone pairs.

Single-bonded oxygen with the minus: one bond, negative → no hydrogen, three lone pairs.

Formula CH₃COO⁻, and the count that matters for the next two sections is the six unshared pairs. When you meet this ion again as a resonance and arrow-pushing example, the tail of the first arrow starts on one of those three pairs on the charged oxygen — a pair that is on the page only because you put it there.

Reading one back

The skill that matters is going both directions, and the direction that feels harder at first is reading a skeletal structure and saying what it is. Work it in a fixed order and it stops being hard.

Worked example — a skeletal structure with an OH

Take a five-vertex zigzag with an OH drawn on the second vertex from the left.

Count the vertices and ends: five carbons. Read the heteroatoms: one oxygen, drawn, with its hydrogen drawn. Fill hydrogens by counting lines: the left end has one line, so CH₃; the second vertex has two lines plus the OH, so one hydrogen, CH; the third and fourth have two lines each, CH₂; the right end has one line, CH₃.

Put it together: CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃, pentan-2-ol. The whole read is one pass, and the hydrogens were never guessed — they were subtracted.

Three more, in the same three steps

A branched chain. A four-vertex zigzag with a short line off the second vertex: five carbons, no heteroatoms. Vertex 1 is a chain end, CH₃. Vertex 2 has three lines — two along the chain and one to the branch — so one hydrogen, CH. Vertices 3 has two lines, CH₂; vertex 4 is an end, CH₃; the branch tip is an end, CH₃. C₅H₁₂, 2-methylbutane.

A ring with a substituent. A hexagon with a line drawn off one corner: seven carbons. The substituted corner has three lines, so one hydrogen; the other five corners have two each, so CH₂; the line's far end is a CH₃. C₇H₁₄, methylcyclohexane. Notice that a ring vertex is read exactly like a chain vertex — the ring is not a special rule, it is just a zigzag that closed.

An alkene. 2-methylbut-2-ene: a four-carbon chain with a methyl on C2 and a double bond between C2 and C3. C1 is an end, CH₃. C2 has the double bond (two bonds), the C1 bond and the methyl bond — four already, so zero hydrogens. C3 has the double bond and one bond to C4, so one hydrogen. C4 is an end, CH₃, as is the methyl. C₅H₁₀: two fewer hydrogens than 2-methylbutane above, which is what one degree of unsaturation costs.

One molecule, several drawings

A skeletal structure is a claim about connectivity, not about the page. Rotate the drawing, bend the zigzag round a corner, start the chain from the other end, draw the branch pointing up instead of down — as long as the same atoms are joined to the same atoms, it is the same compound. This matters more than it sounds: a great deal of exam anxiety comes from seeing a structure that "looks different" from the one in the notes and assuming it is a different molecule.

The 120° angles of the zigzag are convention rather than measurement in one direction and real in the other. They are real in that an sp³ carbon's bonds genuinely splay apart near that angle, so the zigzag is a better picture than a straight line. They are convention in that a drawn chain is free to rotate about every single bond, so no particular drawn shape is the molecule's shape. Alkanes & Conformations is where that freedom gets taken seriously, and Stereochemistry is where the parts of a drawing that are not free to move get their own notation.

Reading the condensed form

Condensed formulas are the other notation you will be handed, usually in an exam stem where a drawing would be expensive to typeset, and they have their own small grammar. Written left to right, each carbon is followed by its own hydrogens: CH₃CH₂CH₂OH is three carbons in a row with the OH on the last. Parentheses mean "hanging off the carbon just written": CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ is a three-carbon chain whose middle carbon carries an OH, and (CH₃)₂CH– is a carbon bearing two methyls and one hydrogen, with its fourth bond going to whatever comes next. (CH₃)₃C– is the same idea with three methyls and no hydrogen. Translating a condensed formula is the skeletal read in reverse: mark every carbon, attach what is bracketed after it, and join the rest in the order written.

Two traps that cost marks rather than understanding. First, a heteroatom at a bend is not an extra carbon: in a zigzag with an O written at one of the bends, that bend is the oxygen and nothing else, so a five-segment chain containing one O is five carbons, not six. Count carbons only at the vertices that carry no letter. Second, a drawn H on a heteroatom is not a chain end: the H of an OH or an NH is a written hydrogen, not an implied carbon, so it never adds to the carbon count — which is exactly why it is written out rather than left implied.

Rings, and why they are the clearest case for the notation

A six-membered ring drawn as a hexagon is six carbons and twelve hydrogens, and the hexagon says that in one stroke where a Lewis structure needs eighteen atomic symbols. A benzene ring is that hexagon with three alternating double bonds, or with a circle inside it to mean the same delocalized system.

Rings are also where the notation starts carrying information a Lewis structure obscures. The shape of a drawn ring is a claim about its geometry: a hexagon drawn flat is a convenience, and the chair drawing you will meet in the Alkanes chapter is the same six carbons drawn to show what they actually do in space. Being fluent enough to stop decoding the notation is what frees you to notice that.

A practical test of fluency: you should be able to read the carbon count and find the functional group without stopping to translate the drawing into a Lewis structure, because every mechanism question in this course assumes you can.

What carries forward

Every drawing after this section uses this notation, and two later chapters depend on being fluent in it. Stereochemistry adds wedges and dashes to skeletal structures to show which bonds come toward you and which go away — meaningless if the underlying skeleton is still being decoded. And mechanisms ask you to push arrows between atoms that are not written down, which only works if you can see the implied carbons and the undrawn lone pairs as clearly as the drawn ones.
