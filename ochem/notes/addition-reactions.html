The alkene is the nucleophile now

This is a genuine role reversal from Module 6: the pi bond's electron density, exposed above and below the sigma framework, makes the alkene itself act as a nucleophile (from the Nucleophiles lesson) — it attacks an electrophile rather than being attacked. The general pattern is called electrophilic addition: an electrophile adds to one carbon of the double bond, generating a carbocation at the other carbon, which a nucleophile then captures.

Hydrohalogenation (HX addition)

With HBr: the pi bond attacks the H of H–Br (curved arrow from the pi bond to H, and the H–Br bond breaks heterolytically, both electrons going to Br⁻). This forms a carbocation at the more substituted carbon (Markovnikov selectivity — see next lesson) and releases Br⁻. Br⁻ then acts as the nucleophile, attacking the carbocation to form the C–Br bond. Net result: H and Br end up on adjacent carbons, with H on the carbon that had more H's already and Br on the more substituted carbon.

This carbocation intermediate is the exact same species from the SN1/E1 lessons — it can rearrange via hydride or alkyl shifts if a more stable carbocation is reachable, exactly as in SN1/E1, giving "unexpected" addition products at a nearby carbon.

Halogenation (X₂ addition) — no carbocation, no rearrangement

With Br₂ or Cl₂, the mechanism is subtly different: the pi bond attacks one Br of Br–Br, but instead of forming a free carbocation, the departing Br⁻ bridges back to form a three-membered bromonium ion (a cyclic, positively-charged intermediate) that shields one face of the molecule. A separate Br⁻ then attacks from the opposite face (backside, SN2-like) in an anti (backside) fashion. Because the second attack is forced to the opposite face, halogenation adds the two halogens with anti stereochemistry — a directly testable, predictable spatial outcome, unlike hydrohalogenation's carbocation which can be attacked from either face.

Acid-catalyzed hydration

Water adds across the double bond using H₃O⁺ (or H₂SO₄/H₂O) as catalyst: the pi bond attacks a proton from H₃O⁺, forming a carbocation (Markovnikov selectivity again); water then attacks the carbocation as the nucleophile; a final deprotonation (by another water molecule) regenerates the catalyst and gives the neutral alcohol. This is the classic bridge to Module 8 — it's the most direct lab synthesis of an alcohol from an alkene, and its carbocation intermediate means it shares hydrohalogenation's rearrangement risk.
