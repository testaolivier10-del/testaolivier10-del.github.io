The ARIO framework

When comparing the acidity of two H atoms, walk through four questions in order: Atom (what atom holds the negative charge in the conjugate base — bigger and more electronegative is more stable), Resonance (can the negative charge delocalize?), Induction (are electronegative atoms nearby pulling electron density away inductively?), Orbital (what hybridization is the atom holding the charge — more s-character is more stable). Atom effects are usually the biggest, so check that first; the others break ties.

Atom effects: two trends, two different reasons

Across a period (left to right), acidity increases with electronegativity: C–H (pKa ≈ 50) < N–H (≈ 38) < O–H (≈ 16) < F–H (≈ 3.2). More electronegative atoms hold negative charge more comfortably.

Down a group, acidity increases even though electronegativity decreases: H–F (pKa 3.2) < H–Cl (≈ −7) < H–Br (≈ −9) < H–I (≈ −10). Size wins here — a larger atom spreads the negative charge over a bigger volume (more polarizable electron cloud), which stabilizes it more than the electronegativity difference costs. This trend often surprises students because it runs opposite to the periodic table's electronegativity arrow.

Rule of thumb: down a group, size dominates and acidity increases with atom size; across a period, electronegativity dominates and acidity increases with electronegativity. Different rows on the periodic table, different reasons.

Resonance and induction

Carboxylic acids (pKa ≈ 4.76) are far more acidic than alcohols (pKa ≈ 16) mainly because of resonance: the carboxylate anion delocalizes its negative charge over two equivalent oxygens (exactly like the acetate example from the Resonance lesson), while an alkoxide keeps its full negative charge on one oxygen. Induction is a separate, additive effect: chloroacetic acid (ClCH₂COOH, pKa ≈ 2.87) is more acidic than acetic acid (pKa 4.76) because the electronegative chlorine pulls electron density away from the carboxylate through the sigma bonds, stabilizing the negative charge further — and this effect fades quickly with distance (an extra carbon between the Cl and the COOH sharply reduces the effect).

Orbital hybridization

Comparing C–H acidity: terminal alkyne C–H (sp, pKa ≈ 25) > alkene C–H (sp², pKa ≈ 44) > alkane C–H (sp³, pKa ≈ 50). More s-character means the orbital holding the lone pair (in the conjugate base) is closer to the nucleus and lower in energy, stabilizing the negative charge. This is purely an orbital effect — none of the other three factors differ between these three C–H bonds.
