Acids & Bases · Section 21 of 118

Factors affecting acidity

Practice this — interactive lesson

The pKa table is a list of results. This section is the explanation behind it. Given any two hydrogens, you should be able to say which is more acidic and why, without looking anything up — and the reasoning is always the same: the more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid. Everything below is a way of asking how stable that anion is.

The ARIO framework

Four structural factors determine anion stability. Check them in this order, because they are roughly in order of size, and the first one that differs usually settles the question.

  • A — Atom. Which atom carries the negative charge in the conjugate base?
  • R — Resonance. Can the charge be delocalized over more than one atom?
  • I — Induction. Are there electronegative atoms nearby pulling density away through sigma bonds?
  • O — Orbital. What is the hybridization of the atom holding the charge?

Atom effects are usually the largest and resonance is usually next; induction and orbital effects most often break ties. But the order is a heuristic, not a law — a strongly resonance-stabilized carbanion can beat an unstabilized alkoxide, and the point of naming all four is that you can tell which are in play.

A — Atom: two trends, two different reasons

Across a period, acidity increases with electronegativity: C–H (pKa ≈ 50) < N–H (≈ 38) < O–H (≈ 16) < H–F (3.2). Twenty-two orders of magnitude from nitrogen to oxygen alone. A more electronegative atom is more willing to hold a negative charge, so its anion is more stable and its acid is stronger.

Down a group, acidity increases even though electronegativity decreases: H–F (3.2) < H–Cl (−7) < H–Br (−9) < H–I (−10). Here size wins. A larger atom spreads the charge over a much bigger, more polarizable electron cloud, and that dilution stabilizes the anion far more than the loss of electronegativity costs. A second effect points the same way and is worth naming, because half the textbooks lead with it: the H–X bond also gets longer and weaker down the group, from 570 kJ/mol for H–F to 300 for H–I, so there is less to break as well as a better home for the charge. Size and polarizability is the explanation this course uses, with bond strength reinforcing it; if you meet the bond-strength argument on its own, it is the same trend seen from the other end.

The rule of thumb: across a period, electronegativity dominates; down a group, size dominates. Both trends increase acidity, but for opposite-sounding reasons, which is why this pair of trends is the most reliably confusing thing in Acids & Bases. The same size argument explains why thiols (pKa ≈ 10.6) are about 250,000 times more acidic than alcohols (pKa ≈ 16), despite sulfur being less electronegative than oxygen.

R — Resonance

Delocalizing a negative charge over several atoms is powerfully stabilizing, and resonance is the biggest single lever in the table after the choice of atom.

Compare ethanol (pKa 16) with acetic acid (pKa 4.76). Both give up an O–H proton, and in both cases the charge lands on oxygen — so factor A is identical. The difference is entirely in what happens next: acetate spreads its charge equally over two equivalent oxygens, while ethoxide must keep all of it on one. Eleven pKa units, a hundred billion-fold, from delocalization alone.

Phenol (pKa 10) sits between them, and the reason is instructive. Phenoxide delocalizes its charge into the aromatic ring — but onto carbon atoms, which hold negative charge much less comfortably than oxygen does. Real stabilization, but less of it than acetate gets. Six units more acidic than an alcohol, five units less acidic than a carboxylic acid.

Note what that comparison does not say. Delocalizing onto carbon is worth less than delocalizing onto oxygen, but it is still worth a great deal: phenol beats an alcohol by six units on carbon delocalization alone. The extreme case is cyclopentadiene, pKa 16 — a plain hydrocarbon, no heteroatom anywhere, as acidic as an alcohol. Its anion is delocalized over five carbons and, with six π electrons in a planar ring, it is aromatic — a special stability you will meet properly in Aromatic Chemistry. Aromatic stabilization is resonance stabilization in its strongest available form, and it is worth thirty-four pKa units against an ordinary alkane. Whenever a conjugate base turns out to be aromatic, expect the acid to be far stronger than its formula suggests.

Worked example — why a 1,3-diketone is as acidic as phenol

A ketone's alpha C–H has a pKa around 20 — already remarkable for a C–H, because the resulting enolate delocalizes onto the carbonyl oxygen.

Put a second carbonyl on the other side, as in pentane-2,4-dione, and the alpha hydrogen between them drops to pKa 9. The anion now delocalizes onto two oxygens rather than one.

That is roughly as acidic as phenol, and more acidic than ammonium — from a hydrogen on carbon. This is why 1,3-dicarbonyls are the workhorse nucleophiles of

Enolate Chemistry: an ordinary base like ethoxide deprotonates them completely.

I — Induction

An electronegative atom pulls electron density through the sigma framework, helping to spread out a nearby negative charge. The effect is real, additive, and short-ranged.

CH₃COOH4.76baseline
ClCH₂COOH2.86one Cl, adjacent
Cl₂CHCOOH1.29two Cl
Cl₃CCOOH0.65three Cl
FCH₂COOH2.59F beats Cl
ClCH₂CH₂CH₂COOH4.52Cl three carbons away

Two lessons from that table. Induction is cumulative, though with diminishing returns — the first chlorine is worth 1.90 pKa units, the second 1.57, the third only 0.64, because each one has less charge density left to pull on — and it dies off sharply with distance. Move the chlorine three carbons from the acid group and almost nothing is left of the effect.

That distance dependence is the fingerprint that distinguishes induction from resonance, which propagates along a conjugated system with little loss.

Induction also runs in the other direction, and the effect is small but real. An alkyl group is weakly electron-donating compared with a hydrogen, so it pushes density toward a nearby negative charge and destabilizes it. Formic acid, HCOOH, has pKa 3.75; acetic acid, CH₃COOH, has 4.76. The only difference is one methyl group in place of one hydrogen, and it costs a full pKa unit. It is also the usual explanation for why ethanol (16) is a shade less acidic than water (15.7).

O — Orbital

Compare the acidity of three carbon–hydrogen bonds where nothing differs but hybridization: terminal alkyne C–H (sp, pKa ≈ 25), alkene C–H (sp², ≈ 44), alkane C–H (sp³, ≈ 50).

More s-character means the orbital holding the lone pair in the conjugate base is closer to the nucleus and lower in energy, which stabilizes the negative charge. An sp orbital is 50% s; an sp³ orbital is 25%. Twenty-five orders of magnitude of acidity difference from nothing but the mixing ratio of the orbitals.

The same effect explains why protonated pyridine (pKaH 5.2) is far more acidic than a protonated alkylamine (pKaH ≈ 10): pyridine's lone pair sits in an sp² orbital and is held more tightly, making it a weaker base.

Worked example — running the framework

Which is more acidic: ethanol or ethanethiol?

A: the charge lands on O in one case and S in the other — same group, so size dominates. Sulfur is bigger and more polarizable, so ethanethiol should be the stronger acid. R: no resonance in either. I: no electronegative substituents in either. O: both sp³.

Only factor A differs, and it points to the thiol. Measured: ethanol 16, ethanethiol 10.6 — the thiol is about 250,000 times more acidic. The framework got it right, and it took one step.

Worked example — when two factors disagree

The single-factor comparison above is the easy case. Here is the one exams use to separate students: which is more acidic, ethanol or pentane-2,4-dione — and the acidic hydrogen of the diketone is on carbon.

A says ethanol. Ethanol's proton leaves an alkoxide, charge on oxygen. The diketone's leaves a carbanion, charge on carbon. Oxygen is far more electronegative, and A is supposed to be the biggest lever, so the alcohol should win comfortably.

R says the diketone. The alkoxide has nowhere to put its charge. The diketone's anion is flanked by two carbonyls and delocalizes onto both oxygens, so the charge spends most of its time on oxygen anyway — and on two of them.

R wins, and not narrowly. Ethanol 16, pentane-2,4-dione 9: the C–H is ten million times more acidic than the O–H. Resonance did not merely offset the atom penalty, it overturned it, because delocalization onto two oxygens is worth more than parking a full charge on one.

The lesson is the one the ARIO ordering states and students forget: the order is a search heuristic, not a ranking of strengths. A is checked first because it settles most comparisons quickly, not because it always wins. When the first factor you find and the second point opposite ways, you have to draw both anions and think — and the tell that you are in that situation is a C–H that is unusually acidic.

Worked example — finding the most acidic proton in a molecule

The commonest exam question on this topic gives you one drawn molecule and asks which hydrogen comes off first. Take 4-hydroxybutan-2-one, CH₃–CO–CH₂–CH₂–OH, and go site by site. There are four kinds of hydrogen on it.

The O–H. Conjugate base: a primary alkoxide, charge on oxygen, no resonance, nothing withdrawing within three bonds. That is an ordinary alcohol — pKa about 16.

The C3 hydrogens, between the carbonyl and the CH₂OH. Conjugate base: an enolate, charge delocalized onto the carbonyl oxygen. A real stabilization, and the reason this C–H is thirty units more acidic than an alkane's — pKa about 20.

The C1 methyl hydrogens, on the other side of the carbonyl. Also alpha to C=O, so also about 20. Two alpha positions, similar acidity; nothing here changes the answer.

The C4 hydrogens, on the carbon bearing the OH. Conjugate base: a bare carbanion next to an oxygen. No resonance, and induction from the adjacent oxygen is worth a unit or two at most against a starting point near 50. Not a contender.

Verdict: the O–H, by about four pKa units — ten thousand to one. This is worth doing slowly because the intuition it corrects is a strong one: a hydrogen next to a carbonyl feels "activated", and it is, by thirty orders of magnitude relative to an alkane. It is still not as acidic as an ordinary alcohol. Rank against the table, not against your sense of how special the site looks.

Two practical notes. If a question asks what one equivalent of sodium hydride does to this molecule, the answer is that it removes the O–H and nothing else. And if it asks for the most basic site rather than the most acidic proton, the answer is the carbonyl oxygen — the carbonyl lone pairs are the most available pair in the molecule, which is why acid catalysis of carbonyl chemistry starts by protonating exactly there.

Two more levers the four letters do not name

Charge. A positively charged acid is always far stronger than its neutral analogue, because losing the proton neutralizes the charge rather than creating one. H₃O⁺ is pKa −1.7 while water is 15.7; NH₄⁺ is 9.2 while NH₃ is 38. Same atom, same hybridization, no resonance and no induction in either pair — seventeen and twenty-nine pKa units purely from where the charge starts and ends. It is the single largest effect in the table, and it is the reason acid catalysis works at all: protonating a substrate is how you manufacture a strong acid out of a weak one.

Solvation, and the sterics that block it. Every pKa in the table was measured in a solvent, and a small, compact anion is easier for water molecules to surround than a bulky one. Methanol has pKa 15.5 and tert-butanol 18 — the alkoxides differ in nothing but bulk, and the bulky one is stabilized less because water cannot get close enough to solvate it. Remove the solvent and the order reverses: in the gas phase tert-butoxide is the more stable anion. Treat solvation as a reminder rather than a fifth factor: the four structural effects explain the anion, and the solvent has the last word on the number.

Running the framework on a base

Every question above asked which acid is stronger. Exams ask the mirror question just as often — which of these is the stronger base — and it needs no new theory, only one translation: the stronger base is the one whose conjugate acid has the higher pKa. Convert, look up, compare, and you are back on familiar ground.

Ethoxide against acetate, for example. Conjugate acids: ethanol 16, acetic acid 4.76. Ethanol is the weaker acid, so ethoxide is the stronger base, by eleven units — which is the same resonance argument as before, read backwards. Or ammonia against water: conjugate acids NH₄⁺ at 9.2 and H₃O⁺ at −1.7, so ammonia is the stronger base by about eleven units, and its lone pair is the one that gets protonated when both are present. The one trap is picking the wrong conjugate acid: the conjugate acid of ammonia is ammonium, not ammonia itself, and confusing a base's own N–H pKa with its pKaH is the single most common way to get these backwards.

Always compare the conjugate bases, not the acids. The question "which is more acidic" is answered by asking "which anion is more stable," so draw both anions before reasoning. Students who try to reason directly about the acids get tangled; those who draw the two conjugate bases side by side usually see the answer immediately.

What carries forward

These four factors are the general theory of anion stability, and anion stability is what sets leaving-group ability, nucleophile strength, enolate formation and carbanion chemistry. You will apply them directly to carboxylic acids (Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives), to alpha hydrogens (Enolate Chemistry), to amine basicity (Amines), and to the substituent effects that control aromatic substitution (Aromatic Chemistry). Very little in the second half of the course is new physics; most of it is this section applied to a new structure.
