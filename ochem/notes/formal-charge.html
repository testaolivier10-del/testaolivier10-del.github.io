The formula

An atom's formal charge compares the electrons it "owns" in a structure to the electrons it would have on its own as a neutral atom.

FC = (valence electrons) − (lone-pair electrons) − (number of bonds)

Every bond — single, double, or triple — counts as one bond here, since you're counting how many bonds surround the atom, not how many electrons are in them.

Worked examples

NH₄⁺: nitrogen has 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs left. FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1, matching the ion's overall charge.

OH⁻: oxygen has 6 valence electrons, 1 bond, and 3 lone pairs (6 nonbonding electrons). FC = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1.

H₃O⁺: oxygen has 6 valence electrons, 3 bonds, 1 lone pair (2 electrons). FC = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1, matching the ion's charge.

Why it matters

When a molecule or ion could be drawn several valid ways, the best Lewis structure is usually the one where formal charges are smallest and negative charge sits on the most electronegative atom. You'll use this to pick the right resonance structure, spot the electrophilic atom in a molecule, and explain why a mechanism attacks where it does.

Perchlorate, ClO₄⁻: chlorine has 7 valence electrons, 4 single bonds, 0 lone pairs. FC = 7 − 0 − 4 = +3 on chlorine, even though the whole ion is −1 overall. That's exactly why formal charge matters — it flags this as an unrealistic structure, and a chemist would look for one that spreads the charge out better.

The built-in check

The sum of every atom's formal charge in a structure must equal the molecule or ion's overall charge — for a neutral molecule, that sum must be exactly 0. If your total doesn't match, recheck your bond and lone-pair counts.
