Amines · Section 96 of 121

Making amines

Practice this — interactive lesson

There is one obvious way to make an amine, and it does not work. Every method here is a way around that single failure.

The problem: alkylation cannot be stopped

Treat ammonia with an alkyl halide and the nitrogen does the SN2 you expect, giving a primary amine. That amine is a better nucleophile than ammonia was, because the new alkyl group donates electron density to nitrogen, so it attacks the next molecule of halide faster than the ammonia still in the flask does — and the secondary amine it produces is faster again. Out comes a mixture of primary, secondary and tertiary amine plus quaternary ammonium salt, in proportions set by relative rates rather than by how much halide you weighed out.

Two honest qualifications. The run is not monotonic all the way: a tertiary amine is a poorer nucleophile than the secondary amine that made it, because three alkyl groups crowd the SN2 transition state, so it is the early steps that outrun ammonia rather than every step outrunning the last. And it is halide stoichiometry that cannot save you — a large excess of ammonia genuinely does bias the mixture toward the primary amine, and is how simple ones are made industrially. What you cannot do is control the outcome by adding less halide.

The reactions section flagged this and named exhaustive alkylation, Gabriel and reductive amination; here those are taken apart and the rest of the toolkit added.

One case welcomes the runaway: if a quaternary ammonium salt is the target, excess CH3I run to completion gives a single product, because there is nowhere further to go.

Gabriel synthesis: alkylate something that can only react once

Phthalimide's N–H sits between two carbonyls, which makes it acidic enough (pKa about 8.3) for KOH to deprotonate. The anion does an SN2, and afterwards the nitrogen has no N–H left and its lone pair is delocalized into both carbonyls — an imide, not an amine, so it cannot alkylate again. Hot aqueous acid or base, or hydrazinolysis with H2NNH2, then releases a clean primary amine.

The catch: the alkylation is still an SN2, so the halide should be primary or methyl and unhindered. Secondary substrates give poorer yields with elimination alongside; tertiary ones give only alkene. One more consequence of its being an SN2: if the carbon carrying the halide is a stereocenter, the nitrogen arrives on the opposite face and the configuration is inverted. Reductive amination, which builds the same bond by adding hydride to a flat C=N, gives a racemic mixture at that carbon instead. Where the stereochemistry matters, that difference alone decides the route.

Azide displacement: use a nucleophile that is not an amine

Azide, N3−, is small and an excellent nucleophile, and its displacement product is an alkyl azide — not an amine and not nucleophilic, so there is nothing to over-alkylate. LiAlH4, or H2 over Pd, then gives the primary amine. Same SN2 limits, fewer steps than Gabriel.

Cyanide, then reduce: the route that adds a carbon

RX + NaCN gives RCN, and LiAlH4 reduces that to RCH2NH2. The nitriles section covers it; the bookkeeping is the point here. The nitrile carbon becomes the CH2, so the amine has one more carbon than the halide did. Gabriel and azide hand back the skeleton you put in; this lengthens it.

Reductive amination: build the C–N bond by reduction instead

An aldehyde or ketone condenses with ammonia or a primary amine to an imine, or with a secondary amine to an iminium ion (the imine chemistry of Carbonyl Chemistry; enamines are put to work in Enolate Chemistry), and that C=N is reduced in the same flask. The escape is structural: the C–N bond comes from hydride adding to a C=N rather than from displacement, so the product amine has no halide left to attack, and the product is set by what you put in.

Two limits are worth naming, because they are not the SN2 ones. The nitrogen ends up on the old carbonyl carbon, so a carbon with no hydrogen on it is unreachable this way — tert-butylamine is not a reductive amination product, and neither is an aryl amine. And making a primary amine from ammonia is the one case that does over-alkylate, because the primary amine produced competes with ammonia for the next carbonyl.

The reagent is the trick. NaBH3CN and NaBH(OAc)3 are attenuated hydride sources: they survive the mildly acidic pH (around 4–5) imine formation wants, and reduce the protonated imine far faster than the carbonyl, so the ketone survives to condense. NaBH4 would simply give the alcohol.

Amide reduction: acylate once, then take the oxygen away

Acylation is self-limiting: the amide nitrogen it produces has its lone pair tied up in the carbonyl and is not nucleophilic, so one acylation gives one product. LiAlH4 then reduces the C=O to CH2, with no carbon lost. It is the cleanest general route to a secondary or tertiary amine: RCOCl + R′NH2 → RCONHR′ → RCH2NHR′.

Nitro reduction: the route to an aromatic amine

Every route so far builds the C–N bond on an sp³ carbon, and the paragraph above said flatly that an aryl amine is out of reach that way. Here is how you reach one: you do not make the C–N bond at all. You install a nitro group on the ring by nitration, then reduce it. H2 over Pd or Pt, or Fe/HCl, or Sn/HCl, or SnCl2 all take ArNO2 to ArNH2. The carbon count does not change, and the nitrogen lands exactly where the nitro group was — which is what makes the pair so useful, because nitration is a reaction whose position can be directed, so it decides where the amine ends up. Nitration and the rules that steer it are covered in Aromatic Chemistry; the reduction itself is an oxidation-level change and nothing more.

Losing a carbon on purpose

The Hofmann rearrangement takes a primary amide with Br2 and NaOH to a primary amine with one fewer carbon: the alkyl group migrates from the carbonyl carbon to nitrogen, giving an isocyanate that hydrolyzes and expels that carbon as CO2. Butanamide, four carbons, gives propylamine, three. The Curtius rearrangement reaches the same isocyanate by heating an acyl azide, made from the acid chloride and NaN3, and loses the same carbon.

The steps of the Hofmann rearrangement are worth having, because the migration is the surprising one. Hydroxide deprotonates the amide N–H; the anion attacks Br2 to give an N-bromoamide; hydroxide removes the second N–H to give an N-bromoamide anion. That anion then does the key step: the alkyl group migrates from the carbonyl carbon to the nitrogen at the same moment bromide leaves, so no nitrene is ever free. What is left is an isocyanate, R–N=C=O. Water adds to it to give a carbamic acid, R–NH–COOH, which loses CO2 spontaneously. The carbon that leaves as CO2 is the original carbonyl carbon, and the migrating group keeps its configuration — a stereocenter that migrates arrives with retention.

Worked example 1 — butylamine from 1-bromopropane

Step 1 — count carbons. Target four, starting material three. The count has to go up by one. That single observation kills Gabriel, azide, amide reduction and reductive amination in one stroke, and it kills Hofmann and Curtius twice over, since they run the other way.

Step 2 — what is left? Only the cyanide route adds exactly one carbon.

Step 3 — check the substrate. Cyanide displacement is an SN2, so the halide has to be primary or methyl. 1-Bromopropane is primary. Fine.

Step 4 — write it. CH3CH2CH2Br + NaCN → CH3CH2CH2C≡N, butanenitrile. Then LiAlH4 and aqueous workup → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2.

Step 5 — check the answer. Four carbons, a primary amine, nitrogen on the end. The nitrile carbon is the one that became the CH2 bearing nitrogen — that is always where the extra carbon ends up.

Worked example 2 — N-methylcyclohexylamine, four attempts, two of which fail

Target: cyclohexyl–NH–CH3, a secondary amine. The carbon count is not constrained here, so the first question is the other one: how substituted is the nitrogen? Secondary — so every route that runs an SN2 on a nitrogen surrogate is out before we start.

Attempt A — Gabriel on cyclohexyl bromide, then methylate. Fails twice over. Gabriel can only ever hand back a primary amine, and cyclohexyl bromide is a secondary halide, so the phthalimide alkylation would give mostly cyclohexene by E2.

Attempt B — cyclohexylamine + CH3I. The right connectivity, the wrong control. The secondary amine product is at least as nucleophilic as the starting material, so it takes a second methyl and a third: a mixture ending at the quaternary salt.

Attempt C — reductive amination. Two ways round, and both work. Cyclohexanone + CH3NH2 + NaBH3CN puts the nitrogen on the old carbonyl carbon, C1 of the ring. Or cyclohexylamine + formaldehyde + NaBH3CN delivers the methyl instead. Either gives cyclohexyl–NH–CH3 cleanly.

Attempt D — acylate, then reduce. Also clean, and the route to reach for when the reductive amination reagents are not to hand — but watch the count. Cyclohexylamine + CH3COCl gives the amide, and LiAlH4 reduces its C=O to CH2, which lands on cyclohexyl–NH–CH2CH3, the ethyl amine, because the acetyl group carried two carbons. To reach N-methyl you must acylate with formic acid or a formate ester, whose single carbon becomes the methyl.

Choose with two questions. Does the carbon count have to change? That cuts most of the table at once. Then: how substituted is the target nitrogen? Every route running an SN2 on a nitrogen surrogate — Gabriel, azide, cyanide — gives only a primary amine. For anything more substituted, reductive amination or amide reduction.
