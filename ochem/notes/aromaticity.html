The four requirements for aromaticity

A ring is aromatic only if it satisfies all four of these at once:

  • Cyclic — it has to be a closed ring.
  • Fully conjugated — every atom in the ring must have a p orbital available, so the pi system runs unbroken all the way around. In practice that means every ring atom is sp² or sp hybridized; a single sp³ atom anywhere in the ring breaks the chain. This is the same p-orbital continuity that mattered for resonance (Resonance lesson).
  • Planar — the ring has to be flat enough for all those p orbitals to actually line up and overlap.
  • 4n+2 pi electrons — Hückel's rule. The ring must hold exactly 2, 6, 10, 14... pi electrons, for some whole number n.

Why 4n+2: molecular orbital reasoning

Where does an odd-looking rule like 4n+2 come from? Take it in three steps.

First, when the ring's p orbitals combine, they produce a set of pi orbitals at fixed energy levels. Those levels come in a specific pattern: one orbital alone at the bottom, and then pairs of orbitals above it. Two orbitals at exactly the same energy are called degenerate — the word just means equal in energy, nothing more.

Second, a degenerate pair is stable only when it is completely full. This is the same Hund's rule from the Orbitals lesson: given two equal-energy orbitals and only two electrons to put in them, the electrons go in singly rather than pairing up, leaving two unpaired electrons — a high-energy, unstable arrangement. Fill the pair right to the top with four electrons and that problem disappears.

Third, count. Filling the bottom orbital takes 2 electrons. Filling each degenerate pair above it takes 4 more. So the counts that leave nothing half-filled are 2, then 2+4=6, then 2+4+4=10, then 14 — which is exactly the list 4n+2 generates. That is the whole content of Hückel's rule: it is the arithmetic of leaving no half-filled shell.

This clean-shell filling is what gives aromatic systems their exceptional extra stability, called resonance energy (or aromatic stabilization energy). For benzene it has been measured experimentally, via heats of hydrogenation — the same technique from the Alkene Structure lesson — at roughly 36 kcal/mol more stable than a hypothetical non-aromatic "cyclohexatriene" would be.

Benzene's real structure isn't three localized double bonds alternating with three single bonds — it's six completely equivalent C–C bonds (each 1.39 Å, exactly between a typical single and double bond length), a direct physical signature of full resonance delocalization around the entire ring, not just between two adjacent positions as in the acetate examples from the Resonance lesson.

Antiaromatic: same requirements, wrong electron count — and actively destabilized

A ring that's cyclic, fully conjugated, and planar, but has exactly 4n pi electrons (4, 8, 12...) is antiaromatic — the molecular orbital filling pattern leaves two electrons unpaired in a degenerate pair of orbitals (an unstable, high-energy, biradical-like arrangement, by Hund's rule). Antiaromatic compounds are so destabilized that many real molecules that would be antiaromatic if perfectly planar (like cyclooctatetraene) instead pucker out of planarity specifically to avoid full conjugation and escape antiaromatic destabilization — sacrificing planarity is worth it to dodge the far worse fate of antiaromaticity.

Nonaromatic: simply missing a requirement

A ring that fails any of the first three requirements is simply nonaromatic — not cyclic, or not fully conjugated (an sp³ carbon anywhere in the ring will do it), or not planar. Nonaromatic means neither specially stabilized nor specially destabilized: the ring just behaves like an ordinary alkene or diene, and Module 7 addition chemistry applies to it normally.

1,3-cyclohexadiene is the standard example. Its two sp³ CH₂ carbons break the ring's conjugation, so it is nonaromatic — even though the two C=C double bonds it does have are perfectly ordinary alkenes.
