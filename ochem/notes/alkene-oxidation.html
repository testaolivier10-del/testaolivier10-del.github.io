Alkenes & Alkynes · Section 48 of 117

Oxidative cleavage & dihydroxylation

Practice this — interactive lesson

The reactions in this section all oxidize a carbon–carbon double bond, and they differ in how far they go. One adds a single oxygen, one adds two hydroxyls, and one cuts the molecule in half. Choosing between them is choosing how much of the alkene you want left.

Epoxidation: one oxygen, ring intact

mCPBA (meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid) delivers a single oxygen atom across the double bond to give an epoxide. The reaction is concerted — the oxygen is transferred in one step through a cyclic transition state — so it is stereospecific: a cis alkene gives the cis-substituted epoxide and a trans alkene gives the trans one, cleanly.

Epoxides are useful precisely because they are strained and therefore reactive, which is the subject of their own section in the alcohols chapter. Here the point is what happens when you open one with water.

The two diols, and how to choose between them

A 1,2-diol (a glycol) can be made from an alkene in two ways, and they give opposite stereochemistry. This is the most examinable fact in the section.

  • OsO₄ (often with NMO as a co-oxidant to make it catalytic) adds both oxygens to the same face through a cyclic osmate ester. The result is a syn (cis) diol. Cold, dilute, basic KMnO₄ does the same job more cheaply and less cleanly.
  • mCPBA, then aqueous acid makes the epoxide first and then opens it. The nucleophile attacks the protonated epoxide from the face opposite the C–O bond being broken, so the two oxygens end up on opposite faces: an anti (trans) diol.
OsO₄ → syn diol. Epoxidation then hydrolysis → anti diol. Same starting alkene, same molecular formula in the product, different diastereomer. If a question specifies which diol it wants, it is testing exactly this pair.

Which diastereomer, exactly — and is it chiral?

"Syn" and "anti" name the relative arrangement of the two new OH groups. To turn that into an actual answer you need the alkene's geometry too, because the product is fixed by both together. Work the four combinations once, on but-2-ene, and you never have to work them again.

Notice the pattern rather than memorizing four cells: the two rows are swapped. Changing either the alkene geometry or the reagent flips the answer; changing both flips it back. And notice the second column of the top row: cis-but-2-ene plus OsO₄ gives a single achiral compound, because the two stereocenters it creates are mirror images of each other through the middle of the molecule — the meso case from Stereochemistry. The racemic entries are the reverse: two stereocenters that are not internally compensated, formed as an exactly 50:50 pair because the reagent can approach either face of a flat alkene.

The general rule behind the table: a stereospecific reaction on an achiral starting material can control relative stereochemistry but not absolute. OsO₄ can guarantee the two OH groups are on one face; it cannot guarantee which face, so wherever the product is chiral it comes out racemic. Getting a single enantiomer needs a chiral reagent — which is what the Sharpless dihydroxylation is for, and why that work won a Nobel Prize.

Ozonolysis: cutting the double bond in half

Ozone (O₃) cleaves a C=C completely, breaking both the σ and the π bond and leaving two separate carbonyl fragments. The workup then decides what those fragments are:

It is worth knowing the shape of how, because it explains why there is a workup step at all. Ozone adds across the alkene in one step to give an unstable molozonide; that falls apart and reassembles into the slightly less unstable ozonide, in which the two former alkene carbons are held apart by a bridge of oxygens. The ozonide is the species in the flask when the ozone is switched off — and it is neither of the carbonyls yet. The workup reagent is what takes it apart, and that is why the workup, not the ozone, decides the answer.

  • Reductive workup — dimethyl sulfide (Me₂S) or zinc in acetic acid. Stops at the carbonyls: a carbon that bore two substituents becomes a ketone, and one that bore a hydrogen becomes an aldehyde.
  • Oxidative workup — hydrogen peroxide. Anything that would have been an aldehyde is oxidized on to a carboxylic acid. Ketones, having no hydrogen to lose, are unaffected.
The terminal-alkene trap. A =CH₂ end has two hydrogens on it, so it is the fragment that keeps climbing. With Me₂S it gives formaldehyde, HCHO — easy to overlook in a product list, but it is there. With H₂O₂ it goes to formic acid and on to CO₂, which bubbles out of the flask and is not isolated at all. So oct-1-ene plus O₃ then H₂O₂ gives heptanoic acid and nothing else you can weigh. Working a structure problem backwards, this is the one fragment that can be invisible: if the fragments you are given do not add up to the molecular formula, a lost CH₂ unit is the first thing to suspect.

Hot, concentrated, acidic KMnO₄ does the same cleavage with the oxidative outcome, and is sometimes written in place of ozonolysis with peroxide workup.

Ozonolysis is a structure-determination tool as much as a synthesis one. Run it on an unknown alkene, identify the two carbonyl fragments, and mentally rejoin them at their carbonyl carbons — that reconstructs where the double bond was. Questions that give you the fragments and ask for the starting alkene are asking you to run exactly that reasoning backwards. One case breaks the "two fragments" habit: if the double bond is in a ring, cutting it does not make two molecules, because the two ends are still joined through the rest of the ring. Cyclohexene plus O₃ then Me₂S gives a single molecule, hexanedial, OHC–(CH₂)₄–CHO. Seeing one product with two carbonyl groups is the signature of a cyclic alkene, and seeing one product with one carbonyl group at twice the expected amount is the signature of a symmetric acyclic one.
Worked example — reading a cleavage backwards

An unknown alkene C₆H₁₂ is ozonolyzed with Me₂S workup, giving propanal and propanone (acetone).

Rejoin at the carbonyl carbons. Propanal is CH₃CH₂CHO, so that carbon carried one hydrogen and an ethyl group. Propanone is (CH₃)₂CO, so that carbon carried two methyls.

Rebuild the double bond between those two carbons: CH₃CH₂–CH=C(CH₃)₂, which is 2-methylpent-2-ene. Check the formula: C₆H₁₂. ✓

The tell in the question is that one fragment is an aldehyde and one a ketone, which immediately says the original alkene was trisubstituted — one carbon bore a hydrogen, the other did not.

Worked example — one alkene, six reagents

Substrate: 2-methylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CH–CH₃. Before touching a reagent, label the two carbons: the left one carries two methyls and no hydrogen; the right one carries one methyl and one hydrogen. Every answer below is read off that labeling.

(a) mCPBA. One oxygen across the double bond, ring intact: the epoxide, 2,2,3-trimethyloxirane. Nothing is cleaved and no OH appears.

(b) OsO₄, then NaHSO₃. Two OH on the same face: 2-methylbutane-2,3-diol, the syn diol. Only one stereocenter is created here (C3; C2 carries two methyls and is not a stereocenter), so in this particular substrate "syn" has no visible consequence — the product is simply racemic. Pick a substrate with two stereocenters if you want to see syn and anti differ.

(c) mCPBA, then H₃O⁺. The same diol constitution, reached by backside opening of the epoxide: the anti diol. Same formula, and here the same compound as (b) for the reason just given.

(d) O₃, then Me₂S. Cut the bond and cap each end with an oxygen. The two-methyl carbon has no hydrogen, so it can only be a ketone: propanone. The other carbon has a hydrogen, so it is an aldehyde: ethanal. Products: propanone + ethanal.

(e) O₃, then H₂O₂. Same cut, oxidative workup. The ketone is unchanged — no hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon to lose. The aldehyde climbs one more rung. Products: propanone + ethanoic acid.

(f) KMnO₄, hot and concentrated. Same outcome as (e): propanone + ethanoic acid. Cold, dilute and basic instead, and you would get the syn diol from (b).

The method, in two questions: does the reagent cut the bond or add across it, and if it cuts, does each carbon have a hydrogen? Everything in the summary table is those two questions answered.

KMnO₄ appears twice on purpose, and the conditions are doing all the work: cold and dilute it stops at the diol, hot and concentrated it cleaves right through. A question that specifies KMnO₄ without conditions is incomplete, and one that specifies them is telling you which row to read.

What carries forward

Two threads. The syn/anti diol pair is a stereochemical choice you make by reagent, which is the same kind of control as Lindlar against dissolving metal in the last section — and both are the sort of thing a synthesis question is built around. And ozonolysis run backwards is a structure-determination technique, which puts it alongside the spectroscopy chapter rather than only alongside the reactions: given fragments, find the parent.
