Aromatic Follow-Through · Section 102 of 121

Diazonium salts and Sandmeyer

Practice this — interactive lesson

Electrophilic aromatic substitution can put a handful of groups on a ring: a halogen, a nitro group, a sulfonic acid, an alkyl or acyl group. It cannot put on an OH, a CN or an F; it manages I only with an added oxidant and awkwardly; and it cannot remove a group once it is there.

Diazonium chemistry does all of those, through one intermediate that is worth the whole section: an aryl diazonium salt, Ar–N2+.

Three steps to the salt, all of them already yours

Nothing in getting to Ar–N2+ is new. Nitrate the ring with HNO3/H2SO4, which is ordinary electrophilic aromatic substitution and which decides, through the directing rules, where the nitrogen will end up. Reduce the nitro group with Fe/HCl, Sn/HCl or H2 over Pd, as in Amines. Then diazotize the resulting primary aromatic amine with NaNO2/HCl at 0–5 °C, whose nitrosonium mechanism and whose cold you also have from that chapter.

Two consequences of those three steps run through everything below. The diazonium group can sit only where the nitration put it, so the whole synthesis is decided by the directing effects of the first step. And the salt is made cold and used immediately — but several of the reactions that use it are then deliberately heated, which is a distinction worth keeping straight.

Why N2+ is such a good leaving group

You met this claim in Amines; here is what it buys. It leaves as nitrogen gas. The product is a molecule of N2 — one of the most stable molecules there is, and a gas that bubbles out of the solution and never comes back.

The reason an aryl diazonium salt can be kept at all is the same fact that makes it interesting: the cation it would leave behind is an aryl cation, which is so bad that the salt sits there in the cold instead of decomposing. An alkyl diazonium salt would give a perfectly ordinary carbocation and is gone before you can use it — which is why this chemistry is aromatic only.

That is the entire trick of the section. An aryl halide will not ionize because an aryl cation is unstable, and a diazonium salt goes anyway — the leaving group is good enough to make even that worthwhile. It converts a ring position into something that can become almost anything.

Four of those are groups electrophilic substitution cannot deliver at all: CN, OH, F and I. The last row is the strangest and the most useful.

The copper is doing real work rather than decorating the arrow. Cu(I) hands the diazonium an electron, N2 leaves, and an aryl radical picks its halide or cyanide off the resulting Cu(II) — a chain, not an ionization. The uncatalyzed rows, KI and warm aqueous acid, do go through the aryl cation, which is why they need no metal and why they are the slower, hotter ones.

Note the acid in the hydrolysis row. Warm the salt in basic water and the phenol you have just made is deprotonated to phenoxide, which promptly couples with the diazonium salt still in the flask — you get an azo dye rather than a phenol. Same two reagents, two outcomes, decided by pH.

One thing this table does not say. The reagent goes on the diazonium salt, not on the amine — you cannot skip the diazotization and write “aniline + CuCN”. Write the salt out as its own intermediate every time, and the two rules above — nitration fixes the position, and the cold belongs to making the salt rather than to using it — stay visible while you plan.

Removing a group is a synthetic tool

Replacing N2+ with H deletes the whole substituent. That sounds pointless until you remember what an amino group does while it is there: NH2 is a powerful ortho/para director, so it can be installed purely to steer the next substitution, and then removed.

Note that an amine cannot serve as a blocker: it is far too strong a director to occupy a position quietly. Blocking para so that substitution is forced ortho is the sulfonic acid trick, and it works for one reason only: sulfonation is reversible, so the group can take the para position, physically hold it while the real electrophile goes ortho, and then be removed with hot dilute acid. (A sulfonic acid is a strong deactivator and a meta director in its own right, which is beside the point — it is there to occupy a chair, not to vote.)

Worked example — 1,3,5-tribromobenzene from benzene

Why not just brominate three times? Bromine is an ortho/para director. The second bromine goes ortho or para to the first, the third does the same, and the 1,3,5 pattern is unreachable — you would end up at 1,2,4.

Step 1. HNO3/H2SO4. Nitrobenzene.

Step 2. Fe/HCl, then NaOH. Aniline. You have installed the strongest ortho/para director available.

Step 3. Br2 in water, no catalyst. 2,4,6-tribromoaniline. Aniline is so activated that all three positions react at once, without a Lewis acid, and the product precipitates. The “too reactive to control” behavior that was a nuisance for a phenol two sections ago is exactly what is wanted here.

Step 4. NaNO2/HCl, 0–5 °C. The diazonium salt. Keep it cold.

Step 5. H3PO2. The nitrogen is replaced by H, leaving 1,3,5-tribromobenzene.

The move to generalize: the NH2 group was never part of the target. It was installed because it directs, used, and then deleted. When a substitution pattern looks forbidden, ask which temporary director would have made it allowed.

Azo coupling, and why dyes are colored

A diazonium salt is also a weak electrophile. It is not strong enough to attack benzene, but a strongly activated ring — a phenol or an aniline — will react with it, giving an azo compound, Ar–N=N–Ar′.

The pH is not optional. A phenol is coupled in mildly basic solution, pH 8–10, because it is the phenoxide and not the phenol that is nucleophilic enough — but go much past pH 10 and the diazonium ion itself is converted to an unreactive diazotate. An aniline is coupled mildly acidic, pH 4–7: enough acid to keep things in solution, not enough to protonate the amine, since an anilinium ion has no lone pair to donate and deactivates its own ring. Coupling goes para to the activating group; if para is blocked it goes ortho, and if both are blocked it does not go at all.

The product has two rings joined by a conjugated N=N bridge, so its π system is long, its HOMO–LUMO gap is small, and it absorbs visible light. Azo compounds are intensely colored, and they were the foundation of the synthetic dye industry — methyl orange is one.

What carries forward

Nitrate, reduce, diazotize cold, then substitute. The diazonium group leaves as N2, which makes it the best leaving group on a ring and the route to OH, CN, F and I. Installing an amine to direct and then deleting it is the aromatic protecting-group move, and coupling a diazonium onto an activated ring makes a dye. When a target's substitution pattern looks impossible by directing effects alone, this is the chapter to check.
