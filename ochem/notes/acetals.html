Carbonyl Chemistry · Section 66 of 121

Acetals & hemiacetals

Practice this — interactive lesson

Mix acetone with methanol and add a trace of acid. Two methanol molecules add to the carbonyl carbon, one after the other, and one molecule of water comes off. The product, 2,2-dimethoxypropane, has two O–CH3 groups on the old carbonyl carbon and no C=O at all.

That product is an acetal: a carbon bonded to two OR groups, where R is any carbon group, with no carbonyl left. Halfway there, after the first methanol has added, the carbon carries one OH and one OCH3. A carbon with one OH and one OR is a hemiacetal. “Hemi” means half.

Every step of the reaction is reversible. That turns it into a switch: it can hide a carbonyl, and later bring the same carbonyl back. The switch is why this reaction matters so much in practice.

Two older names. A hemiacetal made from a ketone was traditionally called a hemiketal, and the full two-OR product from a ketone a ketal. Exams and older texts still use these words. Current IUPAC usage calls both cases hemiacetal and acetal, whatever the carbonyl was, and so does this course. The chemistry is identical either way.

Part one: the hemiacetal (steps 1–3)

The mechanism has seven steps. Each one either moves a proton on or off an oxygen, or makes or breaks one bond to the central carbon. The figures split the seven into three parts. Steps 1–3 are the acid-catalyzed hydration from the previous section, with methanol in place of water.

Step 1. A lone pair on the carbonyl oxygen takes H+ from the acid catalyst. The oxygen now carries a positive charge, and it pulls electrons out of the C=O bond. The carbon becomes much more electron-poor, so it is a much stronger electrophile. Putting H+ on an oxygen to activate its neighbor is the same move that turned an alcohol’s OH into a leaving group in Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry.

Step 2. A lone pair on methanol’s oxygen forms a bond to the carbon. At the same moment, the C=O π bond moves onto the positive oxygen, which becomes a neutral OH. This is ordinary nucleophilic addition. It needs two arrows: without the second one, the carbon would end up with five bonds.

Step 3. The oxygen that came from methanol now has three bonds and a positive charge. Another methanol molecule takes its H+, and the electrons of the O–H bond stay on the oxygen. The product is the hemiacetal: one carbon bearing both an OH and an OR.

Hemiacetals from simple aldehydes and ketones are usually too unstable to isolate. The equilibrium sits back toward the carbonyl. There is one enormously important exception, taken up at the end of this section.

Part two: water leaves and an oxocarbenium ion forms (steps 4–5)

Step 4. The acid protonates the hemiacetal again, this time on its OH. The OH becomes OH2+, which can leave as a neutral water molecule. The OH could not leave on its own, because it would have to go as hydroxide, a strong base.

The OCH3 oxygen can take a proton too. If it does, methanol leaves instead of water, and that just runs steps 1–3 backward to the protonated ketone. Only protonation of the OH moves the reaction forward.

Step 5. The C–O bond to the OH2+ breaks, and both of its electrons leave with the water. At the same time, a lone pair on the OCH3 oxygen moves in to form a C=O π bond. The product is an oxocarbenium ion: a carbon double-bonded to a positively charged oxygen.

Losing a leaving group from carbon usually makes a carbocation, which is high in energy. Here the neighboring oxygen rescues it. Put the charge on carbon, and you get the left-hand structure below, in which the carbon has only six electrons. Move one oxygen lone pair into the C–O bond, and you get the right-hand structure, in which the charge sits on oxygen and every atom has a full octet. The real ion is a blend of the two, a resonance hybrid, with the positive charge shared between carbon and oxygen.

Orbitals show why the lone pair can move. The cation’s carbon is sp2 and flat, with an empty p orbital. The oxygen beside it holds one lone pair in a p orbital parallel to that empty one. The two overlap side on, so the pair spreads over both atoms: that shared pair is the C=O π bond.

An oxocarbenium ion is far more stable than an ordinary alkyl carbocation. It follows the delocalization-beats-localization principle from Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons. That extra stability is what lets an ionization that would otherwise be prohibitive run under mild acid catalysis.

Part three: a second alcohol completes the acetal (steps 6–7)

Step 6. A second methanol attacks the carbon of the oxocarbenium ion. The carbon is flat, so methanol can reach it from either face. The arrows are the same pair as in step 2: the lone pair forms the new C–O bond, and the C=O π bond moves back onto the positive oxygen.

Step 7. The newly added oxygen loses H+ to a methanol molecule. That gives the acetal: a carbon bonded to two OR groups, with no OH remaining and no carbonyl.

Count the protons. One goes on in step 1 and comes off in step 3. One goes on in step 4 and comes off in step 7. The acid ends as it began, so it is a catalyst. A drawn mechanism that does not balance this way is missing a step.

Every step is reversible. Start from the acetal, add water and an acid catalyst, and the same seven steps run backward to the original carbonyl and two molecules of alcohol. Nothing in the mechanism favors one direction; only the conditions do.

Driving it either way

Because the whole thing is an equilibrium, you steer it with Le Châtelier’s principle rather than with different reagents: remove a product, and the equilibrium moves to replace it.

To form the acetal, use excess alcohol and remove the water as it is produced. A Dean–Stark trap does this: water boils off with the solvent, condenses, and collects in a side arm, where it cannot run back into the flask. Molecular sieves, porous solids that soak up water, do the same job.

To hydrolyze the acetal back, use excess water with dilute aqueous acid.

In practice, a diol is usually used instead of two separate alcohol molecules. Take cyclohexanone and ethylene glycol, HOCH2CH2OH, which carries both alcohol groups on one molecule. Both of its oxygens end up on the old carbonyl carbon. They close a five-membered ring of O, CH2, CH2 and O, called a 1,3-dioxolane.

The cyclic acetal forms faster and hydrolyzes more slowly than one made from two separate alcohols. Once the first oxygen has added, the second is already tied on nearby. Forming the second C–O bond is then intramolecular, and it costs little entropy, the measure of lost freedom of motion. A carbonyl protected as its 1,3-dioxolane is the standard version of this operation.

Worked example — using a protecting group

The starting material is ethyl 4-oxocyclohexanecarboxylate: a cyclohexane ring with a ketone at one end and an ester at the other. An ester is a C=O whose carbon also carries an OR group; it has its own chapter, Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives. The goal is to reduce only the ester, to a CH2OH. LiAlH4 would reduce both groups. NaBH4 would reduce only the ketone, which is the wrong one.

Protect: ethylene glycol with catalytic TsOH (p-toluenesulfonic acid, a strong acid that dissolves in organic solvents), with the water removed. The ketone becomes a cyclic acetal. The ester is untouched.

React: LiAlH4, then a water workup. LiAlH4 is a strong hydride reducing agent, taught in Oxidation & Reduction and used here only for what it does to an ester. The ester becomes a primary alcohol, CH2OH. The acetal is completely inert to hydride.

Deprotect: dilute aqueous H3O+. The acetal hydrolyzes back to the ketone.

Net result: the ester is reduced and the ketone comes back untouched. It takes three steps to make one change that no single reagent could.

Why acetals work as protecting groups

An acetal is stable to base, to nucleophiles, to hydride reducing agents and to oxidizing agents. It is stable to Grignard reagents too, the carbon nucleophiles (R–MgBr) of Organometallics. It reacts only under aqueous acid, and even then reversibly.

The reason is structural. There is no carbonyl to attack and no acidic proton to remove. There is also no leaving group that a base or a nucleophile could push out. Without a proton on it, an OR group would have to leave as an alkoxide, RO−, and an alkoxide does not leave.

Protonation is what turns that OR into a neutral alcohol, which can leave, and protonation needs acid. So an acetal behaves like an ether, with all of an ether’s inertness, plus a built-in way to come apart when you want it to.

A good protecting group needs three things. It goes on in high yield. It survives the chemistry you intend to do. It comes off under conditions the rest of the molecule tolerates. Acetals do all three for carbonyls, which is why they are the default choice.

The other selectivity question: which carbonyl gets protected. Acetal formation is itself a nucleophilic addition, so it follows the reactivity order this chapter has built since its first section. Give a molecule holding both an aldehyde and a ketone one equivalent of diol, and the aldehyde is protected. It is the more reactive carbonyl, for the same steric and electronic reasons as always. Students often protect the group they want to keep instead of the one that is in the way. Decide which carbonyl you intend to react first, and check that the protection masks the other one.
Check that the deprotection conditions are survivable. Removing an acetal needs aqueous acid. So an acetal cannot protect a carbonyl in a molecule that has something else acid-sensitive, such as a tert-butyl ether or a tertiary alcohol that could dehydrate. Choosing a protecting group is always a question about the whole molecule, not just the group being protected.

The exception that runs your metabolism

Take 5-hydroxypentanal. It has an OH five carbons along from its aldehyde group. The chain can curl round until that OH oxygen reaches its own C=O. The attack closes a six-membered ring that contains one oxygen, and the product is a cyclic hemiacetal. At equilibrium, most of the compound is in this ring form.

The general rule: when the alcohol and the carbonyl are in the same molecule and can form a five- or six-membered ring, the intramolecular reaction is so favorable that the cyclic hemiacetal dominates.

That is what a sugar is. Glucose in solution exists over 99% as its six-membered cyclic hemiacetal, not as the open-chain aldehyde.

Ring closure creates a new stereocenter at the former carbonyl carbon, called the anomeric carbon (C1 in glucose). Its OH can point to either face of the ring. The two configurations, α and β, are the anomers from Stereochemistry. In a Haworth drawing of D-glucose, which shows the ring as a flat hexagon seen nearly edge-on, the α anomer has its C1 OH pointing down and the β anomer has it pointing up.

Cap the anomeric OH with a second alcohol, and the hemiacetal becomes a full acetal. In sugar chemistry, that acetal is called a glycoside. Linking two sugars through such a bond gives a disaccharide, and linking thousands gives starch or cellulose. Starch joins its glucose units through the α anomer and cellulose through the β anomer, and that one difference is why people can digest starch but not cellulose.

What carries forward

Protecting-group strategy is essential to any multi-step synthesis, and it appears throughout the rest of the course, gathered in Synthesis & Retrosynthesis. The oxocarbenium ion reappears wherever an oxygen stabilizes an adjacent positive charge. And the cyclic hemiacetal is the structural basis of all of carbohydrate chemistry, in Biomolecules, which is where this material leads if you continue into biochemistry.
