Step one: hemiacetal formation

Under acid catalysis, the carbonyl oxygen is protonated first, making the carbonyl carbon an even stronger electrophile (the same activation strategy as protonating an alcohol's OH, Module 8). An alcohol then attacks as the nucleophile — ordinary nucleophilic addition, exactly as in the previous lesson — forming a tetrahedral intermediate that, after losing the proton from oxygen, is called a hemiacetal: a carbon bonded to both an OH and an OR group.

Step two: loss of water forms an oxocarbenium ion

Under continued acid catalysis, the hemiacetal's OH is protonated and leaves as water (exactly like alcohol dehydration, Module 8) — but the resulting carbocation is special: it's directly stabilized by resonance donation from the adjacent oxygen's lone pair, forming an oxocarbenium ion (a resonance hybrid between a carbocation and an oxygen-stabilized C=O⁺ form). This resonance stabilization — the same delocalization-beats-a-localized-charge idea from the Resonance lesson, Module 2 — is why this normally-unfavorable ionization proceeds readily under mild acid catalysis.

The oxocarbenium ion is dramatically more stable than an ordinary alkyl carbocation because the adjacent oxygen lone pair can fully delocalize into the empty p orbital — the positive charge is really shared between carbon and oxygen, not localized on carbon alone.

Step three: a second alcohol completes the acetal

A second equivalent of alcohol then attacks the oxocarbenium ion (nucleophilic addition to this cation, not to a neutral carbonyl), and losing the final proton gives the acetal: a carbon bonded to two OR groups (no OH remaining). Every step in this sequence is reversible — running the whole process in reverse (acetal + water + acid catalyst) regenerates the original carbonyl plus two equivalents of alcohol.

Why acetals matter: protecting groups

A carbonyl is reactive toward nucleophiles (Nucleophilic Addition lesson), strong reducing agents (LiAlH₄), Grignards, and many other reagents you'll want to use elsewhere in a molecule during a multi-step synthesis. Converting it to an acetal temporarily removes that reactivity entirely — acetals are stable to base, to nucleophiles, and to reducing agents, reacting only under aqueous acid. This lets a chemist "protect" a carbonyl, perform unrelated chemistry elsewhere in the molecule, and then "deprotect" (hydrolyze the acetal back to the carbonyl) with dilute aqueous acid once that other chemistry is done — an essential practical strategy anywhere a molecule has more than one reactive functional group.
