Three arrows, one step

E2 is concerted, like SN2 — but instead of two arrows it takes three, all firing at the same instant.

Arrow 1: a base removes a hydrogen on the carbon next to the leaving group (the beta carbon). Tail on the base's lone pair, head on that beta-hydrogen.

Arrow 2: the electrons from that C–H bond become a new π bond between the beta and alpha carbons. Tail on the beta carbon, head on the alpha carbon.

Arrow 3: the C–leaving-group bond breaks and the leaving group departs with both electrons. Tail on the alpha carbon, head on the leaving group.

No one of these happens before the others. All three are one motion.

Why geometry matters: anti-periplanar

This only works when the beta-hydrogen being removed and the leaving group are lined up anti-periplanar — on opposite sides, 180° apart. That specific alignment lets the electrons flow smoothly from the breaking C–H bond into the new π bond as the leaving group departs; any other angle doesn't line the orbitals up correctly. This is exactly why E2 depends so heavily on conformation (which dihedral angles are accessible) — exactly the anti-periplanar requirement worked out in the Conformational Analysis lesson, Module 4.

Worked example: ethoxide (a base) removes a beta-hydrogen from a bromoalkane while the C–Br bond breaks, forming but-2-ene + ethanol + bromide, all in one concerted step.

Zaitsev vs. Hofmann: which hydrogen leaves

When a carbon has more than one available beta-hydrogen, the choice of which one gets removed decides the product. A small base (like ethoxide or hydroxide) can reach a crowded, more-substituted hydrogen, giving the more substituted alkene — the Zaitsev (major) product, generally the more stable alkene. A bulky base (like tert-butoxide) can't easily reach a crowded hydrogen, so it grabs a more accessible, less hindered one instead, favoring the less substituted "Hofmann" alkene.

Rate law, and E1 as the elimination analog of SN1

E2 is bimolecular: rate = k[base][substrate] (same shape as SN2). E1 is the unimolecular elimination — it shares SN1's ionize-first step (leaving group departs to form a carbocation), then a base removes a beta-hydrogen to form the alkene, giving rate = k[substrate] with no base concentration (same shape as SN1). SN1/E1 (ionize first) vs. SN2/E2 (concerted) is the key organizing pair for this whole module.
