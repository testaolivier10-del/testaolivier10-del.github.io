Functional group interconversion

Practice this — interactive lesson

Say a route has built the carbon chain of butan-1-ol, but the target is butanoic acid. The skeleton is already right, and only the group on the end carbon has to change. Changing one functional group into another like this is a functional group interconversion. The reactions in Making carbon–carbon bonds build the skeleton; interconversions do most of the rest.

Each interconversion is usually easy on its own. Routes still go wrong here, because there are so many of them. Learning them as one map rather than a long list keeps them straight, and the map is the oxidation ladder from Oxidation & Reduction.

Two kinds of move

Start with ethanol. Oxidize it to ethanal, and the carbon that carries the OH trades a C–H bond for a second bond to oxygen. That is an oxidation, so it needs an oxidant such as PCC. Turn ethanol into bromoethane instead, and the same carbon trades its bond to oxygen for a bond to bromine. Its bonds to hydrogen stay as they were, so nothing is oxidized or reduced, and PBr₃ does the job with no redox reagent at all.

Every interconversion is one of those two kinds:

  • Up or down the ladder. The carbon gains bonds to O, N or halogen and loses bonds to H, or the reverse. The rungs run alkane, alcohol, aldehyde or ketone, carboxylic acid. These moves need an oxidizing or a reducing agent.
  • Sideways along a rung. One bond to O, N or halogen is traded for another, as in alcohol to alkyl halide, or acid to ester to amide. These moves are substitutions, and they need no redox reagent.

Naming the kind of move tells you which class of reagent to reach for before you recall a single reagent name. It also catches the two commonest planning errors: trying to reach a carboxylic acid from an alcohol by a substitution, and trying to turn an ester into an amide with a reducing agent.

Where the other groups sit on the ladder

The ladder was built from four groups: alkane, alcohol, aldehyde and carboxylic acid. Every other group sits on one of those rungs, and the same count places it. Count the bonds from the carbon to O, N or halogen, with a double bond counting as two and a triple bond as three. One bond puts a group on the alcohol rung, two on the aldehyde and ketone rung, and three on the acid rung.

The alcohol rung. An alkyl halide, an ether and an amine each have one bond from the carbon to a halogen, an oxygen or a nitrogen. So each sits beside the alcohol, and interconverting them is substitution.

The alkene belongs on the alcohol rung too. It has no bond to O, N or halogen, so place it by what adding water does to it. Adding water to ethene gives ethanol. One carbon gains a C–H bond and the other gains a C–O bond, so the change is neither an oxidation nor a reduction, as Oxidation levels in organic chemistry showed for hydration. Removing water reverses it. So alcohol, alkyl halide and alkene form a triangle, and you can move between any two of them without an oxidant or a reductant.

The aldehyde and ketone rung. An acetal carbon has two single bonds to oxygen. An imine carbon has a double bond to nitrogen, which counts as two. An alkyne belongs here for the same reason an alkene belongs on the alcohol rung: hydrating propyne gives acetone (propan-2-one), and no oxidant is involved.

The carboxylic acid rung. An ester, an amide, an acyl chloride and an anhydride each have three bonds from the carbonyl carbon to O, N or Cl. A nitrile carbon has a triple bond to nitrogen, which also counts as three. So nitrile → carboxylic acid is a hydrolysis and needs no oxidant. A primary amine, RCH₂NH₂, has only one bond from that carbon to nitrogen and sits on the alcohol rung. So nitrile → primary amine needs LiAlH₄ and drops two rungs.

Reagent pairs with opposite outcomes

Hydrogenate but-2-yne over Lindlar's catalyst and you get cis-but-2-ene. Reduce it with sodium in liquid ammonia and you get trans-but-2-ene. The starting material is the same; only the reagent differs, and it decides the product.

Read the tables for these pairs rather than row by row. Four come up again and again: cis against trans alkene, syn against anti diol, aldehyde against carboxylic acid, and Markovnikov against anti-Markovnikov alcohol. Those pairs are where synthesis questions are built, because only you can make those choices.

The fourth pair, Markovnikov against anti-Markovnikov, is drawn in the worked example at the end of this page.

Which reductant spares which group

Suppose a molecule carries both a ketone and an ester, and only the ketone should be reduced. NaBH₄ does exactly that. When a molecule has more than one group that can be reduced, the three reagents in this table, all from Reducing carbonyls, decide the outcome.

Knowing that ranking tells you whether a group elsewhere in the molecule will survive a step. When it will not, the group has to be shielded first, which is the subject of Protecting groups.

Going around rather than through

Some conversions have no direct reagent, or none that gives the product you want. They take two steps instead. Four of these detours come up often.

Putting the alkene where you want it

2-Methylbutan-2-ol can lose water in two directions. Heat it with concentrated H₂SO₄, and the reaction goes through a carbocation and gives mostly 2-methylbut-2-ene. That is the more substituted alkene, the one with more carbons attached to its double-bond carbons, and it is the product Zaitsev's rule predicts. A carbocation can also rearrange before it loses a proton, which is a second reason this route gives you little control.

To get the other alkene, make the alkyl halide first. HBr turns the alcohol into 2-bromo-2-methylbutane. An E2 elimination with a bulky base such as tert-butoxide, t-BuO⁻, then removes a hydrogen from one of the exposed CH₃ groups rather than from the more crowded CH₂, and gives mostly the less substituted alkene, 2-methylbut-1-ene.

A small base such as ethoxide would give mostly the Zaitsev alkene again. So it is the base you choose for the E2 step, not the alcohol, that decides where the double bond goes.

An anti-Markovnikov alkyl halide

For a bromide there is a one-step answer. HBr with a trace of peroxide adds to propene by a radical pathway and puts the Br on the end carbon, giving 1-bromopropane. Markovnikov / anti-Markovnikov explains why.

That peroxide effect works only with HBr. HCl and HI add the Markovnikov way whether a peroxide is present or not, so an anti-Markovnikov chloride or iodide takes two steps. Hydroboration–oxidation of propene gives the anti-Markovnikov alcohol, propan-1-ol, and then SOCl₂ turns it into 1-chloropropane.

An iodide comes from the same alcohol with phosphorus and iodine. The two make PI₃ in the flask, and PI₃ replaces the OH just as PBr₃ does.

The two-step route also suits a bromide whose C–Br carbon is a stereocenter with a configuration you need. The radical addition is not a reliable way to set that configuration. Hydroboration, by contrast, adds H and B to the same face of the double bond, and PBr₃ then replaces the OH with inversion, so the outcome of each step is predictable.

An aldehyde from an ester

Treat methyl butanoate with LiAlH₄ and you get butan-1-ol, not butanal. The aldehyde does form on the way, but it is more electrophilic than the ester it came from, so it takes a second hydride as soon as it appears.

There are two ways round this. One is DIBAL-H (diisobutylaluminum hydride), a milder hydride reagent: one equivalent at −78 °C stops at the aldehyde, and both the single equivalent and the low temperature are essential. The other is to reduce all the way to butan-1-ol with LiAlH₄ and then oxidize back up to butanal with PCC. The second route takes two steps and uses only reagents from the tables.

Moving a group along a chain

Sometimes a target is the starting material with its functional group on the neighboring carbon. Eliminate to the alkene, then add back with the opposite regiochemistry, which means that the group goes onto the other carbon of the double bond. This is the standard way to shift an OH from one carbon to the next, and it is almost always the intended answer when a target differs from its starting material only in where the group sits.

Worked example — the group is on the wrong carbon

Target: propan-1-ol, from propan-2-ol.

Both compounds have three carbons and one OH, and both sit on the alcohol rung. Only the position of the OH differs, so the route needs no C–C chemistry and no redox reagent. The move is to eliminate, then add water back the other way round.

1. Conc. H₂SO₄, heat → propene.
2. BH₃, then H₂O₂/HO⁻ → propan-1-ol. Hydroboration is anti-Markovnikov, so it puts the OH on the less substituted carbon, C1.

Acid-catalyzed hydration in step 2 would put the OH straight back on C2.

What carries forward

Almost nothing on this page is new chemistry. Nearly every row is a reaction from an earlier chapter, sorted so you can search it. What is new is a habit. Faced with a gap between two structures, ask whether the step moves up or down the ladder or sideways along a rung, and whether one reagent can make it or it needs two steps. Planning a multistep route puts that habit to work.
