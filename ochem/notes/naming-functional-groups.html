IUPAC Nomenclature · Section 20 of 117

Functional group priority

Practice this — interactive lesson

A molecule with one functional group is easy to name. A molecule with three has to decide which of them the name is about, and that decision is the single most important rule in nomenclature, because it sets the suffix, it can change which chain is the parent, and it overrides the numbering rule you just learned.

One group gets the suffix. Everything else becomes a prefix.

Exactly one functional group in a name is the principal characteristic group. It is named as the suffix at the end of the name, and it is chosen by a fixed priority order — not by which one you think is more interesting, and not by which one is drawn first. Every other functional group in the molecule is demoted and cited as a prefix, alongside the alkyls and halogens from the previous section.

This is why the same group has two names depending on company it keeps. An –OH is the suffix -ol when it is the highest-priority group present, and the prefix hydroxy- when something outranks it. Nothing about the oxygen changed; its rank did.

The priority order

Highest first. The top of the list is the carboxylic acid and its derivatives — the groups you get by swapping the acid's –OH for something else.

carboxylic acid > ester > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine > alkene / alkyne > alkane
Below the line, and never a suffix under any circumstances: ethers, halides, nitro groups.

Learning it as a list of ten is harder than learning it in three blocks. The acid derivatives come first, in the order acid, ester, amide, nitrile. Then the carbonyls that are not derivatives, aldehyde before ketone. Then the ones that are just a heteroatom carrying a hydrogen: alcohol, then amine. Multiple bonds rank below all of them, and the three groups on the second line cannot be suffixes at all.

Two more acid derivatives slot in when they turn up. An anhydride (two acyl groups sharing one oxygen) ranks between the acid and the ester, and an acyl chloride (RCOCl — the acid with its –OH swapped for Cl) between the ester and the amide. Each is named as a suffix in its own right — ethanoic anhydride, ethanoyl chloride, better known as acetic anhydride and acetyl chloride — and neither is ever likely to be demoted, so most textbook lists leave them out. Know where they go.

It is worth being clear about what this order is and is not: it is not an oxidation ranking. Count the bonds from the functional carbon to oxygen or nitrogen. An acid, an ester, an amide and a nitrile carbon each have three, so those four sit at the same oxidation level and the sequence within the block is a convention. An aldehyde carbon and a ketone carbon each have two, so that pair is a convention as well — the aldehyde's carbon carries a hydrogen where the ketone's carries a carbon, which is why only the aldehyde can be oxidized further, but the level is the same. (Oxidation levels are made precise in Oxidation & Reduction; this is a preview.) What makes the convention useful is that within the derivative block it runs in the same direction as reactivity: an acyl chloride or an acid converts to an ester, an ester to an amide, and neither step runs backwards directly — an ester or an amide is taken back to the acid by hydrolysis first.

An ester's name is two words, and which fragment is which trips people up. In ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, the ethyl is the carbon chain on the single-bonded oxygen — the alcohol's — and the ethanoate is the acid's chain, counted from the C=O. Find the C=O, count its chain for the second word, and whatever hangs off the other oxygen is the first. An amine's substituents on nitrogen take the locant N (N-methylpropan-1-amine); that convention is developed in Amines.

When the suffix begins with a consonant the parent's final e is kept, and when it begins with a vowel the e is dropped: propane becomes propanol, not propaneol, but it becomes propanenitrile with the e intact. This is spelling rather than chemistry, and it is worth getting right because it is visible.

Two of the same principal group take a multiplying prefix on the suffix — -diol, -dione, -dioic acid — and because di begins with a consonant the parent keeps its e: butane-1,4-diol, pentane-2,4-dione, hexanedioic acid. Writing butan-1,4-diol is the misspelling this rule exists to catch, and it is common because butan-1-ol is right.

The principal group takes the lowest locant, and it outranks everything

In the previous section the numbering was chosen to give the substituents the lowest locants, because an alkane has nothing else to number. Once a principal characteristic group is present, that rule is demoted. The order is now:

  • First, the principal characteristic group gets the lowest possible number. This decides the direction on its own, whenever it can.
  • Then double and triple bonds.
  • Then the substituents cited as prefixes, by first point of difference.
  • Then, as a last tie-break, alphabetical order.

So a six-carbon chain with an OH on C2 counting from the right and a methyl on C2 counting from the left is 5-methylhexan-2-ol, not 2-methylhexan-5-ol. The alcohol wins the low number and the methyl takes whatever is left. Giving the lowest locant to the substituent out of habit is one of the most common errors on this material.

There is a second consequence that is easy to miss: the parent chain must contain the principal characteristic group. If the longest chain in the molecule does not pass through the carbon bearing the –OH, then the longest chain is not the parent — you take the longest chain that does contain it, even if that chain is shorter. Priority beats length.

For a carboxylic acid, an aldehyde, a nitrile or an ester on a chain, the functional carbon is necessarily at the end of that chain, so it is always C1 and the locant is not written. Ethanoic acid needs no number; butanal needs no number. Writing "butan-1-al" is not wrong so much as redundant, and it reads as though you have not noticed why.

That is for chains. When the same group hangs off a ring, its carbon is bonded to a ring carbon and cannot be part of the ring, so the ring is the parent and the group's own carbon is not counted in it: the suffix becomes -carboxylic acid or -carbaldehyde, as in cyclohexanecarboxylic acid and cyclohexanecarbaldehyde, and the ring carbon bearing the group is C1. A ketone or an alcohol on a ring needs no such device, because its carbon is a ring carbon: cyclohexanone, cyclohexanol, with C1 at the group and no locant written when it is the only thing there.

Worked example — three groups, one name

A five-carbon chain carries a carboxylic acid at one end, a ketone at C3 and a bromine at C4.

Rank the groups. Carboxylic acid outranks ketone, which outranks nothing here; bromine is a prefix always. So the acid is the principal characteristic group and takes the suffix -oic acid. The ketone is demoted to oxo-.

Number. The acid carbon is C1 by necessity, which fixes the direction with nothing left to decide.

Assemble, alphabetically. bromo before oxo: 4-bromo-3-oxopentanoic acid.

Notice that a molecule most chemists would describe out loud as "a keto acid" contains no mention of a ketone in its systematic name. The word oxo is where it went.

Reading a name back into a structure

Half of every nomenclature exam runs the procedure the other way — "draw 3-hydroxybutanal" — and the mistake that direction invites is reading the name left to right, drawing the prefix first and then guessing where the suffix goes. Read it from the end instead: the suffix says which fragment sits at C1 (or at the locant it carries), the root says how long the chain around it is, and each prefix then lands on its number.

Worked example — draw 3-hydroxybutanal

Suffix first. -al: an aldehyde. Its carbon is at the end of the chain, so C1 is a CHO. Draw that carbon with its double-bonded oxygen and its hydrogen.

Then the root. butan: four carbons in the parent, counting the CHO carbon. Draw three more in a zigzag from it, and number them 2, 3, 4 away from the aldehyde.

Then each prefix at its locant. 3-hydroxy: an –OH on C3. C3 is the third carbon from the CHO, not from the far end — the numbering was fixed the moment the suffix claimed C1.

The result is CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO, and the figure draws it. Read left to right instead and the OH goes down first, on whichever carbon you happened to start with, and the aldehyde lands wherever is left — which is how 4-hydroxybutanal, a different compound, gets drawn in its place.

Acetone is the one worth pausing on. It is a ketone and it is the principal characteristic group of its own molecule, yet its name contains no suffix at all — a reminder that a retained name is a word, not a derivation, and cannot be taken apart the way a systematic name can.

What carries forward

This priority order is not only a naming rule. It is very close to a reactivity order for the acid derivatives at the top of it, and the same ranking reappears in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives as the ladder that says an acyl chloride converts to an ester and not, directly, the reverse. When you meet it there, it will already be familiar — you will have been using it to decide suffixes for eight chapters.
