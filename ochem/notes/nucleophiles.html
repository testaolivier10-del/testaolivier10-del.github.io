How Reactions Happen · Section 37 of 121

Nucleophiles

Practice this — interactive lesson

Mix hydroxide ion with bromomethane, CH₃Br. One of the oxygen's lone pairs reaches the carbon and becomes a new C–O bond. At the same moment the C–Br bond breaks, and bromide leaves with both of its electrons. The product is methanol.

Hydroxide brought the electron pair for the new bond, so hydroxide is the nucleophile. The carbon that received the pair is the electrophile, the subject of Electrophiles. Bromide, the group that left with a pair, is the leaving group (Leaving groups). This one-step swap is the SN2 reaction, which Substitution & Elimination covers in full.

Nearly every reaction in this course pairs a species that brings electrons with a species that receives them, and this page is about the first of the two. Once you can look at a flask of reagents and say which one is the nucleophile, you know where the tail of your first curved arrow goes.

What it is

A nucleophile is any species with an electron pair it can donate to form a new bond. The name means "nucleus-loving": the pair is drawn to an electron-poor atom, one with a full or partial positive charge. In the language of Lewis acids/bases, a nucleophile is a Lewis base. Chemists use the word nucleophile when the pair goes to an atom other than a proton, most often carbon.

The pair can come from three places, and each one gives a family of nucleophiles.

  • Anions. Hydroxide (HO⁻), alkoxides (RO⁻), cyanide (⁻CN), thiolates (RS⁻), halides, and hydride donors, which are reagents that deliver H⁻, a hydrogen carrying both bonding electrons. The most reactive anions in the course are carbanions, carbons that carry a lone pair and a full negative charge. Carbon is the least electronegative atom in this list, so it holds a negative charge worst and gives its pair up most readily.
  • Neutral molecules with a lone pair. Water, alcohols, ammonia, amines and phosphines.
  • π bonds. Alkenes, alkynes and aromatic rings. They are weak nucleophiles, but they are nucleophiles, and they supply all the reactivity of Alkenes & Alkynes and Aromatic Chemistry.

Beginners tend to miss the π family, because a π bond has no charge and no lone pair. Its electrons sit above and below the plane of the molecule, as the π-bond figures in Bonding show, and there an electron-poor atom can reach them.

What makes one stronger

Nucleophilicity, or nucleophile strength, is how fast a species uses its pair to form a new bond. Six factors control it. Four do most of the work: charge, electronegativity, polarizability, and crowding around the donating atom, called sterics. Two matter in closer comparisons: delocalization and hybridization. The solvent can change the outcome on top of all six, so it gets its own section below.

Charge. Let methoxide, CH₃O⁻, and methanol, CH₃OH, each attack bromomethane. Methoxide reacts far faster. The rule is that a species with a negative charge is a stronger nucleophile than its neutral partner: HO⁻ beats H₂O, RO⁻ beats ROH, and H₂N⁻ beats NH₃.

Here is why. When methanol loses its O–H proton, the pair that held the hydrogen stays on oxygen as a third lone pair. The oxygen keeps the same nuclear charge but now carries a full negative charge, so it holds its pairs less tightly, and they sit higher in energy. Less energy is then needed to start the new bond, so the reaction runs faster. Energy diagrams, later in this chapter, draws that energy as a hill the reaction climbs.

This is why chemists so often remove the proton from an alcohol before using it. Methanol is a middling nucleophile, and sodium methoxide, NaOMe, is a strong one, which is why NaOMe appears in so many reaction conditions. The rule compares a species with its own neutral partner. Between different atoms or different structures, the other factors below can outweigh charge.

Electronegativity. Compare the amide ion, H₂N⁻, with hydroxide, HO⁻. Each carries one negative charge, and nitrogen and oxygen sit side by side in the same row. Oxygen is the more electronegative atom (3.44 against 3.04), so it holds its lone pairs more tightly and shares them less readily. The amide ion is the stronger nucleophile.

Across the whole row the order is H₃C⁻ > H₂N⁻ > HO⁻ > F⁻: within a row, nucleophilicity falls as electronegativity rises. The values come from Electronegativity. The solvent does not change this order. The rule compares atoms of similar size, though. Down a column the size changes a lot, and a different factor comes in.

Polarizability. Iodide, I⁻, and fluoride, F⁻, sit at opposite ends of one column. In water or methanol, iodide is by far the better nucleophile, and the column runs I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻. Part of the reason is that iodide is polarizable.

An atom is polarizable when its electron cloud is easy to push out of shape. Iodine is a large atom that holds its outer electrons loosely and far from the nucleus, so its cloud deforms easily. As iodide nears a δ+ carbon, its cloud stretches toward the carbon and starts to bond before the atoms are close. Fluorine is small and holds its electrons tightly and close in, so its cloud barely changes. Big and loose means polarizable; small and tight means not.

Sulfur and oxygen follow the same trend. Sulfur is the larger, more polarizable atom, so a thiolate, RS⁻, is a better nucleophile than an alkoxide, RO⁻. Polarizability comes back in leaving-group ability, and it already appeared in the acidity trend down a group (Factors affecting acidity).

The rest of iodide's advantage comes from the solvent. In a solvent that cannot form hydrogen bonds, the halide order reverses, so polarizability alone does not settle it. The solvent section below shows why.

Sterics. A nucleophile has to physically reach the atom it attacks, so bulky groups near the donating atom slow it down, sometimes drastically. tert-Butoxide, (CH₃)₃CO⁻, attacks through the same O⁻ as methoxide, but its three methyl groups keep it away from a crowded carbon. Crowding is also what separates nucleophilicity from basicity, so the last section of this page draws this case.

Delocalization. Compare acetate, CH₃COO⁻, with methoxide, CH₃O⁻. Acetate spreads its charge over two oxygens by resonance, so neither oxygen carries all of it. Methoxide keeps the whole charge on its one oxygen. Acetate is a mediocre nucleophile, and methoxide is a strong one.

The rule is that an anion whose charge is spread by resonance is a weaker nucleophile than one whose charge sits on a single atom. With less electron density at any one site, it has less to donate, and for the same reason it is also a weaker base. Resonance already gave you the idea underneath: whatever stabilizes an anion also makes it less eager to attack.

Hybridization of the donating pair. Trimethylamine, pyridine and acetonitrile all donate from a nitrogen lone pair. In trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N, the nitrogen is sp³ and the pair has 25% s character. In pyridine, a benzene-like ring with one nitrogen in it, the ring nitrogen is sp² and its pair has 33% s character. In acetonitrile, CH₃C≡N, the nitrogen is sp and its pair has 50% s character.

More s character holds a pair closer to the nucleus, so the pair is less available. Trimethylamine is the best nucleophile of the three, and acetonitrile is the weakest. Factors affecting acidity used the same s-character argument for anions. Hybridization is a smaller effect than charge or sterics, but it explains several comparisons that otherwise look arbitrary.

Solvent matters too, and it can reverse the order

A protic solvent has an O–H or N–H bond, so it can donate hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen bond is the attraction between a hydrogen on O or N and a lone pair or negative charge nearby. Water, methanol, ethanol and acetic acid are protic. An aprotic solvent has no O–H or N–H bond. Acetone, DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide, (CH₃)₂S=O), DMF (dimethylformamide), acetonitrile and THF (tetrahydrofuran) are aprotic. Both kinds can be highly polar; the words only say whether an O–H or N–H is there to form hydrogen bonds.

Dissolve sodium fluoride in methanol. Each fluoride ion ends up inside a tight shell of methanol molecules, each pointing its O–H hydrogen at the ion. Fluoride is small and its charge is packed into a small volume, so methanol holds it firmly. Stripping that shell away costs more energy than the reaction gains, and fluoride's nucleophilicity collapses. Iodide is large, its charge is spread out, and methanol holds it only loosely. So in methanol or water the halide order is I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻, the exact reverse of the basicity order.

In a polar aprotic solvent such as DMSO or DMF, there is no O–H to cage anything. These solvents surround cations well, through their oxygen atoms, and anions poorly, so the anion is left bare and much more reactive. The halide order flips back to the basicity order, F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻, and every anion is a stronger nucleophile than it was in methanol, often by several orders of magnitude.

There is no single nucleophilicity ranking. Any list of nucleophile strengths holds for one kind of solvent. If an exam question names a solvent, that is the information you need to answer it, not scene-setting. If it names none, it is asking about the row trend, which solvent does not change, or it is incomplete.
Worked example — the same reaction in acetone and in methanol

Sodium iodide reacts with 1-bromobutane: iodide replaces bromide, the same kind of swap as hydroxide and bromomethane at the top of this page. Chemists run it in acetone, a polar aprotic solvent. In methanol the same reaction is slower.

Nothing about the substrate changed. In methanol, iodide is surrounded by hydrogen-bonded solvent that must be stripped away before it can attack. In acetone there is nothing to strip. Iodide actually gains the least from the switch, because methanol holds it loosely to begin with; a small anion such as chloride gains far more. In SN2 chemistry the solvent works like a reagent, not a background detail.

Worked example — ranking four nucleophiles toward CH₃Br in methanol

Rank CH₃S⁻, CH₃O⁻, CH₃OH and CH₃COO⁻. Apply the factors in order, and stop as soon as one of them separates a pair.

Charge first. CH₃OH is the only neutral species, and its own anion, CH₃O⁻, is in the set. Methanol goes last. It is weaker than all three anions, which each bring a full negative charge.

Delocalization next, among the three anions. CH₃COO⁻ spreads its charge over two oxygens, while CH₃O⁻ and CH₃S⁻ each keep it on one atom. Acetate is the weakest of the three anions and goes third.

Then the column, between the two localized anions. CH₃S⁻ and CH₃O⁻ differ only in the donating atom, and sulfur sits below oxygen. Sulfur is larger and more polarizable, and methanol cages it far less tightly. The thiolate wins.

Sterics does not come into it, because each species has a single methyl group and none of the donating atoms is crowded. Naming the factor you did not need is part of the answer.

Final order: CH₃S⁻ > CH₃O⁻ > CH₃COO⁻ > CH₃OH. This is not the basicity order, since methoxide is a much stronger base than the thiolate. The last section of this page is about that difference.

When a nucleophile has two ends: ambident nucleophiles

Cyanide, ⁻C≡N, has a lone pair on carbon and another on nitrogen, so it could attack through either atom. A nucleophile with two atoms that can attack is called ambident. With bromomethane, cyanide attacks through carbon and gives acetonitrile, CH₃C≡N.

Cyanide almost always attacks through carbon. You might expect nitrogen, the more electronegative atom, to be the one that attacks. But because carbon is less electronegative, its lone pair is the more available and the more polarizable of the two, and the C–C bond that forms is the stronger bond. So cyanide adds a carbon, not a nitrogen. That makes it one of the few reagents in an introductory course that lengthens a carbon chain.

An enolate is the anion left when a base removes a hydrogen from the carbon next to a C=O; the figure draws the enolate of acetone. Enolates raise the same question more sharply. One resonance form puts the negative charge on oxygen, and the other puts it on carbon. The form with the charge on oxygen contributes more, yet the enolate usually reacts at carbon. Which end reacts depends on the electrophile and the conditions, and Enolate Chemistry takes that up properly.

For now, the useful habit is to notice when a nucleophile has more than one atom that could attack, and to ask which one does, rather than assuming the charge marks the spot.

Nucleophilicity is not basicity

Hydroxide and tert-butoxide show the difference. tert-Butoxide is the stronger base: its conjugate acid, tert-butanol, is a weaker acid than water. Yet toward a crowded carbon it is a far weaker nucleophile than hydroxide.

The two ideas often rise and fall together, which is why they are easy to confuse, but they measure different things. Basicity is thermodynamic. It describes where a proton-transfer equilibrium settles, and pKa measures it (Acids & Bases). Nucleophilicity is kinetic. It describes how fast a species attacks an electron-poor atom, usually carbon. One is about where an equilibrium lies, and the other is about how fast a reaction goes.

Crowding pulls the two apart. A proton is tiny and sits on the outside of a molecule, so tert-butoxide can reach it past its own methyl groups. A carbon that carries other groups is buried among them, and the bulky tert-butyl group cannot get close enough to it. Chemists use this on purpose. When Substitution & Elimination wants elimination (removing an H and a leaving group from neighboring carbons to make a C=C) rather than substitution, it picks a bulky base for exactly this reason.

Polarizability pulls them apart in the other direction. Iodide is an excellent nucleophile and a very weak base. A thiolate, RS⁻, is a stronger nucleophile than an alkoxide, RO⁻, and at the same time a much weaker base; Lewis acids/bases flagged this pair under hard and soft. Any reagent that is a good nucleophile but a weak base tends to favor substitution over elimination.

What carries forward

The split between a strong nucleophile that is a weak base and a strong base that is bulky decides SN2 against E2 in Substitution & Elimination, and it is one of the most heavily tested judgments in the course. The nucleophile decides the product in nucleophilic addition (Carbonyl Chemistry) and in acyl substitution (Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives). And every alkene and aromatic reaction you will see starts from the fact that a π bond is a nucleophile.
