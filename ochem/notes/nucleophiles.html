What it is

A nucleophile ("nucleus-loving") is any species with an available electron pair — a lone pair, a negative charge, or a π bond — that it can donate to form a new bond. Nucleophiles are drawn toward electron-poor (electrophilic) centers. Common ones: anions (OH⁻, CN⁻, RS⁻), neutral molecules with lone pairs (H₂O, NH₃), and π bonds (alkenes).

What makes one stronger

More available electron density (like a full negative charge) means a stronger nucleophile. A more electronegative atom actually holds its electrons more tightly, making it a weaker nucleophile, not stronger — counterintuitive, but electronegativity and nucleophilicity pull in opposite directions.

Two words worth pinning down: protic, and polarizable

A protic solvent has an O–H or N–H bond, so it can hand out hydrogen bonds — water, methanol, ethanol. An aprotic solvent has no such bond and cannot — acetone, DMSO, DMF. Both kinds can be polar; the difference is only whether there is an O–H or N–H available for hydrogen bonding.

Polarizable describes how easily an atom's electron cloud gets squashed out of shape. A big atom like iodine holds its outermost electrons loosely and far from the nucleus, so that cloud distorts easily — it can reach out toward an electrophile before the atoms are even close, and it can spread a negative charge over a large volume. A small atom like fluorine holds its electrons tightly and close in, so its cloud barely budges. Big and loose means polarizable; small and tight means not. This one property comes back for leaving groups and for acidity down a group.

Solvent matters too

In polar protic solvents (water, methanol), small, highly charged anions like F⁻ get "caged" by hydrogen bonds, blunting their nucleophilicity. Large, polarizable anions like I⁻ aren't held back the same way — so I⁻ beats F⁻ as a nucleophile in these solvents, even though F⁻ is more electronegative.

Every mechanism starts with finding the nucleophile — it's where the arrow's tail will start. Deprotonating CH₃OH (pulling the H⁺ off its O–H) to CH₃O⁻ makes it a substantially stronger nucleophile by giving it a full negative charge to donate.

Nucleophilicity ≠ basicity

Basicity is thermodynamic (equilibrium proton affinity, exact once you cover pKa); nucleophilicity is kinetic (speed of attack on carbon). They usually track together, but steric hindrance splits them: tert-butoxide is a stronger base than hydroxide but a much weaker nucleophile, since its bulky methyl groups block it from a crowded carbon.
