Spectroscopy · Section 84 of 117

IR

Practice this — interactive lesson

Every structure in this course has so far been asserted. This chapter is about how anyone knows. Four techniques, used together, determine a molecular structure from a sample: IR finds the functional groups, mass spectrometry gives the molecular weight and formula, and the two NMR experiments map the carbon skeleton. IR is the fastest of the four and usually the first one you look at.

What IR measures

Every covalent bond behaves approximately like a spring connecting two masses, stretching and compressing at a specific natural frequency. Infrared light carries just the right energy to excite those vibrations, and a molecule absorbs IR at the frequencies matching its bonds' natural frequencies.

An IR spectrum plots absorption against frequency, expressed as wavenumber in cm⁻¹ — a direct map of which bonds are present. One caveat worth knowing: a vibration is only IR-active if it changes the molecule's dipole moment, which is why the perfectly symmetric C=C of trans-2-butene shows no absorption at all.

Two variables set the frequency

Following the same physics as a real spring, a stronger bond vibrates at a higher frequency — a stiffer spring oscillates faster — and lighter atoms vibrate at a higher frequency, since there is less mass to move.

That explains the whole layout of the spectrum. C≡C (stronger) absorbs near 2150 cm⁻¹, C=C near 1650, C–C near 1000. And any bond to hydrogen appears at very high wavenumber — 2800–3600 cm⁻¹ — because hydrogen is so light.

This makes IR a direct physical confirmation of the bond-order and hybridization ideas from Foundations and Alkenes & Alkynes. A C≡C really is stronger than a C=C, and the spectrum measures it. The same logic runs in reverse to explain fine detail: an amide's C=O absorbs lower than a ketone's precisely because nitrogen's resonance donation weakens that bond — the bond-strength rule predicting a spectroscopic observation from a structural argument you already had.

Two shapes are worth internalizing. An alcohol's O–H is broad because hydrogen bonding creates a spread of slightly different environments; a carboxylic acid's is broader still, sprawling across 2500–3300 and often swallowing the C–H peaks. An N–H, which hydrogen-bonds less, is narrower — and a primary amine shows two N–H peaks where a secondary shows one, which distinguishes them at a glance.

The 3000 cm⁻¹ line is a useful marker: C–H absorptions just below it are sp³, and any appearing just above it indicate sp² C–H, meaning an alkene or an aromatic ring. One band sits above even that: a terminal alkyne’s ≡C–H at 3300, sharp and strong where an O–H at the same wavenumber would be broad. That is what separates a nitrile from a terminal alkyne when both show a peak near 2200 — the alkyne has the 3300 band, the nitrile has nothing there.

The carbonyl region in detail

The C=O stretch is the most useful single signal in IR — strong, sharp, and in a region where almost nothing else absorbs. Its exact position then identifies which kind of carbonyl.

Carbonylcm⁻¹
acid chloride1800
anhydride1760 and 1820 (two peaks)
ester1735
aldehyde1725
ketone1715
carboxylic acid1710
amide1650–1690

That ordering is the reactivity ladder of Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives, read as a frequency. Stronger resonance donation into the carbonyl weakens the C=O, lowering its frequency — and weaker donation leaves a stronger bond that absorbs higher. The acid chloride at 1800 and the amide at 1660 sit at opposite ends of both scales for the same reason. Conjugation lowers any of these by roughly 30 cm⁻¹, since the extended pi system delocalizes the C=O. Ring size moves it the other way: a carbonyl inside a small ring absorbs higher, because squeezing the C–C–C angle down forces more s character into the carbon’s bonds to its neighbors and into the C=O with them, and more s character means a shorter, stiffer, faster-vibrating bond. Cyclohexanone is an ordinary 1715; cyclopentanone is 1745; cyclobutanone is 1785. Same carbonyl every time, and the number is reporting ring strain.

Worked example — identifying C₃H₆O from IR alone

Three isomers to distinguish: acetone, propanal, and allyl alcohol.

Strong peak at 1715, no O–H: a ketone. Acetone.

Strong peak at 1725, plus two weak peaks at 2720 and 2820: an aldehyde — that pair of weak C–H stretches is diagnostic for an aldehyde and appears nowhere else. Propanal.

Broad peak at 3350, plus C=C at 1650 and C–H above 3000, and no carbonyl: allyl alcohol.

Three isomers separated in seconds, without any other technique. This is what IR is for.

Worked example — running the four questions on a real peak list

Data: C₈H₈O, and an IR with peaks at 3060 (medium), 2980 (medium), 1685 (very strong, sharp), 1600 and 1580 (medium, several), 1450 (medium), 760 and 690 (strong).

Question 1 — anything at 1650–1800? Yes: 1685, very strong and sharp. There is a carbonyl. But 1685 is below the 1715 of a plain ketone, and the only entry in the table that sits that low is an amide — and there is no N–H, and the formula has no nitrogen. So this is a conjugated carbonyl: conjugation drops any C=O by roughly 30 cm⁻¹, and 1715 − 30 = 1685.

Question 2 — anything broad above 3200? No. No O–H and no N–H, so not an acid, not an alcohol, not an amide. The formula's single oxygen is already spent on the C=O, so it is fully accounted for.

Question 3 — anything just above 3000, or the 1450–1600 cluster? Both: 3060 is sp² C–H, and 1600/1580/1450 is the aromatic C=C cluster. There is a benzene ring. The 2980 peak, just below 3000, says there is also at least one sp³ C–H.

Question 4 — anything sharp near 2250? No. No nitrile, no alkyne.

Assemble. DoU = (2×8 + 2 − 8)/2 = 5: four for the ring, one for the C=O. A benzene ring, a carbonyl conjugated to it, an sp³ C–H, and C₈H₈O in total leaves exactly CH₃ over. That is C₆H₅–CO–CH₃, acetophenone. The strong pair at 760 and 690 is the one part of the fingerprint region worth reading: a monosubstituted ring gives two strong bands near 750 and 690, so even the substitution pattern is confirmed.

Note what did the work: not one peak, but the carbonyl position — 1685 rather than 1715 — agreeing with the aromatic evidence. Two independent readings of the same spectrum pointing at the same conjugation.

The fingerprint region

Below about 1500 cm⁻¹ the spectrum becomes a dense tangle of overlapping single-bond stretches and bending vibrations — the fingerprint region. It is too complex to assign peak by peak, but it is unique to each specific compound, so it is definitive for confirming a match against a reference spectrum.

Treat the two halves differently: above 1500, read functional groups; below 1500, compare against a known. Trying to interpret the fingerprint region from first principles is a waste of effort.

IR tells you what groups are present, not how they are connected. A carbonyl peak says there is a C=O somewhere; it cannot tell you where in the skeleton. That is what NMR is for, and the two are used together — which is why a structure-determination problem hands you several spectra at once rather than one.

How to read a spectrum in thirty seconds

A reliable order. Is there a strong peak at 1650–1800? If so there is a carbonyl, and its exact position says which kind. Is there a broad peak above 3200? An O–H or N–H, with breadth distinguishing them. Is there anything just above 3000, or peaks in the 1450–1600 cluster? Unsaturation, probably aromatic. Anything sharp near 2250? A nitrile.

Four questions, and you have the functional groups. Then hand the skeleton problem to NMR.

And read the empty regions as hard as the peaks. A spectrum with no absorption between 1650 and 1800 rules out every carbonyl compound at once — aldehyde, ketone, ester, acid, amide, acid chloride and anhydride together — which on a multiple-choice question usually eliminates most of the options in one glance. The same goes for a clean baseline above 3200: no alcohol, no acid, no amine, no amide. An absence is often the stronger statement, because a small peak can be an impurity while a missing band cannot.

What carries forward

IR is the first step in every structure-determination problem in this chapter, and it pairs naturally with the degrees-of-unsaturation calculation from Alkenes & Alkynes — DoU tells you how many rings and pi bonds to look for, and IR tells you what kinds they are.
