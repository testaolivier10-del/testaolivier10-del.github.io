What IR actually measures: bond vibration frequency

Every covalent bond behaves approximately like a tiny spring connecting two masses, constantly stretching and compressing at a specific natural frequency. Infrared light has just the right amount of energy to excite these vibrations, and a molecule absorbs IR light specifically at the frequencies matching its bonds' natural vibration frequencies. An IR spectrum plots the amount of light absorbed (or transmitted) versus frequency (measured as wavenumber, in cm⁻¹) — a direct map of exactly which bonds are present and how they're vibrating.

Two variables set the frequency: bond strength and atom mass

Following the same physics as a real spring (Hooke's law), a stronger bond vibrates at a higher frequency (a stiffer spring oscillates faster), and lighter atoms vibrate at a higher frequency (less mass to move, for the same restoring force). This is why a C≡C triple bond (stronger, from the Alkynes lesson) absorbs at higher wavenumber (~2150 cm⁻¹) than a C=C double bond (~1650 cm⁻¹), which in turn absorbs higher than a C–C single bond (~1000 cm⁻¹) — and why an O–H or N–H stretch (very light hydrogen) shows up at very high wavenumber (~3200-3550 cm⁻¹) compared to a C–H stretch on a heavier framework nearby.

This bond-strength relationship means IR spectroscopy is a direct, physical confirmation of the same bond-order and hybridization ideas from Modules 1, 4, and 7 — a C≡C really is a stronger bond than a C=C, and the IR spectrum proves it quantitatively.

Key diagnostic absorptions worth recognizing on sight

O–H (alcohol): broad, strong, ~3200-3550 cm⁻¹ (the breadth comes from hydrogen bonding creating a range of slightly different O–H environments). N–H (amine/amide): sharper and weaker than O–H, ~3300-3500 cm⁻¹. C=O (carbonyl): strong, sharp, ~1650-1750 cm⁻¹ — one of the most useful and reliable signals in the whole spectrum, since almost nothing else absorbs as strongly in that exact range. The precise carbonyl position further distinguishes carbonyl type: ester ~1735 cm⁻¹, ketone/aldehyde ~1715 cm⁻¹, carboxylic acid ~1710 cm⁻¹ (broadened by its own O–H, which also shows a very broad ~2500-3300 cm⁻¹ absorption), amide lower still (~1650-1690 cm⁻¹, since nitrogen's resonance donation into the carbonyl — Amine Structure lesson — weakens the C=O bond itself, precisely predicting a lower vibration frequency, exactly as the bond-strength rule requires).

The fingerprint region

Below about 1500 cm⁻¹, the spectrum becomes a dense, hard-to-interpret tangle of overlapping single-bond stretches and bending vibrations — the fingerprint region. While too complex to assign bond-by-bond, this region is unique to each specific compound (like an actual fingerprint), so it's extremely useful for confirming an exact match against a known reference spectrum, even when it can't be interpreted from first principles alone.
