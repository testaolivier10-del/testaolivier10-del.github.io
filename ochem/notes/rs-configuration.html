The four-step algorithm

1. Rank the four groups on the stereocenter by CIP priority (below). 2. Orient the molecule so the lowest-priority group (usually the last-place group, often H) points directly away from you, into the page. 3. Trace a path from priority 1 → 2 → 3 among the remaining three groups. 4. If that path goes clockwise, the center is R (Latin rectus, "right"); if counterclockwise, it's S (sinister, "left").

CIP priority rules

Compare the atoms directly attached to the stereocenter first: higher atomic number wins (this is why the periodic table matters here — you're literally reading off atomic number). If two attached atoms tie (e.g., both carbon), look at what those atoms are attached to, and compare the sets of atoms one step further out — highest-priority atom in that next set wins the tie. Keep moving outward, atom by atom, until a difference appears.

A double or triple bond is treated as if it were duplicated into two or three single bonds to a phantom copy of the same atom. A C=O oxygen is therefore counted as if the carbon were bonded to two separate oxygens for priority purposes — this is why a carbonyl carbon almost always ranks very high in CIP priority comparisons.

The "lowest priority away from you" flip trick

If you can't physically rotate the molecule so the lowest-priority group points away (common when working from a flat drawing), trace the 1→2→3 path as drawn anyway, then flip your answer (R becomes S, S becomes R) — because you were viewing it from the wrong side. This flip-the-answer trick is a completely reliable shortcut and saves you from needing to redraw the whole structure.

Worked example: bromochlorofluoromethane, CHFClBr

Attached atoms: Br, Cl, F, H. By atomic number: Br (35) > Cl (17) > F (9) > H (1) — so priority order is Br(1) > Cl(2) > F(3) > H(4), no ties to break. Point H away from you and trace Br → Cl → F: if that's clockwise, the center is R; if counterclockwise, S.

Worked example: butan-2-ol, H on a hashed bond

Priorities: OH first (O beats C outright). The two carbons tie, so go one bond out — the ethyl carbon carries (C,H,H), the methyl carbon only (H,H,H) — so CH₂CH₃ is second, CH₃ third, H last. In the lesson's drawing, OH sits at the top, CH₂CH₃ lower left, CH₃ lower right, and H on a hashed bond pointing down and away. Tracing OH → CH₂CH₃ → CH₃ runs counterclockwise, and because H already points away there is no flip: that center is S.

Worked example: glyceraldehyde, H on a wedge

Priorities: OH first; then the carbon tie breaks in favour of CHO, because its carbon carries (O,O,H) once the double bond is duplicated into a phantom oxygen, against CH₂OH's (O,H,H); H is last. Drawn with OH at the top, CHO lower left, CH₂OH lower right, the 1 → 2 → 3 path again runs counterclockwise — but here H is on a bold wedge, pointing at you, so you are reading the center from the wrong side and the answer flips to R.

Those two examples trace the same direction on the page and have opposite configurations. The only difference is which bond the H sits on. This is the single most common way to lose marks on a stereochemistry question: reading the rotation correctly and then forgetting to check which way the lowest-priority group points.
