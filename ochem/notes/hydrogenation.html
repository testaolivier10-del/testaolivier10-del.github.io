Catalytic hydrogenation

Practice this — interactive lesson

Stir cyclohexene with a little powdered palladium under hydrogen gas, and it takes up one molecule of H₂ and becomes cyclohexane. One hydrogen atom has joined each carbon of the old C=C. This is catalytic hydrogenation: adding H₂ across a multiple bond with the help of a metal.

Why a metal, and not a reagent in solution? Reducing a carbon usually means handing it a hydride, H⁻. A hydride is a nucleophile, and so is an alkene's pi bond, so an isolated C=C has nothing electrophilic for the hydride to attack. The hydride reagents that do reduce C=O are taught later, in Oxidation & Reduction. A carbon–carbon multiple bond needs a different route: bring the alkene and the hydrogen together on a metal surface and let them combine there.

The basic reaction

Three metals do the job. Palladium on carbon (Pd/C) is palladium dusted onto powdered charcoal. Platinum is used as the metal or as its oxide, PtO₂. Raney nickel is a spongy, finely divided nickel. With any of them, H₂ adds two hydrogens across a C=C and the alkene becomes an alkane.

The metal is a catalyst: it speeds the reaction up and comes out unchanged. Without it, hydrogen gas and an alkene sit together in a flask at room temperature and nothing happens. That is not because the reaction is uphill. The alkane is lower in energy than the alkene and H₂ it came from, so the reaction releases heat. The problem is the barrier: the H–H bond has to break first, and without a surface to help, that costs too much.

How the metal delivers the hydrogens

The metal surface does the work in stages. H₂ molecules adsorb onto the metal, which means they stick to its surface, and each one splits into two H atoms bound to the metal. The alkene adsorbs too. It lies flat, with its pi bond facing the metal. One H atom then moves across to one carbon. For a moment the other carbon is still bonded to the metal. A second H atom then moves to that carbon, and the finished alkane, which no longer sticks, drifts away.

Both hydrogens come up from the metal, so both reach the alkene from below. A flat alkene has two faces, the side above its plane and the side below, and only the face lying on the metal can be reached. Hydrogenation is therefore a syn addition: the two new C–H bonds form on the same face.

A ring makes this visible. Hydrogenate 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene, and both hydrogens go in on the face that was touching the metal. That leaves both methyls on the other face, so the product is cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane.

A ring cannot turn one of its carbons relative to the next, so a syn addition shows up as a cis product. Hydrogenating a 1,2-disubstituted cycloalkene gives the cis product.

That is the answer exams grade by, and it is the right way to predict the product. Real hydrogenations are a little less tidy. The first hydrogen transfer can run backwards: the metal can take back an H, sometimes from a different carbon, before the second H arrives. Each round trip gives the double bond a chance to shift along the chain or to flip between cis and trans. Palladium does this most, and 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene over Pd gives a real share of the trans product. Platinum stays much closer to clean syn addition. Partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils turns some cis double bonds into trans ones this same way, which is where trans fats come from (see Biomolecules).

Syn fixes the arrangement, not the mirror image

Now give the two ring carbons different groups. 1-Ethyl-2-methylcyclohexene can lie on the metal with either face down. Whichever face is down, both hydrogens arrive on it, so every molecule comes out cis. But the two faces give mirror images: one product has both new hydrogens on hashes, and the other has both on wedges.

The catalyst has no preference between the two faces, so half the molecules go each way. The product is one diastereomer, the cis one, formed as a racemic mixture of its two enantiomers.

This is what "syn" does and does not settle. It fixes the relative stereochemistry: which new group is on the same side as which. It does not fix the absolute stereochemistry, which enantiomer you get. The same holds for an open-chain alkene that gains two stereocenters: syn addition gives one diastereomer, as a racemate. This is not special to hydrogenation. An achiral starting material and an achiral reagent cannot favor one mirror image over the other.

1,2-Dimethylcyclohexene is the case where both faces give the same molecule. cis-1,2-Dimethylcyclohexane has a mirror plane, so it is meso and has no enantiomer. There is nothing to pair up, and the reaction gives a single product.

What gets reduced, and in what order

Put a molecule under H₂ over Pd/C at one atmosphere, the pressure of a hydrogen balloon, and its C=C is reduced in minutes. The C=O of a ketone in the same flask is left alone. Catalytic hydrogenation is strong, but it does not reduce everything. Its preference for some groups over others is its chemoselectivity, and the order matters more than any single entry:

  • Readily reduced: alkenes; alkynes, all the way to alkanes; imines (C=N double bonds); nitro groups, –NO₂, which become amines, –NH₂; and azides, –N₃, which also become amines.
  • Needs forcing conditions: ketones and aldehydes. They need higher pressure and a more active catalyst such as PtO₂. Over Pd/C under ordinary conditions they survive.
  • Essentially untouched: esters, carboxylic acids and amides. A benzene ring needs severe conditions, high pressure and high temperature, so it survives an ordinary hydrogenation intact.

This order is what makes the reaction useful. A molecule with an alkene, a ketone and a benzene ring is reduced at the alkene alone over Pd/C at one atmosphere. The hydride reagents NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄ do the reverse: they reduce the ketone and leave the alkene. Those reagents belong to Oxidation & Reduction, where the two approaches are compared in full.

Worked example — 4-phenylbut-3-en-2-one, three ways

The molecule is drawn at the top left of the figure below: a benzene ring joined to a C=C, which is joined to a ketone. Predict the product under each set of conditions.

Step 1 — sort the groups by the order above. The alkene is readily reduced. The ketone needs forcing conditions. The benzene ring needs severe ones. Everything below is read off that order.

Step 2 — H₂, Pd/C, 1 atm. Only the alkene is in range. The product is 4-phenylbutan-2-one, with the ring and the ketone untouched.

Step 3 — H₂, PtO₂, high pressure. Now the ketone is in range too, as well as the alkene. The product is 4-phenylbutan-2-ol, a secondary alcohol. The ring holds out longest, but with more pressure, heat and time it would be reduced as well.

Step 4 — NaBH₄ in methanol, then workup. Sodium borohydride hands a hydride ion to a carbonyl carbon. It does not react with an isolated C=C or with a benzene ring, so it reduces the ketone and only the ketone. The product is 4-phenylbut-3-en-2-ol, with the alkene intact.

Step 5 — compare steps 2 and 4. Same molecule: one reagent removes the alkene and keeps the C=O, and the other removes the C=O and keeps the alkene. That is what "complementary" means here, and it is why synthesis questions so often hand you a molecule with both.

Step 4 has a catch. In this molecule the C=C sits right next to the C=O, so the two pi bonds are linked (the molecule is a conjugated enone), and a hydride can sometimes add to the far end of the C=C instead of to the C=O. Conjugated systems are taught in Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions, and hydride addition to them in Oxidation & Reduction. Until then, treat a C=C next to a C=O as a flagged exception, not a case you are expected to predict.

Stopping an alkyne halfway, in either geometry

Take but-2-yne, CH₃–C≡C–CH₃. It is an alkyne: its two middle carbons share a triple bond, one sigma bond and two pi bonds, and the four carbons lie in a straight line. Alkynes, the next section, covers them in full. Two pi bonds mean hydrogenation can happen twice, and the useful chemistry here is stopping after the first.

Over ordinary Pd/C, but-2-yne takes up two molecules of H₂ and becomes butane: the reaction runs past the alkene. Two other sets of conditions stop at but-2-ene, and they give opposite geometries. That makes the pair one of the most useful stereochemical tools in the course.

Lindlar catalyst is palladium on calcium carbonate that has been poisoned, which means partly deactivated on purpose, with lead and with quinoline, a nitrogen compound that also clings to the metal. It is active enough to reduce an alkyne and too weak to reduce the alkene that forms. It is still a surface reaction, so the addition is syn, and the product is the cis (Z) alkene: cis-but-2-ene from but-2-yne.

Sodium in liquid ammonia is a dissolving-metal reduction. Ammonia boils at −33 °C, so it is used as a cold liquid, and sodium dissolves in it. There is no surface. Instead the alkyne takes on an electron, then a proton, then a second electron and a second proton:

  • Sodium gives the alkyne one electron. The result is a radical anion: one carbon carries an unpaired electron, and the other carries a lone pair and a negative charge.
  • The negative carbon takes a proton (H⁺) from ammonia. That leaves a vinyl radical, a radical on a carbon of a C=C.
  • A second sodium atom gives that carbon one more electron, making a vinyl anion: a lone pair and a negative charge on a carbon of a C=C.
  • The vinyl anion takes a second proton from ammonia, and the alkene is finished.

The geometry is decided at the vinyl anion. Its lone pair can sit on either side, but the anion settles with its two methyls as far apart as possible, on opposite sides. It holds that shape long enough to be protonated, which is what "configurationally stable" means here, and the proton goes where the lone pair was. The vinyl radical before it flips between its two bent shapes far too fast to decide anything, which is why the anion is the one that sets the geometry. The product is the trans (E) alkene: trans-but-2-ene from but-2-yne.

Learn the two as a pair, with the geometry attached, because that is how they are examined. "Make the cis alkene" means H₂ over Lindlar catalyst. "Make the trans alkene" means Na in liquid NH₃. One alkyne, two conditions, and you choose which stereoisomer you get.

Heats of hydrogenation, as a measuring tool

But-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene all take up H₂ to give the same product, butane. Each releases heat as it does so, and the three amounts differ:

Alkene (all give butane)SubstitutionHeat released (kcal/mol)Heat released (kJ/mol)
But-1-enemonosubstituted30.3127
cis-But-2-enedisubstituted, cis28.6120
trans-But-2-enedisubstituted, trans27.6116

Read the column rather than the single numbers. All three end at the same molecule, so the one that gives out the least heat must have started lowest. trans-But-2-ene releases 2.7 kcal/mol (11 kJ/mol) less than but-1-ene, so it was 2.7 kcal/mol more stable to begin with. The 1.0 kcal/mol (4 kJ/mol) between cis and trans is the cost of two methyls crowding each other on the same side.

The order that falls out, trans-disubstituted > cis-disubstituted > monosubstituted, is the alkene stability order from Alkene structure, which lists the same values as ΔH° with a minus sign. This reaction is how that order was measured. The comparison only works between alkenes that give the same alkane. 2-Methylpropene gives 2-methylpropane rather than butane, so its 28.4 kcal/mol (119 kJ/mol) is not strictly on this scale.

The same ruler comes back later. Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions uses it to show that buta-1,3-diene is about 15 kJ/mol (about 3.6 kcal/mol) more stable than two separate double bonds would be. The reaction is a measuring tool as well as a way to make alkanes.

What carries forward

Two things. First, stereochemistry: hydrogenation is syn, and the Lindlar and dissolving-metal pair is the standard way to build an alkene of known geometry from an alkyne, which is a large part of why alkynes are useful in synthesis. Second, chemoselectivity: set the order above beside the hydride reagents of Oxidation & Reduction, and you can answer most questions of the form "which reagent, and what survives?"
