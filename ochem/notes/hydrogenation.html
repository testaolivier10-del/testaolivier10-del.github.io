Alkenes & Alkynes · Section 53 of 121

Catalytic hydrogenation

Practice this — interactive lesson

An alkene's pi bond is nucleophilic, and that one fact rules out the obvious way of reducing it. Reduction usually means handing a carbon a hydride, H⁻ — but a hydride is itself a nucleophile, and an isolated C=C offers it nothing electrophilic to attack. (The hydride reagents that do this job on carbonyls are taught in Oxidation & Reduction, later.) Reducing a carbon–carbon multiple bond therefore needs a different mechanism entirely: adsorb both the alkene and hydrogen gas onto a metal surface and let them combine there.

The basic reaction

H₂ with a metal catalyst — palladium on carbon (Pd/C), platinum, or Raney nickel — adds two hydrogens across a double bond to give an alkane. The catalyst is essential: hydrogen gas and an alkene in a flask together do nothing at room temperature, because breaking the H–H bond costs too much without a surface to do it on.

The mechanism explains the one stereochemical fact you need. Both hydrogens are delivered from the metal surface, which the alkene is lying against, so they can only arrive on the face that is touching it. Hydrogenation is therefore a syn addition: the two new C–H bonds form on the same face.

On a ring, syn addition is directly visible in the product. Hydrogenating a 1,2-disubstituted cyclohexene gives the cis product, because both hydrogens arrive on the same face of the ring. That is a stereochemical claim you can be examined on, and it comes straight from the surface mechanism.

Say precisely what "syn" does and does not buy you. It fixes the relative stereochemistry — which new group is on the same side as which — because both hydrogens arrive from one face. It does not fix the absolute stereochemistry, because the alkene has two faces and the catalyst surface has no preference between them. Half the molecules lie down one way up and half the other.

So on a ring, where the relative answer is the whole answer, syn addition gives a clean single product: 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene gives cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane, which is achiral (meso) and is the only product. On an open chain, syn addition to an alkene that generates two stereocenters gives one diastereomer — but as a racemic mixture of its two enantiomers. Getting a single enantiomer out of a plain H₂/Pd/C reduction is not possible, and the reason is general rather than special to hydrogenation: an achiral substrate plus an achiral reagent cannot prefer one mirror image over the other.

What gets reduced, and in what order

Catalytic hydrogenation is powerful but not indiscriminate, and the ordering matters more than the list:

  • Readily reduced: alkenes, alkynes (all the way to alkanes), imines, nitro groups (to amines), and azides.
  • Requires forcing conditions: ketones and aldehydes need higher pressure and a more active catalyst such as PtO₂. Under ordinary conditions over Pd/C they survive.
  • Essentially untouched: esters, carboxylic acids and amides. Benzene rings need severe conditions — high pressure and temperature — which is why an aromatic ring survives an ordinary hydrogenation intact.

That ordering is what makes the reaction useful rather than merely strong. A molecule containing an alkene, a ketone and a benzene ring is hydrogenated at the alkene alone over Pd/C at one atmosphere — the exact complement of the hydride reagents — NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄, taught in Oxidation & Reduction — which reduce the ketone and leave the alkene. That complement is worth filing away now and will be worked properly there.

Stopping an alkyne halfway, in either geometry

One term is needed before the next section formally introduces it. An alkyne is a carbon–carbon triple bond: one sigma bond with two pi bonds stacked across it. Two pi bonds means hydrogenation can happen twice, and the useful chemistry here is about stopping after the first.

An alkyne over ordinary Pd/C is reduced past the alkene straight to the alkane. Two modified conditions stop it at the alkene, and they give opposite geometries — which makes the pair one of the most useful stereochemical tools in the course.

  • Lindlar catalyst — palladium on calcium carbonate, poisoned with quinoline and lead. Deliberately deactivated so it reduces an alkyne to an alkene and no further. It is still a surface reaction, so the addition is syn, and the product is the cis (Z) alkene.
  • Dissolving metal reduction — sodium in liquid ammonia. Not a surface reaction at all. Sodium donates an electron to the alkyne to give a radical anion, ammonia protonates it to a vinyl radical, a second electron gives a vinyl anion, and a second proton finishes the job. The geometry is decided at the anion, which is configurationally stable long enough to matter and sits with its two R groups as far apart as possible — trans. (The radical before it flips between its two bent shapes far too fast to decide anything, which is why it is the anion that is named.) The product is the trans (E) alkene.
Learn these two as a pair with the geometry attached, because that is how they are examined: "make the cis alkene" means Lindlar and "make the trans alkene" means Na/NH₃. One alkyne, two conditions, two stereoisomers you can choose between.
Worked example — 4-phenylbut-3-en-2-one, three ways

The substrate is PhCH=CH–CO–CH₃: a benzene ring, a C=C, and a ketone, all in one molecule. Predict the product of each set of conditions.

Step 1 — sort the groups by the ordering. Alkene: readily reduced. Ketone: forcing conditions only. Benzene ring: severe conditions only. That ordering is the whole answer generator; everything below is reading it off.

Step 2 — H₂, Pd/C, 1 atm. Only the alkene is in range. Product: PhCH₂CH₂–CO–CH₃, 4-phenylbutan-2-one. Ring and ketone come through untouched.

Step 3 — H₂, PtO₂, high pressure. Now the ketone is in range as well, and the alkene certainly is. Product: PhCH₂CH₂–CH(OH)–CH₃, the secondary alcohol. The ring still needs more than this.

Step 4 — NaBH₄ in methanol, then workup. The complement, and a look ahead: sodium borohydride delivers a hydride ion to a carbonyl carbon, and it gets its full treatment in Oxidation & Reduction. A hydride has no reaction with an isolated C=C and none with a benzene ring, so it takes the ketone and only the ketone: PhCH=CH–CH(OH)–CH₃, with the alkene intact.

Step 5 — read steps 2 and 4 side by side. Same molecule; one reagent removes the alkene and keeps the C=O, the other removes the C=O and keeps the alkene. That is what "complementary" means here, and it is why synthesis questions so often hand you a molecule with both.

A caution on step 4: this particular substrate is a conjugated enone, and hydride reagents can also add at the far end of a conjugated system. That belongs with conjugate addition later in the course; for now treat conjugated carbonyls as a flagged exception rather than a case you are expected to predict.

Heats of hydrogenation, as a measuring tool

Because hydrogenating any alkene gives the same kind of product — an alkane — the heat released is a direct measure of how stable the alkene was to begin with. A more stable alkene starts lower down and releases less energy.

Alkene (all give butane)SubstitutionHeat released
But-1-enemonosubstituted127 kJ/mol
cis-But-2-enedisubstituted, cis120 kJ/mol
trans-But-2-enedisubstituted, trans116 kJ/mol

Read the column, not the individual numbers. All three end at the same molecule, butane, so the one that gives out least heat must have started lowest. trans-But-2-ene releases 11 kJ/mol less than but-1-ene, which means it was 11 kJ/mol more stable to begin with — and the ordering that falls out, trans-disubstituted > cis-disubstituted > monosubstituted, is exactly the alkene stability order set out in the first section of this chapter. It was not asserted there; it was measured, this way. The 4 kJ/mol gap between cis and trans is the steric cost of two methyls on the same side, weighed directly. Comparing across different skeletons needs care — 2-methylpropene gives isobutane rather than butane, so its 119 kJ/mol is not strictly on this scale — but within one product the comparison is clean.

This is the measurement behind two claims about stability, one already made and one still to come. It is how alkene stability was shown to rise with substitution at the start of this chapter, and it is how Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions will put a number — about 15 kJ/mol — on the extra stability of buta-1,3-diene against two isolated double bonds. The reaction is useful as a ruler as well as a transformation.

What carries forward

Two things. The syn stereochemistry is a genuine stereochemical handle, and the Lindlar/dissolving-metal pair is the standard way to build a defined alkene geometry from an alkyne — which is why alkynes are so useful as synthetic intermediates. And the chemoselectivity ordering, held next to the hydride table you will meet in Oxidation & Reduction, is most of what you need to answer "which reagent, and what survives".
