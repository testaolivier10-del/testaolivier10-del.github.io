Polymers · Section 115 of 119

What a polymer is

Practice this — interactive lesson

A polymer is a molecule built by joining many small units end to end. The small unit is the monomer, the repeating piece inside the chain is the repeat unit, and the number of repeats is the degree of polymerization.

Nothing in this chapter is a new reaction. Polymers are made by the reactions you already know, run over and over on a molecule that has two reactive ends instead of one. That is the only structural requirement, and it is the whole idea.

Two ways to build a chain

Every polymerization in this course is one of two kinds, and the difference is whether anything is left over.

Addition (chain-growth)Condensation (step-growth)
MonomerAn alkeneTwo functional groups per monomer
Small molecule lostNoneUsually water, an alcohol or HCl — but not always; a polyurethane loses nothing
Repeat unit vs monomerSame atomsMonomer minus what was lost
How chains growOne active end, adding fastAny two pieces can join
ExamplesPolyethylene, PVC, polystyreneNylon, polyester, Kevlar

Read those two column headings as two separate questions rather than one. "Addition or condensation" asks what happens to the atoms: is a small molecule expelled? "Chain-growth or step-growth" asks how the chains get long: does one active end swallow monomer, or can any two pieces join? For the two big families the answers line up, which is why the names get used interchangeably. They come apart for ring-opening. Caprolactam polymerizes at one active end, so it is chain-growth, and it loses nothing, so by atom count it is an addition — yet the chain it gives is a polyamide, exactly like nylon 6,6. When a question asks "which kind", say which of the two questions you are answering.

The fastest way to tell them apart is to count atoms. Compare the repeat unit with the monomer: if the repeat unit is lighter, something was condensed out and it was a step-growth. If the formulas match, nothing was expelled — and for an alkene monomer that means the π bond simply opened, so it was an addition. The one case that breaks the shortcut is a ring-opening polymerization, where a cyclic monomer such as caprolactam — the seven-membered cyclic amide, or lactam, of 6-aminohexanoic acid — becomes nylon 6 with no change of formula at all; check whether the monomer has a C=C before you trust it.

Addition polymerization: one active end

An alkene polymerizes by the mechanism you already have from the addition chapter. A radical, cation or anion adds to the double bond, and the new reactive center adds to the next monomer, and so on.

  • Radical — an initiator such as a peroxide starts it. This is how low-density polyethylene and polystyrene are made. Most of the world's polyethylene is not radical-made: HDPE and LLDPE come from coordination catalysts, which the next section takes up.
  • Cationic — an acid or a Lewis acid (BF3 with a trace of water) starts it, and the chain end is a carbocation. It only runs if that cation is stabilized, so the monomer needs donating groups: isobutylene, CH2=C(CH3)2, whose growing end is tertiary, and vinyl ethers, CH2=CH–OR, where an oxygen lone pair stabilizes the cation directly. Polyisobutylene on its own is not yet butyl rubber: a saturated chain has nothing for sulfur to attack, so the industrial material is isobutylene copolymerized with one or two percent of isoprene, whose retained double bonds give vulcanization something to work on. That copolymer is the butyl rubber of inner tubes.
  • Anionic — a strong base or an organolithium such as BuLi starts it, and the chain end is a carbanion, which needs withdrawing or delocalizing groups: acrylonitrile, CH2=CHCN, methyl methacrylate, and styrene, where the ring spreads the negative charge out. There is no natural termination step, because two carbanions repel each other rather than pairing up, so the chain ends stay alive after the monomer runs out. That is living polymerization, and two things follow. Every chain starts at the same moment and grows at the same rate, so they finish nearly all the same length; and adding a second monomer afterwards extends every chain into a block copolymer.

Regiochemistry follows the same rule it always has: whichever end of the alkene gives the more stable intermediate is where the chain attaches. For a monosubstituted alkene that gives head-to-tail linking, with all the substituents on alternating carbons.

Condensation polymerization: everything joins everything

Here each monomer carries two functional groups, and any two pieces can react with each other — monomer with monomer, monomer with dimer, dimer with trimer. Chains grow slowly at first and the molecular weight climbs steeply only at very high conversion.

That is a real practical difference. In an addition polymerization long chains exist from the start; in a step-growth, 99% conversion still gives a degree of polymerization of only about 100, so the last fraction of a percent matters enormously.

Worked example — read three bottles and say what each gives

The question. Three bottles, and one question about each: chain, network, or neither, and what kind of polymerization? Bottle (a) holds methyl acrylate, CH2=CH–CO2CH3. Bottle (b) holds adipic acid with ethylene glycol. Bottle (c) holds glycerol, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH, with that same adipic acid.

Step 1 — count the sites on one monomer. (a) One C=C, which is two sites; the ester is a spectator, because nothing in the flask attacks it. (b) Two CO2H on one monomer and two OH on the other, so two sites each. (c) Glycerol has three OH; adipic acid has two.

Step 2 — two sites everywhere gives a line, any three ties the lines together. (a) a chain. (b) a chain. (c) a network.

Step 3 — count the atoms to name the kind. (a) The π bond opens and nothing leaves: addition, chain-growth, poly(methyl acrylate). (b) Each join makes an ester and expels H2O: condensation, step-growth, a polyester. (c) Same ester chemistry and the same water, but the third OH ties chains to each other, giving a cross-linked polyester thermoset of the alkyd type. (The named member of that family, glyptal, is the same glycerol with phthalic anhydride rather than adipic acid; swapping in the rigid aromatic diacid is what makes it a coating resin.)

The trap. In (a) it is tempting to count the ester as a second kind of site and predict a network. It is not a site, because nothing here attacks it. A reactive site is a group that reacts under the polymerization conditions, not any group that could react with something.

The other trap. Count on one monomer, never on the pair. A diacid and a diol are two sites each, which is why they give a chain; it is glycerol's third OH, sitting on a single molecule, that makes a network.

Everything past this point is a consequence of two numbers: how many reactive sites the monomer had, and how regular the chain that results is. Chain length, branching, stereoregularity and cross-linking are each taken up properly in the section on structure and properties.

What carries forward

Count the reactive sites to know whether you get a chain or a network. Count the atoms to know whether it was addition or condensation. And read the chain's regularity and branching to predict whether the material is a bag or a bottle — because polyethylene is both, and the difference is not chemistry but architecture.
