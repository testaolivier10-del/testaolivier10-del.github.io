The exception to 2ⁿ

Recall the 2ⁿ rule from the Stereocenters lesson: n stereocenters give a maximum of 2ⁿ stereoisomers. A meso compound is the reason that number is a maximum, not a guarantee: it's a molecule that has stereocenters, yet is achiral overall because it has an internal plane of symmetry, so two of its "expected" stereoisomers turn out to be the exact same achiral molecule.

Worked example: tartaric acid

Tartaric acid, HOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOH, has two stereocenters, so the 2ⁿ rule predicts up to 4 stereoisomers. Two of them — (R,R) and (S,S) — are genuine, non-superimposable mirror images of each other: a real enantiomeric pair, both chiral. But the third combination, (R,S), turns out to have an internal mirror plane cutting the molecule in half, making the top stereocenter's local environment the exact mirror of the bottom one. This (R,S) form is superimposable on its own mirror image (which would be (S,R) — but that's the same molecule, just flipped end-to-end) — so it is achiral, a meso compound. The real count for tartaric acid is 3 stereoisomers, not 4: (R,R), (S,S), and meso.

The signature setup for a meso compound: a molecule that is symmetric end-to-end (the same sequence of groups reading from either end), with stereocenters arranged so that one "cancels out" the other's contribution to the overall shape.

How to test for meso

Draw the molecule out (a Fischer projection, covered next lesson, makes this easiest) and look for an internal mirror plane splitting it into two identical mirror-image halves. If you find one, the molecule is meso — despite having stereocenters, it is achiral, has no enantiomer of its own, and does not rotate polarized light (its specific rotation is exactly zero, since it's achiral).

Relationship between the three tartaric acid stereoisomers

(R,R)- and (S,S)-tartaric acid are enantiomers of each other. Meso-tartaric acid is a diastereomer of both (R,R) and (S,S) — it's a stereoisomer, but not their mirror image (its own mirror image is itself). Meso-tartaric acid also has a different melting point from the (R,R)/(S,S) pair, exactly as you'd expect for a diastereomer relationship.
