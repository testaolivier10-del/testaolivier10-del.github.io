Esters & amides

Practice this — interactive lesson

Four functional groups share one skeleton: a carbonyl carbon bonded to a heteroatom group that can leave. They differ enormously in reactivity — across a range of about 10¹³ — and the ordering is not arbitrary. It follows from one structural question asked of each: how strongly does the attached group donate into the carbonyl?

Four derivatives, one core structure

Every carboxylic acid derivative is RCO–X, where X is a group that can act as a leaving group: acid chloride (X = Cl), anhydride (X = OCOR), ester (X = OR), and amide (X = NR₂). Nitriles are usually treated alongside them, since they hydrolyze to the same acid. They are not on the ladder that follows, because they have no leaving group to expel at all — their own section at the end of this chapter explains what they do instead.

Each X donates a lone pair into the carbonyl by resonance, exactly as the OH does in a carboxylic acid, and the degree of that donation sets the entire reactivity order.

Two rules produce this order and they reinforce each other.

Better resonance donation means a less electrophilic carbonyl. Nitrogen is the best donor of the four — least electronegative, holding its lone pair most loosely — so an amide's carbonyl is the least electron-poor. Chlorine is a poor donor: its lone pairs are held tightly, and its 3p orbital overlaps badly with carbon's 2p, a size mismatch. So an acid chloride's carbonyl is the most electrophilic.

Better leaving group means a faster collapse. Chloride is the conjugate base of HCl, pKa −7, and leaves readily. An amide nitrogen would have to leave as R₂N⁻, conjugate base of an amine at pKa 38, which it essentially never does. An anhydride's carboxylate (pKa 5) and an ester's alkoxide (pKa 16) sit in between.

Both rules point the same way, which is why the ordering is so robust. The practical consequence: any derivative can be converted into any derivative below it on the ladder, but not upward. Acid chloride to ester is easy; ester to acid chloride is not. Going up requires returning to the carboxylic acid and re-activating it with SOCl₂. The same ordering is visible in the infrared, because the harder the attached group donates, the more single-bond character the C=O has and the lower it stretches: acid chloride ≈ 1800 cm⁻¹, ester ≈ 1735, amide ≈ 1650. Two entries need a footnote rather than a rung. An anhydride gives two bands, ≈ 1820 and 1760, because its two carbonyls are close enough to couple and their in-phase and out-of-phase stretches separate; the pair straddles the acid chloride, so 1820 sitting above 1800 is not a ladder violation but a two-oscillator effect. And a carboxylic acid is famous for ≈ 1710 cm⁻¹, which is not a measure of how hard its OH donates: the isolated monomer absorbs near 1760, above an ester, and almost any real sample — neat liquid, KBr disc, concentrated solution — is the hydrogen-bonded dimer, whose H-bonding to the carbonyl oxygen drains the C=O and drags the band down to 1710. Dilute the same acid enough in a non-polar solvent and the 1710 band shrinks while a 1760 band grows. So read the infrared as the ladder with two asterisks, not as one number per rung.

Amide resonance is worth its own paragraph

Nitrogen's donation into the carbonyl is strong enough to have measurable structural consequences. The C–N bond in an amide has about 40% double-bond character, which means rotation about it is restricted — a barrier of roughly 20 kcal/mol, enough that the two methyls of dimethylformamide appear as separate NMR signals at room temperature. The whole amide unit is planar, and the nitrogen is not pyramidal as an amine's would be.

This has a consequence far outside organic chemistry. The peptide bond is an amide, and its planarity and restricted rotation are what limit a protein backbone to two rotatable angles per residue. Alpha helices and beta sheets exist because amide resonance takes a third degree of freedom away.

It also explains why amides are not basic. An amine's lone pair is available and it is a decent base (conjugate acid pKa ~10); an amide's lone pair is tied up in the carbonyl and it is essentially non-basic (conjugate acid pKa ~0). Same nitrogen, ten orders of magnitude apart, because of one resonance interaction. And when an amide finally is protonated by strong acid, the proton goes on the oxygen, not the nitrogen: protonating oxygen leaves the C=N⁺ delocalization intact, while protonating nitrogen would destroy the very stabilization that holds the unit together. N-protonation is about seven pKa units worse.

Esters: common, mild, versatile

Esters form from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol under acid catalysis — Fischer esterification, an equilibrium that must be driven by excess alcohol or by removing water — or from a more reactive derivative such as an acid chloride, which is faster and irreversible.

They undergo acyl substitution with a range of nucleophiles. Hydrolysis under acid is reversible and returns the acid and the alcohol; under base it is saponification, and because the product carboxylate is deprotonated and unreactive, the reaction is irreversible and goes to completion. Saponification with a long-chain ester is literally soap-making, which is where the name comes from. Both of those mechanisms are drawn step by step in the next section.

An ester will also swap one alkoxy group for another. Heat an ester in a large excess of a different alcohol, with acid or with a catalytic alkoxide, and the new alcohol attacks, the old alkoxide leaves, and you have a different ester: transesterification. Both possible leaving groups are alkoxides of similar pKa, so neither is favored and nothing drives the reaction except mass action — which is why it is always run with the new alcohol as the solvent. It is how biodiesel is made: a triglyceride plus excess methanol gives three methyl esters and glycerol.

Esters are reduced to primary alcohols by LiAlH₄, but not by NaBH₄ — a selectivity difference worth remembering. Grignard reagents — the carbon nucleophiles of Organometallics, the next chapter — add twice, because the ketone formed after the first addition is more reactive than the ester was, giving a tertiary alcohol with two identical new R groups.

Worked example — one ester, three reagents

Give the product of ethyl benzoate, PhCO–OEt, with (a) NaOH/H₂O then H₃O⁺, (b) excess LiAlH₄ then H₂O, (c) excess methylamine.

(a) Saponification. Hydroxide attacks the carbonyl and the tetrahedral intermediate expels ethoxide. Note that ethoxide (pKa 16) and hydroxide (pKa 15.7) are almost equally good leaving groups, so this step alone would sit near equilibrium; what settles it is the next one, in which the ethoxide just released deprotonates the benzoic acid just formed and cannot undo it. Products after acidic workup: benzoic acid + ethanol. Two separate molecules, not one.

(b) LiAlH₄. Hydride adds, ethoxide leaves, and the benzaldehyde that results is more electrophilic than the ester was, so a second hydride adds at once and cannot be stopped. Products: benzyl alcohol + ethanol. Two alcohols, and the aldehyde cannot be isolated.

(c) Methylamine. Nitrogen attacks, and the intermediate must choose between expelling EtO⁻ (pKa 16) and CH₃NH⁻ (pKa 38). Ethoxide goes. Product: N-methylbenzamide, one rung down the ladder — and it does not come back, because reversing would mean expelling an amide anion.

All three are the same two-step mechanism. The only thing that changed is the nucleophile, and in every case the OEt group is the one that leaves.

When the ring closes on itself

Put the alcohol and the acid on the same molecule and the ester can form intramolecularly. A cyclic ester is a lactone; a cyclic amide is a lactam. Nothing about the chemistry changes — same carbonyl, same leaving group, same acyl substitution — except that the leaving group can no longer wander off, because it is tethered to the other end of the ring.

Ring size decides whether they form at all. Five- and six-membered lactones (γ- and δ-) close readily and are the ones you meet: a 4-hydroxy or 5-hydroxy acid will cyclize on standing. Three- and four-membered rings are strained and do not form spontaneously.

That strain is the point of the exception. A β-lactam is a four-membered cyclic amide, and the ring holds the carbonyl carbon near 90° instead of 120°. In penicillin the nitrogen is also part of a second, fused ring, which forces it pyramidal — so its lone pair can no longer line up with the carbonyl pi system, the resonance that makes amides unreactive is largely switched off, and what is left is a strained, unusually electrophilic carbonyl. Penicillin is a β-lactam, and it kills bacteria by acylating a serine in the enzyme that cross-links their cell wall. A normal amide could not do that.

Amides: the least reactive, and biology's backbone

Amides are so unreactive that hydrolysis requires strong acid or base with prolonged heating. That stability is exactly why they are the linkage holding proteins together — a bond that must survive ordinary aqueous conditions for years while remaining selectively breakable by dedicated enzymes.

LiAlH₄ does reduce amides, and distinctively: the product is an amine rather than an alcohol, because the nitrogen stays attached throughout. That makes amide formation followed by reduction a standard two-step route to an amine, and it is taken up again in Amines.

Worked example — planning down the ladder

You have benzoic acid and need N-methylbenzamide.

Direct reaction of the acid with methylamine gives only an ammonium carboxylate salt — the amine is basic and simply takes the proton.

So go up first, then down: SOCl₂ converts the acid to benzoyl chloride, top of the ladder. Then methylamine, with a second equivalent (or pyridine) to mop up the HCl released, gives the amide cleanly.

Two steps, because the ladder only runs downhill and you had to climb back to the top before descending.

Reactivity and stability are inverse, and both matter. Acid chlorides are useful precisely because they are reactive — and for the same reason they are moisture-sensitive, fume in air, and cannot be stored. Amides are useless as acylating agents and invaluable as structural linkages. When a synthesis specifies an acid chloride, it wants reactivity; when biology specifies an amide, it wants permanence.

What carries forward

The ladder governs every conversion in the next section and is the single most tested idea in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives. Amide resonance recurs in Amines as the explanation for why amides are not basic, and in any discussion of protein structure. And the recognition that reactivity ordering here is really a leaving-group ranking connects this chapter straight back to How Reactions Happen.
