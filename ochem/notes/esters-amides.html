Four derivatives, one core structure

Every carboxylic acid derivative shares the same skeleton — a carbonyl carbon bonded to a heteroatom-containing group (X) that can act as a leaving group: acid chloride (RCOCl), anhydride (RCOOOCR'), ester (RCOOR'), and amide (RCONR'₂). The Carboxylic Acids lesson showed that the OH oxygen's lone pair donates into the carbonyl by resonance; every one of these X groups does the same donation, just to different degrees, and that degree of donation sets the whole reactivity order.

The reactivity ladder: acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide

Two intertwined rules explain the order. First, the better X is at donating electron density by resonance into the carbonyl, the less electrophilic (less reactive) the carbonyl carbon becomes — nitrogen (in an amide) is the best donor of the group (it's the least electronegative, holds its lone pair loosest), so amides are the least reactive. Chlorine (in an acid chloride) is a poor resonance donor (its lone pairs are held tightly and its larger 3p orbital overlaps poorly with carbon's 2p), so acid chlorides are the most reactive. Second, the better X is as a leaving group, the more reactive the derivative — Cl⁻ is an excellent leaving group (conjugate base of a strong acid, HCl), while NR₂⁻ (amide nitrogen leaving as an amide anion) is a terrible one (conjugate base of a very weak acid, an amine). Both rules point the same direction and reinforce each other.

This ranking is not arbitrary — every step down the ladder (chloride → anhydride → ester → amide) trades a worse leaving group/better resonance donor for progressively less reactivity, and each derivative can be made from any derivative above it in reactivity (but not usually the reverse) — exactly the logic explored mechanistically in the next lesson.

Esters: common, mild, versatile

Esters (RCOOR') form from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol under acid catalysis (Fischer esterification, an equilibrium reaction) or from a more reactive derivative (like an acid chloride) reacting with an alcohol. Esters undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution (next lesson) with a wide range of nucleophiles, including hydrolysis back to the acid (saponification under basic conditions is irreversible and gives a carboxylate plus an alcohol) and reduction by LiAlH₄ (but not the milder NaBH₄) all the way to a primary alcohol.

Amides: the least reactive, and biology's backbone

Amides (RCONR'₂) are so unreactive that they require harsh conditions (strong acid or base, with heat) to hydrolyze at all. This stability is exactly why amides are the linkage that holds proteins together (the peptide bond is simply an amide) — a bond that needs to survive ordinary biological conditions but still be selectively breakable by dedicated enzymes (proteases). LiAlH₄ can still reduce amides, but distinctively to an amine rather than an alcohol, since the nitrogen stays attached through the reduction.
