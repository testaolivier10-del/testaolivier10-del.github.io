Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives · Section 74 of 121

Esters & amides

Practice this — interactive lesson

Add a few drops of acetyl chloride to water and it reacts at once, giving off fumes of HCl. Methyl acetate in the same water barely changes unless an acid or a base is added. Acetamide survives until it is heated with strong acid or base for hours. All three are made from acetic acid, and all three react in the same way. What differs is the group bonded to the carbonyl carbon, and across the whole family that one change spreads the rates over a factor of about 10¹³.

This section explains the spread by asking two questions of that group: how easily can it leave, and how strongly does it share its lone pairs with the carbonyl?

Four derivatives, one core structure

Start from acetic acid, CH₃–C(=O)–OH, and replace its OH. Put Cl in its place and you have acetyl chloride. Put in O–C(=O)–CH₃, a second acetic acid unit joined through an oxygen, and you have acetic anhydride. With OCH₃ you have methyl acetate, and with NH₂ you have acetamide.

Each of these is a carboxylic acid derivative: a compound R–C(=O)–X, in which a group X has taken the place of the acid's OH. The four families are the acid chloride (X = Cl), the anhydride (X = O–C(=O)–R), the ester (X = OR) and the amide (X = NR₂, where either R may be an H). The R–C(=O)– part that they share is called an acyl group.

Nitriles, R–C≡N, are usually studied alongside them, because water converts a nitrile into the same carboxylic acid. They are left off the ladder below because they have no group X to lose. Their own section at the end of this chapter explains what they do instead.

In every derivative, the atom of X that is bonded to the carbonyl carbon carries lone pairs, and it shares them with the C=O by resonance. The OH of a carboxylic acid does the same, as the previous section showed. How strongly X shares its lone pairs, and how easily X leaves, together set the order of reactivity.

How a derivative reacts

Take hydroxide and acetyl chloride. The hydroxide adds to the carbonyl carbon just as a nucleophile adds to a ketone in Carbonyl Chemistry, and the C=O π electrons move onto the oxygen. The result is a tetrahedral intermediate: a carbon with four single bonds, one of them to an O⁻.

A ketone's tetrahedral intermediate can only pick up a proton. This one also carries a Cl, which can leave. So a lone pair on the O⁻ moves back down to re-form the C=O, and chloride departs, taking the C–Cl electrons with it. The OH has replaced the Cl.

This exchange, in which a nucleophile adds and a group is then expelled, is called nucleophilic acyl substitution. Every derivative on this page reacts by it, and the next section takes it apart in full. What matters here is that the second step expels X, so the whole reaction depends on how willingly X leaves.

The reactivity ladder

Rank the four acetic acid derivatives by how easily X leaves and you get the ladder below: acetyl chloride at the top, then acetic anhydride, then methyl acetate, with acetamide at the bottom.

Two effects produce this order, and both point the same way.

A better leaving group means a faster second step. A group leaves well when it leaves as a weak base, the rule from How Reactions Happen. Chloride is the conjugate base of HCl, pKa −7, so it leaves readily. An amide's nitrogen would have to leave as R₂N⁻, the conjugate base of an amine with pKa near 38, and it essentially never does. An anhydride's carboxylate (conjugate acid pKa about 5) and an ester's alkoxide (about 16) sit in between.

Stronger donation means a less electrophilic carbonyl. The more lone-pair density X pushes into the C=O, the less positive the carbonyl carbon becomes, and the less it attracts a nucleophile in the first step. Nitrogen is the best donor of the four. It is the least electronegative of the three atoms involved, so it holds its lone pair most loosely, and an amide's carbonyl is the least electron-poor.

The bridging oxygen of an anhydride donates to two carbonyls at once, so each one receives only part of its lone-pair density. That shared donation is weaker than an ester oxygen's, which serves only one carbonyl.

Chlorine donates worst, and the reason is the size of its orbitals. Its lone pairs sit in 3p orbitals, which are larger and more spread out than carbon's 2p, and the C–Cl bond is long. The two orbitals overlap poorly side by side, so little density passes into the C=O, and an acid chloride's carbonyl is the most electrophilic of the four. Chlorine is also more electronegative than nitrogen, so it holds its lone pairs more tightly to begin with.

Because both effects point the same way, the order is robust, and it has a practical consequence: any derivative can be turned into one below it on the ladder, but not into one above it. Acetyl chloride and methanol give methyl acetate easily, but methyl acetate cannot be turned back into acetyl chloride directly. To climb, go back to the carboxylic acid and treat it with SOCl₂, which gives the acid chloride at the top.

The ladder in the infrared

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy measures the frequencies at which bonds stretch, reported in cm⁻¹. A stiffer bond stretches at a higher frequency. The method is taught in Spectroscopy; here only its readings for the C=O matter.

Those readings follow the ladder. An acid chloride's C=O absorbs near 1800 cm⁻¹, an ester's near 1735 and an amide's near 1650. The harder X donates, the more weight falls on the resonance contributor in which the carbon–oxygen bond is single. The C=O loses some of its double-bond character, becomes less stiff, and absorbs at a lower number.

Two entries need a note of their own. An anhydride shows two C=O bands, near 1820 and 1760 cm⁻¹. Its two carbonyls are close enough to stretch together, either in phase or out of phase, and the two motions absorb at different frequencies. So the 1820 band, above the acid chloride's 1800, does not break the ladder. It comes from two coupled bonds, not from weaker donation.

A carboxylic acid usually shows its C=O near 1710 cm⁻¹, but that number does not measure how hard its OH donates. It belongs to the hydrogen-bonded dimer, the pair of acid molecules held together by two hydrogen bonds that the previous section drew. Almost every real sample is the dimer: the neat liquid, a solid pressed into a KBr disc, a concentrated solution. The hydrogen bond to the carbonyl oxygen draws electron density out of the C=O and lowers its frequency.

A single acid molecule, with no partner, absorbs near 1760 cm⁻¹, above an ester. Dilute an acid far enough in a nonpolar solvent and you can watch the change: the 1710 band shrinks and a 1760 band grows. So read the infrared as the ladder with these two footnotes, not as one number per rung.

Amide resonance

Look first at dimethylformamide (DMF), HC(=O)N(CH₃)₂. Its nitrogen donates into the carbonyl so strongly that the C–N bond has about 40% double-bond character. A double bond does not turn, so rotation about this C–N bond is restricted, with a barrier of roughly 20 kcal/mol.

The shape follows from the same donation. The O, C and N, together with the three atoms bonded to that C and N, lie in one plane. The nitrogen is flat rather than pyramidal like an amine's, because its lone pair sits in a p orbital lined up with the C=O π system.

The two methyls of DMF look the same on paper. In the molecule, one sits on the oxygen side of the C–N bond and the other on the hydrogen side, and the locked bond keeps them from swapping. ¹H NMR spectroscopy, which gives a separate signal for each set of hydrogens in a different environment and is also taught in Spectroscopy, shows them as two separate signals at room temperature.

This matters well beyond organic chemistry. The peptide bond that joins amino acids in a protein is an amide, so it is planar and does not turn. That leaves only two bonds free to rotate in each amino acid unit of the backbone. Alpha helices and beta sheets, the regular shapes that proteins fold into, exist because amide resonance takes a third rotation away. Biomolecules returns to this.

Amide resonance also explains why amides are not basic. An amine's lone pair is free, so an amine is a decent base: its conjugate acid has a pKa of about 10. An amide's lone pair is tied up in the carbonyl, so an amide is essentially non-basic: its conjugate acid has a pKa of about 0. The same kind of nitrogen differs by ten orders of magnitude because of one resonance interaction.

Strong acid does protonate an amide, and the proton goes on the oxygen, not the nitrogen. With the proton on oxygen, the nitrogen lone pair still feeds the C=O, so the positive charge is shared between O and N. With the proton on nitrogen, that lone pair is used up in the new N–H bond, and the delocalization that stabilizes the amide is lost. Protonation on nitrogen is about seven pKa units less favorable.

Esters: common, mild, versatile

An ester forms from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol with an acid catalyst. This is Fischer esterification. It is an equilibrium, so it is pushed toward the ester with a large excess of the alcohol or by removing water as it forms. An acid chloride and an alcohol also give the ester, faster and with no equilibrium to fight, because that reaction runs down the ladder. The next section draws the Fischer mechanism.

Water with an acid catalyst runs Fischer esterification in reverse and gives back the acid and the alcohol. Every step is reversible, so this hydrolysis also stops at an equilibrium.

In base, the outcome changes. Take ethyl benzoate and aqueous NaOH. Hydroxide adds to the carbonyl and ethoxide leaves, following the two-step pattern. Ethoxide (ethanol's pKa is 16) and hydroxide (water's is 15.7) are almost equally good leaving groups, so those two steps alone would sit near equilibrium.

A third step settles it. The ethoxide just released is a strong base, and it takes the proton from the benzoic acid just formed. The benzoate ion that results is negatively charged and resonance-stabilized, so no nucleophile attacks it, and the reaction cannot run backward. Base hydrolysis of an ester is therefore irreversible and goes to completion. It is called saponification, from the Latin word for soap: run on the long-chain esters in fats, it is how soap is made. An acidic workup with H₃O⁺ at the end protonates the benzoate and gives benzoic acid.

An ester will also trade one alkoxy group, an O–R group, for another. Heat an ester in a large excess of a different alcohol, with an acid or a catalytic amount of alkoxide. The new alcohol adds, the old alkoxide leaves, and a different ester forms. This is transesterification. The group coming in and the group leaving are alkoxides of similar pKa, so neither side is favored. Only the large excess of the new alcohol pushes the reaction forward, which is why it is run with that alcohol as the solvent.

Biodiesel is made this way. A vegetable oil is a triglyceride, three esters built on one molecule of glycerol, HOCH₂–CH(OH)–CH₂OH. Heated with excess methanol and a catalyst, it gives three methyl esters, which are the fuel, and glycerol.

LiAlH₄ reduces an ester to two alcohols, while NaBH₄ leaves it alone. Oxidation & Reduction explains both halves of that selectivity.

Grignard reagents, RMgX, are carbon nucleophiles taught in Organometallics, the next chapter. They add to an ester twice. The first addition gives a ketone, which is more reactive than the ester was, so a second RMgX adds at once. The product is a tertiary alcohol carrying two identical new R groups.

Worked example — one ester, three reagents

Give the products of ethyl benzoate, C₆H₅–C(=O)–OCH₂CH₃, with (a) NaOH in water, then H₃O⁺; (b) excess LiAlH₄, then H₂O; (c) excess methylamine, CH₃NH₂.

(a) Saponification, the three stages in the figure above. After the acidic workup the products are benzoic acid + ethanol: two separate molecules, not one.

(b) LiAlH₄. Hydride adds and ethoxide leaves. The benzaldehyde that forms is more electrophilic than the ester was, so a second hydride adds at once, and the aldehyde cannot be isolated. The products are benzyl alcohol + ethanol: two alcohols.

(c) Methylamine. Nitrogen adds, and the intermediate must choose between expelling CH₃CH₂O⁻ (pKa 16) and CH₃NH⁻ (pKa 38). Ethoxide goes. The product is N-methylbenzamide, one rung down the ladder, and it does not come back, because going back would mean expelling an amide anion.

All three begin with the same two-step pattern. Only the nucleophile changes, and in every case the OCH₂CH₃ group is the one that leaves.

When the ring closes on itself

Put the OH and the COOH on the same molecule and the ester can form inside it. 4-Hydroxybutanoic acid does this on standing, faster with a trace of acid. Its OH oxygen adds to its own carbonyl carbon, water leaves, and the product is a five-membered ring that contains the ester group.

A cyclic ester is a lactone, and a cyclic amide is a lactam. The chemistry is the same acyl substitution, with one difference: the alcohol part cannot drift away, because it is tied to the other end of the ring.

Lactones are named with Greek letters. Counting from the carbonyl, the next carbon is α (alpha), then β (beta), γ (gamma) and δ (delta). The letter of the carbon that carries the ring oxygen names the lactone. 4-Hydroxybutanoic acid has its OH on the γ carbon, so it closes to a γ-lactone, a five-membered ring called γ-butyrolactone. 5-Hydroxypentanoic acid has its OH on the δ carbon and closes to a six-membered δ-lactone.

Ring size decides whether a lactone forms at all. Five- and six-membered lactones close readily, and they are the ones you meet: a 4-hydroxy or 5-hydroxy acid will cyclize on standing. Three- and four-membered rings are strained and do not form on their own.

That strain is what makes one exception so important. A β-lactam is a four-membered cyclic amide. Its ring holds the carbonyl carbon near 90° instead of the 120° that a flat sp² carbon prefers.

Penicillin contains a β-lactam, and its nitrogen is also part of a second, five-membered ring fused to the first. Belonging to both rings forces that nitrogen to be pyramidal, so its lone pair no longer lines up with the C=O π system. The resonance that makes ordinary amides unreactive is largely switched off, and what remains is a strained, unusually electrophilic carbonyl.

Penicillin kills bacteria by acylating (attaching its acyl group to) the enzyme that cross-links their cell walls. The target is a serine, an amino acid whose side chain ends in CH₂OH, at the enzyme's active site. The serine oxygen adds to the β-lactam carbonyl, and the ring C–N bond breaks. Opening the ring releases its strain, and that is what pays for expelling an amide nitrogen. An ordinary amide could not do this.

Amides: the least reactive, and biology's backbone

Amides are so unreactive that hydrolyzing one takes strong acid or base and prolonged heating. That stability is why an amide is the linkage that holds proteins together. The bond has to survive ordinary water-based conditions for years, and yet enzymes built for the job can still break it selectively.

LiAlH₄ does reduce an amide, and the product is an amine, not an alcohol: the oxygen leaves and the nitrogen stays attached. Oxidation & Reduction shows why. Making an amide and then reducing it is a standard two-step route to an amine, and Amines takes it up again.

Worked example — planning down the ladder

You have benzoic acid and need N-methylbenzamide.

Mixing the acid with methylamine at room temperature gives only a salt, methylammonium benzoate. The amine is a base, and it simply takes the acid's proton. Heating that salt strongly does drive off water and form the amide, but it takes temperatures well above 150 °C, which many molecules do not survive.

So go up first, then down. SOCl₂ turns the acid into benzoyl chloride, at the top of the ladder. Methylamine then gives the amide cleanly. The reaction releases HCl, so use a second equivalent of methylamine, or add pyridine, a weak amine base, to take up that HCl; otherwise it ties up half the methylamine as its salt.

Two steps, because the ladder only runs downhill and you had to climb back to the top before going down.

Reactivity and stability are two sides of one property. Acid chlorides are useful because they are reactive, and for the same reason they react with moisture, fume in air and must be stored dry. Amides are useless for transferring an acyl group and invaluable as structural linkages. When a synthesis calls for an acid chloride, it wants reactivity; when biology uses an amide, it wants permanence.

What carries forward

The ladder governs every conversion in the next section and is among the most tested ideas in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives. Amide resonance returns in Amines, to explain why amides are not basic, and in Biomolecules, in protein structure. And because the reactivity order here is really a leaving-group ranking, this chapter connects straight back to How Reactions Happen.
