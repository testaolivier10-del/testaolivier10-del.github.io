Nitrogen's lone pair: the basis of amine basicity

An amine nitrogen is sp³ hybridized with a lone pair available to accept a proton — the textbook Brønsted-Lowry base (Bronsted lesson) and Lewis base (Lewis Acids lesson) at once. A typical alkyl amine's conjugate acid (the ammonium ion, R-NH₃⁺) has a pKa around 10-11, making amines the most common, most reliably basic functional group in organic chemistry — far more basic than water (conjugate-acid pKa ≈ −1.7) or an alcohol (conjugate-acid pKa even lower).

Alkyl substitution: a small, complicated effect

You might expect more alkyl groups (more electron donation by induction, the same effect that stabilizes carbocations) to make an amine progressively more basic — and in the gas phase, that's exactly what happens. In water, though, solvation effects compete: a more substituted ammonium ion has fewer N–H bonds available to hydrogen-bond with water and stabilize the positive charge, partially offsetting the inductive effect. The net result in aqueous solution is a much smaller, less predictable spread between methyl-, dimethyl-, and trimethylamine than induction alone would suggest — a good reminder that solvent effects can compete with, and even reverse, gas-phase intuition.

Despite this complication, every simple alkyl amine (primary, secondary, or tertiary) still clusters in the same general pKaH range (roughly 9-11) — the differences between them are minor compared to the dramatic effect covered next.

Resonance donation: the dramatic basicity killer

Compare aniline (a phenyl amine, lone pair conjugated with the aromatic ring — previewed further in Module 13) to a plain alkylamine: aniline's conjugate acid has a pKa around 4.6, roughly a million times less basic than cyclohexylamine (pKaH ≈ 10.7). The nitrogen lone pair in aniline is delocalized into the aromatic ring by resonance — exactly the Resonance factor from the ARIO framework — making it far less available to accept a proton. An amide nitrogen (Esters & Amides lesson) is an even more extreme case: its lone pair is so heavily delocalized into the adjacent carbonyl that amides are barely basic at all (conjugate-acid pKa around −1, essentially non-basic under ordinary conditions) — this is also exactly why an amide nitrogen is such a poor nucleophile and leaving group, as already established in Module 10.

The basicity ranking that results

Putting these effects together gives a clear ranking: alkylamine (most basic, pKaH ≈ 10-11) > ammonia (pKaH ≈ 9.2, no alkyl donation) > aniline-type arylamine (pKaH ≈ 4.6, resonance into a ring) > amide (least basic, pKaH ≈ −1, resonance into a carbonyl). Whenever you need to rank the basicity of two nitrogen-containing compounds, the first and most powerful question to ask is: is the lone pair delocalized by resonance into something else, and if so, into what?
