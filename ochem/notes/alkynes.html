sp hybridization and linear geometry

Each carbon of a C≡C triple bond is sp hybridized (from the Hybridization lesson): two sp orbitals, 180° apart, form sigma bonds, and two unhybridized p orbitals (perpendicular to each other and to the sp axis) form two separate pi bonds. A triple bond is therefore one sigma bond plus two pi bonds. The 180° sp geometry makes every alkyne carbon and its two attached atoms perfectly linear.

Terminal alkyne C–H: unusually acidic

A terminal alkyne's C–H (pKa ≈ 25) is dramatically more acidic than an alkene C–H (pKa ≈ 44) or alkane C–H (pKa ≈ 50) — this is the Orbital factor from the ARIO acidity framework (Acidity Factors lesson). The conjugate base's lone pair sits in an sp orbital, which has 50% s-character — far more than an sp² (33% s) or sp³ (25% s) carbanion. Because s orbitals hold electron density closer to the nucleus, more s-character means a more stable (lower-energy) anion, hence a more acidic C–H.

pKa 25 is well within reach of strong, non-nucleophilic-enough bases like NaNH₂ (sodium amide, conjugate acid NH₃ pKa ≈ 38) or NaH. It is NOT acidic enough for hydroxide (conjugate acid water, pKa ≈ 15.7) to deprotonate to any useful extent — the equilibrium would sit almost entirely on the starting-material side.

Making acetylide anions and using them as nucleophiles

Deprotonating a terminal alkyne with NaNH₂ produces an acetylide anion — a strong, small, reactive carbanion nucleophile. Acetylides do exactly what any strong nucleophile does from Module 6: they perform SN2 attacks on unhindered alkyl halides, forming a new C–C bond and extending the carbon skeleton. This is one of organic chemistry's few reliable ways to build a new carbon-carbon bond from scratch.

Addition reactions: partial vs. complete

Because an alkyne has two pi bonds, it can add electrophiles or H₂ once (giving an alkene) or twice (giving an alkane). H₂ with a Lindlar catalyst (a poisoned, less active Pd catalyst) stops cleanly at the alkene stage and delivers syn addition, giving the cis alkene. H₂ with a normal Pd or Pt catalyst goes all the way to the alkane. Acid-catalyzed hydration (H₃O⁺/Hg²⁺ catalyst) adds water Markovnikov-style to give an unstable enol, which immediately tautomerizes to the more stable ketone (or aldehyde for a terminal alkyne) — the same enol/keto relationship that becomes central in Module 11 (Enolate Chemistry).
