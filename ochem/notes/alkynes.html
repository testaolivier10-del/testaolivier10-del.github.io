Alkynes

Practice this — interactive lesson

Alkynes do everything alkenes do, twice over — but their real importance in a synthesis course is different. A terminal alkyne is acidic enough to deprotonate, and the resulting carbanion is one of the very few reliable carbon nucleophiles available at this stage. Alkynes are how you make carbon skeletons bigger.

sp hybridization and linear geometry

Each carbon of a C≡C is sp hybridized: two sp orbitals 180° apart form the sigma bonds, and two unhybridized p orbitals — perpendicular to each other and to the sp axis — form two separate pi bonds. A triple bond is one sigma plus two pi.

The 180° geometry makes every alkyne carbon and its attached atom perfectly linear, which has a structural consequence: a triple bond cannot be accommodated in a ring smaller than about eight members without severe strain. The C≡C is about 120 pm long and worth roughly 200 kcal/mol in total.

Because a triple bond counts as two degrees of unsaturation, an alkyne and a compound with two separate double bonds have the same molecular formula — which is exactly the kind of ambiguity spectroscopy is for.

Terminal alkyne C–H: unusually acidic

A terminal alkyne's C–H has a pKa of about 25, against roughly 44 for an alkene C–H and 50 for an alkane. That is nineteen orders of magnitude more acidic than an alkene, from nothing but hybridization.

This is the Orbital factor from the ARIO framework in Acids & Bases. The conjugate base's lone pair sits in an sp orbital, which is 50% s in character, against 33% for sp² and 25% for sp³. More s-character holds the electron density closer to the nucleus, lowering the anion's energy and making the parent C–H more acidic.

pKa 25 is comfortably within reach of sodium amide (NaNH₂, conjugate acid pKa 38) or sodium hydride. It is not within reach of hydroxide or alkoxide, whose conjugate acids sit around pKa 16 — nine units on the wrong side, so the equilibrium would lie 10⁹ to one against deprotonation. When a question specifies NaNH₂ rather than NaOH, that choice is the whole point of the step.

Acetylide anions: making carbon–carbon bonds

Deprotonating a terminal alkyne with NaNH₂ gives an acetylide anion — a small, strong, genuinely nucleophilic carbanion. Acetylides do exactly what any strong nucleophile does in Substitution & Elimination: SN2 on an unhindered alkyl halide, forming a new carbon–carbon bond.

This matters more than it may look. Most of the reactions you have met so far rearrange or decorate an existing skeleton; this one extends it. Acetylide alkylation, together with the enolate and organometallic chemistry of later chapters, is one of a short list of dependable ways to build carbon frameworks.

Acetylide alkylation only works on methyl and primary halides. An acetylide is a strong base as well as a strong nucleophile, so on a secondary or tertiary substrate it does E2 instead and returns the alkyne unchanged along with an alkene. Planning a synthesis that alkylates an acetylide with tert-butyl bromide is a standard error; if you need that connection, install the branch differently.
Worked example — a two-step chain extension

From acetylene (HC≡CH) to hex-1-yne.

Step 1: NaNH₂ deprotonates one terminus, giving HC≡C⁻ Na⁺.

Step 2: add 1-bromobutane. SN2 gives HC≡C–CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃.

Two carbons plus four carbons, joined by a C–C bond you made. Repeat the sequence on the remaining terminal C–H and you can build an internal alkyne with whatever two groups you like on either end.

Making the triple bond: double dehydrohalogenation

Acetylide alkylation lengthens a chain that already contains a triple bond. The obvious question is where the first one comes from, and the answer is elimination — run twice. Treat a vicinal dihalide (halogens on adjacent carbons) or a geminal dihalide (both on the same carbon) with excess sodium amide, and two successive E2 eliminations remove two molecules of HX: the first gives a vinyl halide, the second gives the alkyne.

The second elimination is the hard one. A vinyl halide has its leaving group on an sp² carbon and its beta hydrogen on the other alkene carbon, which is a much less willing substrate than an ordinary alkyl halide — which is exactly why the reagent is NaNH₂ at temperature and not KOH in ethanol. And because vicinal dihalides come straight from alkenes plus Br₂, the whole sequence is a way of converting an alkene into an alkyne: Br₂, then excess NaNH₂.

Count your equivalents. Making an internal alkyne takes two equivalents of NaNH₂, one per elimination. Making a terminal alkyne takes three, because the product’s own C–H (pKa 25) is immediately deprotonated by the amide still in the flask — the third equivalent is consumed holding the acetylide, and a separate aqueous workup is what gives you back the neutral alkyne. An exam question that specifies "excess NaNH₂, then H₂O" is testing whether you noticed.

Addition reactions: partial versus complete

Two pi bonds means addition can stop once, giving an alkene, or go twice, giving a saturated product. Which one you get is usually a matter of how many equivalents you supply, and the electrophilic additions you met earlier in this chapter all carry over with that one extra decision.

HX. One equivalent adds Markovnikov across one pi bond to give a vinyl halide, with the halogen on the more substituted carbon — for a terminal alkyne, on the internal carbon. A second equivalent adds to that alkene, and Markovnikov again puts the second halogen on the same carbon as the first, because the halogen already there stabilizes the cation beside it by donating a lone pair. The product is a geminal dihalide: 1-butyne plus two equivalents of HBr gives 2,2-dibromobutane, not 2,3-.

X₂. One equivalent gives a trans dihaloalkene, the anti stereochemistry coming from the same bridged-ion opening that makes alkene bromination anti. A second equivalent saturates the rest, giving the tetrahalide. Adding the first equivalent to the alkyne is slower than adding the second to the dihaloalkene it produces, so stopping cleanly at one equivalent needs the bromine added in the cold, and in a measured amount.

Reduction is the case where reagent choice, not stoichiometry, does the deciding — and the pair of partial reductions is particularly valuable because they give opposite alkene geometries.

H₂ with Lindlar catalyst — palladium poisoned with lead and quinoline to make it deliberately less active — stops cleanly at the alkene and delivers both hydrogens to the same face. Syn addition gives the cis alkene.

Na in liquid NH₃, a dissolving-metal reduction, never touches a surface, so nothing forces the two hydrogens to the same face. It runs in four steps, alternating electron and proton:

  1. Sodium dissolves in ammonia to give a solvated electron, which adds to one pi bond. The result is a radical anion — one carbon holds an unpaired electron, the other a lone pair and a negative charge.
  2. That carbanion is basic enough to take a proton from ammonia, giving a vinyl radical.
  3. Here is where the geometry is decided. A vinyl radical is bent, and it inverts back and forth quickly, so it reaches the arrangement with its two substituents as far apart as possible — the trans one — before anything else happens to it. It spends most of its short life there.
  4. A second electron converts it to a vinyl anion, which unlike the radical does not invert quickly, so the trans arrangement is locked in; a second proton from ammonia finishes the job.

Nothing in that sequence is stereospecific in the way a concerted reaction is. The trans product wins because the intermediate had time to pick its shape, which is thermodynamic control acting on an intermediate rather than on a product — and it is why the answer is trans rather than a mixture.

H₂ with ordinary Pd or Pt does not stop; it goes all the way to the alkane.

So a single internal alkyne can be converted into the cis alkene, the trans alkene, or the alkane, purely by reagent choice — which is precisely why alkynes are so useful as synthetic intermediates. Alkene geometry is otherwise hard to control.

Hydration and the enol

Acid-catalyzed hydration of an alkyne, with Hg²⁺ as catalyst, adds water with Markovnikov selectivity to give an enol — a compound with OH attached directly to a C=C. Enols are not stable, and this one immediately tautomerizes: the proton migrates from oxygen to carbon and the pi bond shifts from C=C to C=O, giving a ketone.

The mercury is doing here exactly what it did in the oxymercuration of an alkene: the pi bond attacks Hg²⁺, mercury bridges both carbons, and water opens that bridge at the carbon carrying more positive character — the more substituted one. The Markovnikov outcome is the bridged ion’s doing, not a separate rule for alkynes, and the mercury falls off on its own during the tautomerization rather than needing a borohydride step.

The tautomerization is not a resonance form — atoms move, so keto and enol are genuinely different compounds in equilibrium, drawn with ⇌. The keto form is favored by at least 10⁵ to one — for acetone the equilibrium constant is nearer 10⁸, so the enol content of a bottle of acetone is a few parts per billion — because a C=O is worth substantially more than a C=C plus an O–H.

The regiochemistry can be flipped here too. Markovnikov hydration of a terminal alkyne gives a methyl ketone; hydroboration–oxidation of the same alkyne, using a bulky borane, puts the oxygen on the terminal carbon and gives an aldehyde instead.

The keto–enol relationship introduced here becomes central in Enolate Chemistry. Every enolate reaction — aldol, Claisen, alpha-halogenation, alkylation — runs through the same enol or its anion. Meeting it first as a fleeting intermediate in alkyne hydration is a useful way in, because here it is doing something simple and visible.

What carries forward

Acetylide chemistry is one of the course's main carbon–carbon bond-forming tools and appears in synthesis problems constantly. The Lindlar/sodium-ammonia pair is the standard way to set alkene geometry deliberately. And keto–enol tautomerism, met here in passing, is the foundation of the entire enolate chapter.
