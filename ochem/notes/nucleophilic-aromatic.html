Aromatic Follow-Through · Section 94 of 117

SNAr and benzyne

Practice this — interactive lesson

Everything you know about substitution on a benzene ring so far runs one way: the ring is electron-rich, so it attacks an electrophile. That is electrophilic aromatic substitution, and it is the whole of the aromatic chapter up to here.

The reverse — a nucleophile displacing a leaving group on the ring — looks impossible at first, and for a plain aryl halide it is. Chlorobenzene does not do SN2, because the backside of that carbon is behind the ring and the carbon is sp². It does not do SN1 either, because an aryl cation is badly unstable.

But there are two classical ways round it — and one modern one you already have, since palladium cross-coupling puts a nucleophile-derived group on an unactivated aryl carbon with neither a nitro group nor a brutal base. What decides between the two classical routes is what else is on the ring.

Route one: SNAr, when the ring is electron-poor

Put strong electron-withdrawing groups on the ring, ortho or para to the leaving group, and the picture changes. The nucleophile adds to the carbon bearing the leaving group, giving a negatively charged intermediate called a Meisenheimer complex, and then the leaving group departs.

addition first, elimination second — the opposite order from every substitution you have seen

Aromaticity is lost in the intermediate and restored at the end. That is the cost, and it is why the reaction needs help: the nitro groups pay for it by delocalizing the negative charge onto their own oxygens.

Three consequences fall out of that mechanism, and all three are testable:

  • The withdrawing group must be ortho or para. Only from those positions can the negative charge reach it by resonance. A meta nitro group still withdraws inductively and does speed the reaction up measurably, but induction is the small effect — far too little to make SNAr practical. Resonance is what the reaction needs. How many are needed is a fair question: one ortho or para nitro group is enough with a good nucleophile and heat, two make it a comfortable reaction, and three (picryl chloride) make it near-instantaneous. Exam substrates almost always carry two, which is why 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene is the compound you will keep seeing.
  • Fluorine is the best leaving group here — the reverse of every other substitution. The rate-determining step is the nucleophile's attack, not the C–X bond breaking, and fluorine's electronegativity makes that carbon the most electrophilic. F > Cl > Br > I.
  • No rearrangement. The nucleophile arrives exactly where the leaving group was.
Worked example — 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene + CH3NH2

Step 1. Read the ring. Two nitro groups, at C2 and C4 relative to the fluorine — one ortho, one para. Both can reach the carbon under attack by resonance, so SNAr is on.

Step 2. Is the leaving group a problem? No, and this is the counter-intuitive part. Fluorine is the best leaving group here, because the C–F bond is not breaking in the slow step.

Step 3. Add. The nitrogen of methylamine attacks C1, the carbon carrying the fluorine. That carbon becomes sp³ and holds both the F and the N, which breaks the cycle, so the ring is no longer aromatic. Count what is left: two C=C plus the new lone pair, six π electrons delocalized over the five remaining carbons — a cyclohexadienyl anion, the mirror image of the arenium cation of electrophilic substitution. This is the Meisenheimer complex.

Step 4. Where is the negative charge? On C2, C4 and C6 — ortho and para to the attacked carbon. Two of those three carry a nitro group, so the charge runs straight out onto the nitro oxygens. That is the whole reason the intermediate is reachable.

Step 5. Eliminate. The ring’s electrons come back down onto C1 and push fluoride out; aromaticity is restored.

Product: N-methyl-2,4-dinitroaniline, with the nitrogen exactly where the fluorine was, plus F−. One product, no rearrangement. (This substrate is Sanger’s reagent, used to label the N-terminus of a peptide — and now you know why the label sticks to a nitrogen and stays put.)

Route two: benzyne, when the ring is not activated

Take chlorobenzene with no activating groups and force it with a very strong base — NaNH2 in liquid ammonia. Something quite different happens. The base removes a hydrogen ortho to the chlorine, chloride leaves, and what remains is benzyne: a benzene ring with an extra bond made from two sp² orbitals in the plane of the ring.

That bond is a terrible one. The two orbitals point away from each other rather than overlapping face to face, so benzyne is strained, extremely reactive, and lasts only long enough to be attacked.

The mechanism is elimination first, addition second — the mirror image of SNAr. And because the triple bond is symmetric, the nucleophile can attack either of its two carbons, which gives the reaction its signature: a mixture of two products, one with the nucleophile where the leaving group was and one with it on the neighboring carbon.

That scrambling is how benzyne was proved to exist. Label the carbon bearing the chlorine with 14C, run the reaction, and the label comes out split essentially 50:50 between two positions — in Roberts’ 1953 experiment, chlorobenzene-1-14C with KNH2, the two halves of the product were within a few percent of even. No direct displacement could produce that.

Two products does not always mean two equal products, though. Once the ring carries a substituent, attack at one benzyne carbon leaves a carbanion on the other, and the substituent votes on where that carbanion is allowed to sit. A withdrawing group pulls it close: 3-bromoanisole with NaNH2 gives mostly m-anisidine, because attacking the carbon further from the OCH3 leaves the charge on the carbon next to the oxygen, where the oxygen’s inductive pull stabilizes it. A plain alkyl group barely votes at all, which is why p-chlorotoluene gives a genuine mixture. It is the same question the Birch reduction asks later in this chapter: where is the carbanion happiest?

Benzyne’s other use is as a dienophile. Its in-plane π bond is so poor that it takes a Diels–Alder partner far more eagerly than an ordinary alkene: benzyne generated in the presence of furan gives the bicyclic adduct at room temperature, with no heating and no electron-withdrawing group needed to activate it. If a question makes a benzyne in the presence of a diene, that is what it is testing.

Telling them apart

SNArBenzyne
Ring needsEWG ortho or paraNothing; works on plain rings
ConditionsMild nucleophile, warmVery strong base, NaNH2
OrderAdd, then eliminateEliminate, then add
IntermediateMeisenheimer complexBenzyne
Best halideF, by a wide marginAll work; rates differ, and an ortho H is the real requirement
ProductOne, at the original positionTwo, at adjacent positions
Three traps, in the order people fall into them. First, checking that a nitro group is present rather than checking where it is — meta fails. Second, applying the leaving-group order you learned for SN1 and SN2; here it inverts, and F wins. Third, assuming that any unactivated aryl halide plus NaNH2 makes a benzyne — if both positions ortho to the halide are substituted there is no hydrogen to remove and nothing happens at all.

Read the substrate first. Nitro groups ortho or para to a halide mean SNAr; a bare aryl halide plus NaNH2 means benzyne; and a substrate with no hydrogen ortho to the halide cannot go through benzyne at all, because there is nothing to eliminate.

What carries forward

Two ways to put a nucleophile on a ring, and they are opposites. SNAr is addition–elimination through a stabilized anion, needs withdrawing groups in the right places, and gives one product. Benzyne is elimination–addition through a strained intermediate, needs no activation and a brutal base, and gives two.
