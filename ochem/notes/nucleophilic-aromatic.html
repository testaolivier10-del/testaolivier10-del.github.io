Aromatic Follow-Through · Section 98 of 121

SNAr and benzyne

Practice this — interactive lesson

Everything the course has done with a benzene ring so far runs one way. The ring is electron-rich, so it attacks an electrophile, then loses H+ and becomes aromatic again. That is Electrophilic aromatic substitution.

Now try the reverse on chlorobenzene. Warm it with methoxide, CH3O−, a nucleophile that easily displaces chloride from chloromethane. Nothing happens. Chlorobenzene is an aryl halide: a halogen bonded directly to a carbon of an aromatic ring. Neither of the substitutions you know works on it.

SN2 fails for a geometric reason. The nucleophile has to attack C1 from the side opposite the C–Cl bond, and on a ring that side points into the middle of the ring. To get there, the nucleophile would have to pass through the ring. C1 would also have to turn inside out, and a carbon held flat in a ring cannot.

SN1 fails because the cation it needs is badly unstable. If chloride left on its own, C1 would keep an empty sp² orbital lying in the plane of the ring. The ring’s π electrons are in p orbitals at right angles to that plane, so they cannot spread into the empty orbital, and nothing stabilizes the positive charge.

There are still two classic ways to put a nucleophile on an aromatic ring, and what else the ring carries decides between them. A third, newer route, Palladium cross-coupling, joins a carbon group to an unactivated aryl halide through a metal catalyst, with neither a nitro group nor a strong base.

Route one: SNAr, when the ring is electron-poor

Start with 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene: chlorine on C1, and nitro groups (NO2) on C2 and C4. Warm it with methoxide, and OCH3 replaces the chlorine to give 1-methoxy-2,4-dinitrobenzene. The two nitro groups make the difference. They are strong electron-withdrawing groups, as in Ortho/meta/para directing effects, and they leave the ring electron-poor.

This reaction is SNAr, which stands for nucleophilic substitution at an aromatic carbon. It takes two steps. First, methoxide adds to C1, the carbon that carries the chlorine. C1 now holds both OCH3 and Cl, so it is sp³, and the ring is no longer aromatic. The negatively charged intermediate this makes is a Meisenheimer complex.

Second, the lone pair on C2 moves back in to re-form the C1=C2 double bond. Chloride leaves, and the ring is aromatic again.

SNAr is addition first, elimination second. That is the order of Nucleophilic acyl substitution, where the nucleophile adds to C=O before the leaving group goes. It is not how SN1 or SN2 run: SN1 loses the leaving group first, and SN2 makes one bond and breaks the other in a single step.

The first step is the slow one, because it costs the ring its aromaticity. The nitro groups pay part of that cost. Follow the lone pair from C2 around the ring and it can sit on C2, C4 or C6, the carbons ortho and para to C1. It never sits on C3 or C5.

In 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, both nitro groups sit on carbons that carry the charge. From there the charge moves onto a nitro oxygen, and an electronegative oxygen holds a negative charge far better than carbon does. Spreading the charge this way is what makes the Meisenheimer complex reachable.

Count the electrons in the complex. Because C1 is sp³, the ring of p orbitals is broken, and the other five carbons share six π electrons: two C=C bonds and the lone pair. That makes a cyclohexadienyl anion. It has the same shape as the arenium ion of electrophilic substitution, with the opposite charge. EAS runs through a cation that electron donors stabilize, and SNAr runs through an anion that withdrawing groups stabilize.

The mechanism predicts four things you can test.

The withdrawing group must be ortho or para to the leaving group. Compare 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene with 1-chloro-3-nitrobenzene. In the first, the nitro group is on C4, a carbon the charge visits, so the charge can reach its oxygens. In the second, it is on C3, which the charge never visits. A meta nitro group still pulls electrons through the σ bonds (induction), and that speeds the reaction up measurably. Induction is the small effect, though, far too small to make SNAr practical. The reaction needs resonance.

More withdrawing groups make it faster. One ortho or para nitro group is enough with a good nucleophile and heat. Two make it an easy reaction. Three, as in 2-chloro-1,3,5-trinitrobenzene (picryl chloride), make it nearly instant. Exam questions usually use two, which is why 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene keeps coming up. Nitro is the strongest common activating group; cyano (C≡N) and carbonyl groups also work, less strongly.

Fluorine is the best leaving group here. 1-Fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene reacts far faster than its chloro partner. The order usually given is F > Cl > Br > I, the reverse of SN1 and SN2. The reason is which step is slow. The slow step, which sets the rate, is the nucleophile adding (see Energy diagrams), and the C–X bond does not break in it. So bond strength barely matters. What matters is how hard the halogen pulls electrons away from C1. Fluorine, the most electronegative, pulls hardest: it makes C1 the most electrophilic, and it helps hold the negative charge that the attack creates.

No rearrangement. The nucleophile arrives exactly where the leaving group was, so SNAr gives one product.

Worked example — 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene + CH3NH2

Step 1. Read the ring. The nitro groups are on C2 and C4, one ortho and one para to the fluorine. Both can take charge from the carbon under attack, so SNAr is on.

Step 2. Check the leaving group. Fluorine is no problem. It is the best leaving group in SNAr, because the C–F bond does not break in the slow step.

Step 3. Add. The lone pair on the nitrogen of methylamine attacks C1. C1 becomes sp³ and holds both F and N, so the ring is no longer aromatic. The nitrogen now has four bonds and a positive charge, and the ring carries the negative charge.

Step 4. Place the negative charge. It sits on C2, C4 and C6. Two of those carry a nitro group, so the charge spreads onto the nitro oxygens.

Step 5. Eliminate. The lone pair comes back into the ring, fluoride leaves, and the ring is aromatic again.

Step 6. Lose H+. The nitrogen still carries two hydrogens and a positive charge. A second molecule of methylamine removes one of those hydrogens as H+.

Product: N-methyl-2,4-dinitroaniline, with the nitrogen exactly where the fluorine was. One product, no rearrangement.

1-Fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene is Sanger’s reagent. As a preview of Peptides and proteins: it reacts the same way with the free NH2 at one end of a protein chain. The new bond from that nitrogen to the ring survives the acid that later breaks the chain into amino acids, so the label stays on the end amino acid and identifies it.

Route two: benzyne, when the ring is not activated

Chlorobenzene has no nitro groups, so SNAr is out. Treat it with sodium amide, NaNH2, in liquid ammonia (ammonia cooled below its boiling point, −33 °C), and it does react. The product is aniline, C6H5NH2. The amide ion, NH2−, is a very strong base. It shares a name with the amide group of Esters & amides and nothing else.

This mechanism starts with the base. Amide removes the hydrogen on C2, the carbon next to the chlorine. That leaves an aryl anion: a lone pair and a negative charge on a ring carbon, in the sp² orbital the C–H bond used. The lone pair then pushes chloride off C1. What remains is benzyne, a benzene ring with an extra bond between C1 and C2, drawn as a triple bond.

Benzyne does not last. Amide adds to one end of the extra bond, which leaves a lone pair on the other end. That aryl anion takes H+ from an ammonia molecule, and the product is aniline.

Benzyne is elimination first, addition second: the reverse of SNAr.

The extra bond is a poor one. Each of its two carbons supplies an sp² orbital that lies in the plane of the ring and points outward, away from the other. The two orbitals overlap only weakly, side on, so benzyne is strained, extremely reactive, and lasts only until something attacks it.

The extra bond is also not part of the aromatic system. The ring’s six π electrons stay in p orbitals above and below the plane, at right angles to the new bond, so benzyne is still aromatic. Its reactivity comes from the weak in-plane bond alone.

The extra bond has a carbon at each end, and in the benzyne from chlorobenzene the two ends are equivalent. Amide can add to either one. That gives the reaction its signature: two products, one with the nucleophile where the leaving group was and one with it on the neighboring carbon.

This scrambling is how benzyne was shown to exist. In 1953, Roberts made chlorobenzene with a 14C atom at C1. (14C is a radioactive form of carbon, so its position in a product can be found.) He treated it with potassium amide, KNH2, in liquid ammonia. The aniline came out with its NH2 on the labeled carbon in half the molecules and on the carbon next door in the other half, within a few percent of even. No direct displacement can put the nucleophile on a carbon that never carried the leaving group.

Worked example — 2-chlorotoluene + NaNH2

Step 1. Find the hydrogens next to the halogen. Toluene is methylbenzene, so 2-chlorotoluene has CH3 on C1 and Cl on C2. Of the two carbons beside C2, C1 carries the methyl, so only C3 has a hydrogen to lose.

Step 2. Place the benzyne. Amide removes the hydrogen from C3, chloride leaves from C2, and the extra bond forms between C2 and C3.

Step 3. Add at both ends. Amide adding at C2 puts NH2 where the chlorine was and gives 2-methylaniline. Adding at C3 gives 3-methylaniline. These methylanilines also go by the common names o-toluidine and m-toluidine.

Two products does not always mean two equal amounts. Once the ring carries a substituent, adding to one end of the benzyne leaves a carbanion, a carbon with a negative charge, on the other end. The substituent can favor one of those two positions for the charge.

A methyl group barely chooses. 4-Chlorotoluene gives a benzyne between C3 and C4, and amide adds at both ends, so the product is a real mixture of 4-methylaniline and 3-methylaniline.

An oxygen does choose. Anisole is methoxybenzene, so 3-bromoanisole has OCH3 on C1 and Br on C3. The base removes the hydrogen on C2, between the two groups, because both pull electrons from it and make it the most acidic. The benzyne forms between C2 and C3. Amide then adds to C3, the end further from the oxygen. That leaves the negative charge on C2, right beside the carbon that carries the oxygen, where the oxygen’s inductive pull stabilizes it. The main product is 3-methoxyaniline (m-anisidine).

The oxygen’s lone pairs play no part here, even though OCH3 donates electrons to the ring by resonance in EAS. The carbanion’s lone pair sits in an sp² orbital in the ring plane, at right angles to the π system, so resonance cannot reach it. Only the inductive pull counts. Calculations describe the same result in a second way: the oxygen bends the benzyne slightly, which leaves C3 more open to attack. Both pictures predict the same product.

Asking where a carbanion sits most comfortably will come back in Birch reduction, later in this chapter.

Benzyne has one more use, as a dienophile: the two-carbon partner in a Diels–Alder reaction. Its weak in-plane bond reacts far more readily than an ordinary alkene C=C. Benzyne made in the presence of furan gives the bicyclic adduct at room temperature, with no heating and no withdrawing group to activate it. The furan oxygen ends up as a one-atom bridge across the new ring. When a question makes benzyne with a diene present, this is the reaction it is testing.

Telling them apart

Two questions sort any aryl halide. Ask them in this order.

SNArBenzyne
Ring needsEWG ortho or paraNo activating group; an H next to the halogen
ConditionsGood nucleophile (CH3O−, an amine), often warmVery strong base, NaNH2
OrderAdd, then eliminateEliminate, then add
IntermediateMeisenheimer complexBenzyne
Best halideF, by a wide marginAll work; rates differ, and the H next to the halogen is the real requirement
ProductOne, at the original positionTwo, at neighboring positions
Three traps, in the order people fall into them. First, checking that a nitro group is present rather than where it is: a meta nitro group fails. Second, carrying over the leaving-group order from SN1 and SN2: here it inverts, and F wins. Third, assuming any unactivated aryl halide plus NaNH2 makes benzyne: if both carbons next to the halogen carry substituents, there is no hydrogen to remove, and nothing happens at all.

What carries forward

There are two ways to put a nucleophile on a ring, and they are opposites. SNAr is addition then elimination through a stabilized anion. It needs withdrawing groups in the right places and gives one product. Benzyne is elimination then addition through a strained intermediate. It needs no activation, only a very strong base, and gives two products.
