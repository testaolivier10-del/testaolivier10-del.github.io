Carbonyl Chemistry · Section 69 of 121

Oxidizing an aldehyde

Practice this — interactive lesson

An aldehyde is the one carbonyl in this chapter that is still going somewhere. Leave it in a flask with almost any oxidant — leave it in a flask with air — and it climbs one more rung to the carboxylic acid. A ketone, sitting at the same oxidation level, does nothing at all.

The difference is one hydrogen

Oxidation at a carbon means replacing a C–H bond with a bond to oxygen. An aldehyde's carbonyl carbon still carries a hydrogen, so there is something to take. A ketone's carries two carbons instead, so oxidizing it would mean breaking a C–C bond — which is why ordinary oxidants leave ketones untouched.

It is the same hydrogen count the next chapter will use to decide which alcohols can be oxidized and how many times, applied one rung higher up. Primary alcohols have two hydrogens on the carbon and go twice; secondary have one and go once; tertiary have none and do not go at all.

What is actually oxidized is the hydrate

A carbonyl carbon has no hydroxyl for an oxidant to grip. The hydrate does. The hydrates and cyanohydrins section earlier in this chapter showed water adding across the C=O to give a gem-diol, and that gem-diol has an OH and a hydrogen on the same carbon — which is to say it is an alcohol, and it is oxidized exactly as a primary alcohol is.

This is the mechanism behind a reagent rule the next chapter will state without explaining. Jones reagent, Cr(VI) in aqueous acid, carries a primary alcohol past the aldehyde to the carboxylic acid because the water in the flask keeps regenerating the hydrate. PCC in anhydrous dichloromethane stops at the aldehyde because there is no water, therefore no hydrate, therefore nothing with an O–H left to attack.

The reagents you would actually use

The tests below are diagnostics, not preparations, so it is worth saying plainly what goes over the arrow when the question is “butanal → butanoic acid”. Three answers are standard.

  • KMnO4, usually warm and basic with an acidic workup. Cheap and thorough — and indiscriminate, so it will also cleave an alkene or oxidize a benzylic position elsewhere in the molecule.
  • Jones reagent (CrO3 in aqueous sulfuric acid, or Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4). The workhorse, and the same reagent Oxidation & Reduction uses to take a primary alcohol the whole way — because, as the section above argues, it is the hydrate it attacks either time.
  • Ag2O in aqueous base, the preparative relative of Tollens'. Expensive, but mild enough to leave alkenes, alcohols and acid-sensitive groups alone, which is why it is the one chosen when the rest of the molecule is delicate.

The choice between them is not about whether the aldehyde will oxidize — it will, under all three — but about what else in the molecule you are willing to lose.

Tollens' reagent and the silver mirror

Tollens' reagent is the diamminesilver(I) ion, Ag(NH3)2+, in basic solution. The aldehyde is oxidized to the carboxylate, and the silver(I) is reduced to metallic silver, which plates out on clean glass as a silver mirror. That visible deposit is the whole point.

Tollens' is a very mild oxidant, and the things it does not do are what make it useful. It leaves alcohols alone and it leaves alkenes alone. A reagent that attacks one functional group and ignores its neighbors is a test rather than a synthesis — nobody makes a carboxylic acid this way, because silver is expensive.

Fehling's and Benedict's solutions

Both use Cu(II) held in solution by a chelating ligand under basic conditions, and both signal a positive result by dropping a brick-red precipitate of Cu2O out of a deep blue solution.

Why glucose tests positive and sucrose does not

Glucose is drawn as a ring, and a ring has no aldehyde in it. It tests positive anyway, because the ring is a hemiacetal and hemiacetals open. The equilibrium holds only a trace of the open-chain aldehyde at any moment, but it delivers that trace continuously, and the oxidant consumes it as fast as it appears. Sugars that behave this way are called reducing sugars — named for what they do to the metal, not for what happens to them.

A ketose tests positive too, which is worth knowing because it looks like it should not. Fructose has no aldehyde at any point in the equilibrium, and Benedict's finds it anyway: these reagents are basic, and base isomerizes a ketose to the corresponding aldose through an enediol. So the property that actually decides a positive test is a free anomeric carbon, not an aldehyde group — which is how the carbohydrate section defines a reducing sugar, and it is the more general statement.

Sucrose is negative, because its glycosidic bond ties up both anomeric carbons; neither ring can open. Lactose and maltose each leave one anomeric carbon free, so both are positive. The carbohydrate section develops that bookkeeping properly; here it is the hydrate argument again, with a hemiacetal in place of the gem-diol.

Worked example — reading a pair of test results

An unknown gives a silver mirror with Tollens' and no brick-red precipitate with Fehling's. What can you conclude, and what would you do next?

Step 1 — what a positive Tollens' rules in. Tollens' oxidizes a carbon that carries both an oxygen and a hydrogen. An aldehyde qualifies; so does anything in equilibrium with one, which means a hemiacetal — a sugar with a free anomeric carbon. A ketone does not.

Step 2 — what a negative Fehling's would normally mean. On its own, no aldehyde. That contradicts step 1, so one of the two tests must have a blind spot — and Fehling's has exactly one: it is unreliable with aromatic aldehydes.

Step 3 — reconcile. The pair is not a contradiction but a diagnosis. An aromatic aldehyde is positive to Tollens' and negative to Fehling's, and benzaldehyde is the type case.

Step 4 — the confirmation. Do not settle it with another copper reagent; take the compound up to the acid with Ag2O and identify the product — benzoic acid, if the reading is right — by its melting point. The trap the question is setting is treating a negative result from a reagent with a known blind spot as evidence of absence.

The consequence on the shelf

Aldehydes oxidize in air on standing, by a radical chain that needs no reagent at all. An old bottle of benzaldehyde grows crystals of benzoic acid around the cap, and that contamination is a routine cause of unexplained failures. Aldehydes are therefore stored under inert gas, and are often made immediately before use.

The chain is worth writing out, because it explains why the aldehyde and nothing else in the bottle. The bond that breaks is the aldehyde C–H, and it is the weakest C–H in the molecule at about 88 kcal/mol, because the radical left behind is an acyl radical, stabilized by the carbonyl sitting right next to it. Three steps, in the pattern of the radical chains from How Reactions Happen:

  • Initiation / abstraction. Something — light, a trace metal, an existing radical — takes that aldehyde hydrogen, leaving RC•=O.
  • Oxygen addition. O2 is itself a diradical and adds straight to the acyl radical, giving an acylperoxy radical RC(=O)OO•.
  • Propagation. That peroxy radical takes the aldehyde hydrogen from a second molecule, giving a peroxyacid RCO3H and a fresh acyl radical — so the chain carries on. The peroxyacid then oxidizes another aldehyde to the acid, and two molecules of carboxylic acid come out of it.

Which is the same “one hydrogen” theme as the rest of the section, one level deeper: a ketone has no such C–H to abstract, so it sits in its bottle indefinitely.

A ketone can be oxidized, but only by breaking a C–C bond, and both routes this course covers do exactly that. The Baeyer–Villiger inserts an oxygen into one of those bonds to give an ester. Hot, concentrated KMnO4 cleaves straight through. Neither is something that happens by accident in a bottle.

What carries forward

Aldehyde to carboxylic acid is a one-step move in any synthesis, and the reagent question is only ever how much else in the molecule you are willing to lose. Run it in reverse and the same fact becomes a warning: any route that makes an aldehyde and leaves it sitting has a problem, because air will finish the job for you.
