E1 starts exactly like SN1

E1 begins with the same rate-determining ionization step as SN1: the leaving group departs on its own, forming a flat, sp² carbocation. What happens next is different — instead of a nucleophile attacking the cationic carbon (SN1), a weak base removes a hydrogen from a beta carbon (one bond away from the cation), and the C–H bond's electrons flow in to form a new pi bond.

Zaitsev's rule

When more than one beta-hydrogen is available, removing different ones gives different alkenes. Zaitsev's rule: the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product, for the same hyperconjugation reasons that make more substituted carbocations more stable. Example: ionizing 2-bromo-2-methylbutane gives a cation with two kinds of beta-hydrogen available — removing one from the ethyl CH₂ gives the trisubstituted 2-methyl-2-butene (major), while removing one from a methyl gives the disubstituted 2-methyl-1-butene (minor).

This is the unimolecular, non-stereospecific counterpart to E2's anti-periplanar, bimolecular mechanism (Module 4/6). E1 doesn't require any specific geometry between the leaving group and the beta-hydrogen, because the leaving group is already long gone by the time the hydrogen is removed.

Rate law and the SN1 connection

E1 is unimolecular: rate = k[substrate] (the base's concentration doesn't appear). Because E1 shares SN1's exact carbocation intermediate, the two pathways happen under identical conditions (weak nucleophile/base, polar protic solvent, substrate that ionizes easily) and almost always compete, producing a mixture of substitution and elimination products. Higher temperature favors E1 over SN1 (elimination has a greater increase in entropy, forming two molecules from one), and more steric crowding around the cation also favors elimination.
