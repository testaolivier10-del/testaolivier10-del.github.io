Substitution & Elimination · Section 47 of 121

E1

Practice this — interactive lesson

E1 is the fourth corner of this chapter's square: unimolecular like SN1, eliminating like E2. It shares SN1's first step exactly, which means the two always occur together and always give a mixture — a fact that makes E1 less useful synthetically than the other three, and more important to recognize when it is happening.

E1 starts exactly like SN1

E1 begins with the same rate-determining ionization as SN1: the leaving group departs unassisted, giving a flat, sp²-hybridized carbocation. Everything said about carbocation stability in the SN1 section applies here unchanged — 3° > 2° > 1°, with allylic and benzylic cations stabilized by resonance.

What differs is the second step. Instead of a nucleophile attacking the cationic carbon, a weak base removes a hydrogen from a beta carbon, and the electrons from that C–H bond flow in to form a new pi bond with the cationic carbon. Two arrows in that step: base's lone pair to the hydrogen, and the C–H bond into the C–C bond.

The base is usually something weak that happens to be present — the solvent, or the departed leaving group. E1 does not need a strong base, because the cation is so electron-poor that even water will remove a beta proton from it.

E1 requires no particular geometry between the leaving group and the beta hydrogen, because the leaving group is long gone by the time the hydrogen is removed. This is the sharpest mechanistic contrast with E2, which cannot proceed at all without an anti-periplanar arrangement. E1 is therefore not stereospecific — different diastereomers of the same substrate converge on the same product mixture.

Zaitsev's rule

When several beta hydrogens are available, removing different ones gives different alkenes, and Zaitsev's rule applies cleanly here: the more substituted alkene is the major product.

"More substituted" means one specific count, and it is worth doing slowly the first time, because the whole rule depends on it. Look at the two carbons joined by the double bond and count how many non-hydrogen groups are attached to them, between them — nothing else on the molecule matters. CH₂=CH–CH₂CH₃ has one (the ethyl), so it is monosubstituted. CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ has two, one on each alkene carbon: disubstituted. (CH₃)₂C=CH–CH₃ has three: trisubstituted. (CH₃)₂C=C(CH₃)₂ has four: tetrasubstituted, the maximum, since each alkene carbon has only two places left after the double bond. The propyl group hanging off the far end of a chain counts for nothing — it is not on an alkene carbon.

The reason is alkene stability, and it is the same hyperconjugation argument that stabilizes carbocations — more alkyl groups on the double bond means more C–H bonds able to donate into the pi system. The whole ladder is narrower than students expect: from heats of hydrogenation, a tetrasubstituted alkene is about 4 kcal/mol more stable than a monosubstituted one, and each rung is worth roughly a kcal. Small differences, but they are the differences a product ratio is made of.

Unlike E2, E1 gives Zaitsev products essentially regardless of the base, because the base is weak and small by definition — there is no bulky-base option that would push toward the less substituted alkene instead. Where E2's product can be steered, E1's cannot.

Worked example — 2-bromo-2-methylbutane in warm ethanol

Step 1 (slow): bromide ionizes off, giving the tertiary cation (CH₃)₂C⁺–CH₂CH₃.

Step 2 (fast): ethanol removes a beta hydrogen. Two choices are available.

Two arrows in this step, every time: one from an ethanol oxygen lone pair to the beta hydrogen, and one from that C–H bond into the gap between the beta carbon and the cationic carbon, where it becomes the π bond. The ethanol leaves as EtOH₂⁺.

Taking a hydrogen from the ethyl CH₂ gives 2-methyl-2-butene, trisubstituted — the Zaitsev major product.

Taking one from a methyl gives 2-methyl-1-butene, disubstituted — the minor product.

And running alongside both, ethanol can attack the cation directly, giving the SN1 ether. All three products come out of the same flask.

Rate law and the SN1 connection

E1 is unimolecular: rate = k[substrate]. The base's concentration does not appear, because the base is not involved until after the rate-determining step.

Because E1 and SN1 share the identical carbocation intermediate, they occur under identical conditions — weak nucleophile and weak base, polar protic solvent, a substrate that ionizes readily — and they essentially always compete. Any reaction you would call SN1 is producing some E1 product too, and vice versa. This is why neither is a reaction you would choose for a synthesis where yield matters.

Two levers shift the balance. Higher temperature favors elimination, since it makes two molecules from one and so gains entropy, and the TΔS term grows with T. More substitution around the cation also favors elimination, both because the cationic carbon is crowded and because the resulting alkene is more substituted and more stable.

Carbocations rearrange, so E1 products can have a skeleton you did not expect. Check every E1 intermediate for a hydride or methyl shift that would give a more stable cation, exactly as in SN1 — then eliminate from the rearranged cation. This is the classic explanation for the "wrong" alkene appearing as a major product in an acid-catalyzed dehydration, and it is a difference from E2, which has no intermediate and therefore never rearranges.
Worked example — an E1 that rearranges first

Warm 3-bromo-2,2-dimethylbutane, (CH₃)₃C–CHBr–CH₃, in aqueous ethanol.

Step 1. Bromide leaves, giving a secondary cation on C3.

Step 2 — check for a shift before doing anything else. The carbon next door is quaternary: it carries three methyls and no hydrogen, so there is no hydride to move. But a whole methyl group can move instead, with its bonding pair, exactly as a hydride does — and doing so puts the charge on a carbon bearing two methyls and the rest of the chain, i.e. a tertiary cation. The arrow starts on the C–CH₃ bond and ends on the cationic carbon.

Step 3. Now eliminate from the rearranged cation, (CH₃)₂C⁺–CH(CH₃)₂. Its beta hydrogens sit in two places: on the two methyls, which would give the disubstituted 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene, and on the single C–H of the neighboring carbon, which gives the tetrasubstituted 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene. Zaitsev picks the second.

The point. The alkene you get has its double bond in a position you cannot reach by eliminating from the original C–Br carbon at all. When a cation can rearrange, do the rearrangement first and apply Zaitsev to the rearranged cation — applying Zaitsev to the original skeleton gives the wrong answer with complete confidence.

Where E1 actually shows up

Although it is rarely chosen deliberately with an alkyl halide, E1 is the mechanism of one genuinely important transformation: acid-catalyzed dehydration of alcohols. Strong acid protonates the OH, converting a hopeless leaving group into water; water leaves to give a carbocation; and a weak base removes a beta proton to give the alkene. Every step is E1. Every one of those steps is reversible — protonating an alkene to give a carbocation is exactly step three run backwards, and it is how alkene hydration works — so dehydration is an equilibrium, not a one-way reaction. That is why the practical recipe matters: the alkene is the lowest-boiling thing in the flask, so running the reaction hot and distilling the alkene off as it forms removes a product, pulls the equilibrium over by Le Châtelier, and makes the reaction preparatively useful — a reaction covered properly in Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry.

What carries forward

E1 is the mechanism of alcohol dehydration in Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry, and its reverse — protonating an alkene to give a carbocation — is the mechanism of Markovnikov addition in Alkenes & Alkynes. Recognizing that the same carbocation sits at the center of SN1, E1, alkene addition and alcohol dehydration is what turns four separate reactions into one idea.
