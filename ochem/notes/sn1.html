The key difference from SN2

In SN2, the nucleophile attacks and kicks out the leaving group in one step. In SN1, the leaving group departs first, on its own — before any nucleophile is involved. That first step (ionization) is slow and rate-determining, leaving behind a carbocation: flat, sp² hybridized, and missing an electron pair.

Why the cation is flat — and why that matters

The carbocation carbon is sp² and trigonal planar, with an empty p orbital sticking out equally on both sides of that plane. Nothing blocks either face, so a nucleophile (e.g. water) attacks the front and back at roughly equal rates.

If the original carbon was a stereocenter, attack from both faces gives a racemic mixture (both enantiomers, roughly 50/50) — unlike SN2's clean, single inversion. This loss of stereochemical information is a direct, testable signature of SN1.

What controls the rate: carbocation stability

Since ionization (forming the cation) is the slow, rate-determining step, anything that stabilizes the carbocation speeds up SN1. More alkyl groups around the positively charged carbon donate electron density into its empty p orbital, so tertiary substrates ionize fastest, secondary slower, and primary essentially not at all via SN1.

That donation has a name and a concrete picture worth carrying for the rest of the course: hyperconjugation. An alkyl group attached to the cationic carbon brings its own C–H sigma bonds with it. When one of those C–H bonds happens to line up parallel with the empty p orbital, the pair of electrons in it can spill over into that empty orbital — not a full bond, just a partial sharing that spreads the positive charge off the cationic carbon and onto the neighboring C–H bonds. More alkyl groups means more C–H bonds positioned to do this, which means more spreading, which means a more stable cation. That is the whole mechanism behind 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl. This is the opposite preference from SN2, which favors unhindered primary carbons because a crowded carbon blocks backside attack.

Worked example

tert-Butyl bromide, (CH₃)₃C–Br, ionizes to the tertiary (CH₃)₃C⁺ cation plus Br⁻, then water attacks either face to eventually give the alcohol.

Rate law and solvent effects

SN1 is unimolecular: rate = k[substrate] (nucleophile concentration doesn't appear — contrast with SN2's rate = k[Nu][substrate]). Favored by polar protic solvents (water, alcohols), which stabilize the developing carbocation and departing anion via solvation — the opposite preference from SN2.
