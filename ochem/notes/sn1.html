Substitution & Elimination · Section 45 of 121

SN1

Practice this — interactive lesson

SN1 reaches the same kind of product as SN2 — one group on carbon swapped for another — by a completely different route, and every observable consequence differs: the rate law, the stereochemistry, which substrates work, which solvents help. Learning SN1 is largely a matter of learning how each of those contrasts with SN2, and why.

The key difference from SN2

In SN2 the nucleophile attacks and expels the leaving group in one motion. In SN1 the leaving group departs first, unassisted, before any nucleophile is involved. That ionization step is slow and rate-determining, and it leaves behind a carbocation: flat, sp²-hybridized, with an empty p orbital and only six valence electrons.

The nucleophile arrives afterwards, in a fast second step. A third step — deprotonation — is often needed if the nucleophile was neutral, as when water attacks and must lose a proton to give the alcohol.

Because the nucleophile appears only after the slow step, it does not appear in the rate law at all: rate = k[substrate]. Unimolecular, hence the 1. Doubling the nucleophile concentration does nothing to the rate, which is the cleanest experimental distinction between the two mechanisms.

Why the cation is flat — and why that matters

The shape and the empty orbital are set out in the Carbocations section of How Reactions Happen. All SN1 needs from it is the one consequence drawn above: nothing blocks either face of the intermediate, so nothing chooses between them.

If the original carbon was a stereocenter, attack from both faces gives a racemic mixture — roughly 50:50, and optically inactive. This total loss of stereochemical information is the diagnostic signature of SN1, exactly as clean inversion is the signature of SN2. In practice the mixture often shows slight excess inversion (typically 55:45 up to 70:30), because the departing leaving group lingers briefly as an ion pair and partially shields the face it left from. That detail is evidence for the mechanism rather than an exception to it.

What controls the rate: carbocation stability

Since ionization is rate-determining, the step whose rate you are measuring is the step that makes the cation — so SN1's substrate ordering is nothing but the carbocation stability ladder read as a rate. It runs 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl, and the differences are enormous: in the standard solvolysis comparison (aqueous ethanol or formic acid) a tertiary substrate reacts on the order of 10⁶ times faster than methyl.

Why the ladder runs that way — hyperconjugation, induction, and the counting behind them — belongs to the Carbocations section of How Reactions Happen, and is assumed here. What is specific to SN1 is the link itself: no other mechanism in this chapter has a rate law that reports a cation's stability so directly.

Note that this is the exact opposite preference from SN2, which wants an uncrowded carbon. The same structural feature — alkyl substitution — accelerates one mechanism and shuts down the other, which is why substrate class is the first thing you check in the decision framework.

The exceptions the ordering hides

Delocalization outranks alkyl substitution, and the SN1 consequence is an exception worth attaching to the rule the moment you learn it. A primary benzylic cation is about as stable as a tertiary alkyl one, so benzyl chloride solvolyzes readily despite being formally primary. "No primary SN1" means no simple primary.

Delocalization has a second consequence that is easy to miss: if the charge is spread over two carbons, the nucleophile can attack at either of them, and usually does. Ionize 3-chlorobut-1-ene, CH₂=CH–CHCl–CH₃, and the cation is the resonance hybrid CH₂=CH–CH⁺–CH₃ ↔ ⁺CH₂–CH=CH–CH₃. Water attacking the carbon that held the chlorine gives but-3-en-2-ol; water attacking the far end gives but-2-en-1-ol, and the double bond has moved. Both are ordinary SN1 products of the same cation. This is called allylic rearrangement, and it is a different phenomenon from the hydride shift below — nothing migrated, the charge was simply in two places at once.

The exception runs the other way too. Vinyl and aryl cations do not form at all — for a geometric reason set out in the Carbocations section — so vinyl and aryl halides are inert to SN1 exactly as they are to SN2. Between them, those two facts are why an aryl halide is unreactive toward simple substitution and needs entirely different chemistry.

Worked example 1 — tert-butyl bromide in water, one step at a time

Step 1 (slow, rate-determining). One arrow only: tail on the C–Br bond, head onto bromine. Nothing attacks; the bond simply comes apart. Bromine leaves with both electrons and so carries the charge: Br⁻, with four lone pairs. The carbon it left is now (CH₃)₃C⁺ — three bonds, no lone pair, six valence electrons, a full positive charge, flat and sp².

Step 2 (fast). Water attacks the empty p orbital. One arrow: tail on an oxygen lone pair, head on the cationic carbon. Oxygen started neutral with two lone pairs and has just spent one making a bond, so it is now positive: the product of this step is the oxonium ion (CH₃)₃C–OH₂⁺, an alcohol with an extra proton on it. Writing the neutral alcohol here is the commonest slip in the whole mechanism — count the bonds on oxygen and you will catch it.

Step 3 (fast). A second water molecule takes that proton off. Two arrows: a lone pair on the new water to the O–H hydrogen, and the O–H bond back onto the oxonium oxygen. Out come (CH₃)₃C–OH, tert-butanol, and H₃O⁺.

Three steps, one of which matters for the rate. When the solvent is also the nucleophile, as here, the reaction is called solvolysis — which is the normal way SN1 is run, since a weak nucleophile is required anyway.

Worked example 2 — what racemization actually looks like

Take a single enantiomer: (R)-3-bromo-3-methylhexane, warmed in aqueous acetone. The stereocenter is C3, carrying bromine, a methyl, an ethyl and a propyl — four different groups, so the starting material is optically active and rotates plane-polarized light.

Ionize. Bromide leaves. The cation is tertiary and therefore flat: the carbon that was a stereocenter now has three groups in one plane and an empty p orbital sticking out on both sides of it. The handedness is gone at this point — not scrambled later, gone here.

Attack. Water arrives. It can come in from the top face or the bottom face, and nothing distinguishes them, so it does both at roughly equal rates.

Products. After deprotonation, (R)- and (S)-3-methylhexan-3-ol in about equal amounts — a racemic mixture, optically inactive, from an optically active starting material. Put a substrate that does react by SN2 through the same test — a secondary halide with a good nucleophile — and the product is a single, inverted enantiomer instead. That difference is not a detail; it is the experiment that tells the two mechanisms apart.

In practice the mixture is usually not exactly 50:50 but slightly skewed toward inversion, for the ion-pair reason in the box above.

Rearrangements: the complication

The 1,2-shifts themselves — hydride, methyl, and the rule that one happens whenever it would give a more stable cation — are taught in the Carbocations section. What they do to SN1 is the part that costs marks here: the cation this mechanism makes has a lifetime, and a shift is faster than the nucleophile's arrival.

The consequence is that SN1 can give a product whose skeleton differs from anything you would predict by just swapping the leaving group. The standard case is worth working through hydrogen by hydrogen.

Worked example 3 — 3-methylbutan-2-ol + HBr, and which H moves where

The substrate is CH₃–CH(OH)–CH(CH₃)–CH₃. Number the chain: the OH is on C2, and C3 carries a methyl branch and one hydrogen.

Step 1. HBr protonates the OH. A hydroxide is a hopeless leaving group; an oxonium, –OH₂⁺, leaves as water, which is a fine one. This is the standard trick for making an alcohol react at all.

Step 2 (slow). Water leaves, giving a secondary cation at C2.

Step 3 — the shift. Screen the neighbors by what each shift would leave behind. C3 carries a hydrogen, and moving it to C2 with its bonding pair puts the charge on C3, which holds two methyls and the rest of the chain — a tertiary cation, so that is the one that happens. The arrow starts on the C3–H bond and ends on C2. C1 is a methyl bonded straight onto the cation and its hydrogens could migrate, but that would move the charge to a primary carbon, so they do not. C4 is two carbons away and out of range.

Step 4. Bromide attacks the new tertiary cation at C3.

Product: 2-bromo-2-methylbutane, (CH₃)₂CBr–CH₂CH₃ — not the 2-bromo-3-methylbutane you get by simply swapping OH for Br. The skeleton is rearranged, and a student who never drew the cation has no way to see it coming.

Every SN1 answer needs a rearrangement check. Draw the cation, look at its neighbors, and only then let the nucleophile in. Skipping that check is one of the commonest sources of wrong SN1 and E1 products — and the fact that the products rearrange at all is the clearest evidence that a free cation exists here, because SN2, which has no intermediate, never rearranges.

Two pieces of evidence that the cation is real

Rearrangement is one. The other two are worth naming because exam questions use the terms.

The ion pair. The instant the bond breaks, the cation and the leaving anion are still side by side in the same solvent cage, held together by nothing but electrostatics — that is an ion pair. It only lasts until the solvent gets between them, but while it does the anion partly screens the face it just left, which is why SN1 products are typically 55:45 rather than exactly 50:50 in favor of inversion. A perfectly racemic product would actually be harder to explain than a slightly skewed one.

The common-ion effect. Ionization is reversible: the cation can recapture the leaving group. So adding extra leaving-group anion to the reaction — running a solvolysis of R–Br in the presence of dissolved NaBr, say — slows the reaction down, because the added bromide pushes the first step back toward the starting material. This is the common-ion effect, and it is a sharp piece of evidence: a concerted SN2 has nothing to push back on, so it shows no such rate depression. Careful with the trap — adding an inert salt that shares no ion (NaClO₄) does the opposite and speeds an SN1 up slightly, by making the solvent better at supporting charge.

Solvent

SN1 is favored by polar protic solvents — water, alcohols, acetic acid — for two reasons at once. They stabilize the developing positive charge in the transition state, and their hydrogen bonds stabilize the departing anion. Both effects lower the barrier to the slow step.

This is the opposite of SN2's preference, and it is a useful practical lever: the same secondary substrate can be pushed toward SN2 by running it in DMSO with a strong nucleophile, or toward SN1 by running it in aqueous ethanol with none.

The four variables, summarized

SN1 is favored by: a substituted substrate (3° > 2°, plus allylic and benzylic; never a simple 1° or methyl); a weak, usually neutral nucleophile, frequently the solvent itself; a good leaving group, which matters even more here than in SN2 since it leaves without assistance; and a polar protic solvent.

That middle point deserves a sentence of its own, because "good leaving group" means something slightly different here. In SN2 the nucleophile is pushing the leaving group out, so a mediocre one can be helped along. In SN1 nothing helps: the bond breaks on its own, and the whole barrier is the cost of making that anion. So the ranking by conjugate-base stability bites harder — a tosylate (the conjugate base of a sulfonic acid, with the charge spread over three oxygens) ionizes orders of magnitude faster than the corresponding chloride, and an unactivated alcohol does not ionize at all until the OH is protonated or converted into something else first.

What carries forward

Carbocation stability and rearrangement are the recurring themes of Substitution & Elimination, Alkenes & Alkynes and Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry — they explain Markovnikov addition, alcohol dehydration, and the surprising products of several reactions in between. And E1, the elimination section of this chapter, shares SN1's first step exactly, which is why the two always occur together.
