Acids & Bases · Section 24 of 117

pKa

Practice this — interactive lesson

pKa is the single most useful number in organic chemistry. It tells you whether a base is strong enough to remove a given proton, which direction a proton transfer runs, how good a leaving group something is, and how stable an anion is. Almost every "will this reaction work?" question in the first half of the course can be answered by comparing two pKa values.

Ka and pKa

For an acid HA dissociating in solution, HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, the equilibrium constant is

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

A larger Ka means the equilibrium sits further toward the dissociated side — a stronger acid. Because Ka values for real acids span something like sixty orders of magnitude, chemists take the negative logarithm instead, exactly as they do to turn [H⁺] into pH:

pKa = −log₁₀(Ka)

The negative sign flips the direction, and this is the source of endless confusion, so state it plainly and often: lower pKa means stronger acid. A pKa of −7 is a very strong acid. A pKa of 50 is not an acid in any practical sense.

Because pKa is logarithmic, every 1 unit is a tenfold change in acid strength. Acetic acid (pKa 4.76) is roughly 10¹¹ times more acidic than ethanol (pKa 16) — a hundred billion to one, from a gap that looks like eleven small steps on a table. When comparing two acids, always convert the gap into orders of magnitude before deciding whether it is "a big difference."

Predicting which side an equilibrium favors

In any proton transfer, the equilibrium favors the side with the weaker acid — equivalently, the side whose acid has the higher pKa. The procedure is mechanical: identify the acid on the left, identify the acid that would be formed on the right (the conjugate acid of the base), compare their pKa values, and the side with the higher pKa wins.

The comparison can be made quantitative in one line, and it is worth writing down because exam questions ask for the number as often as for the direction. For any proton transfer:

Keq ≈ 10(pKa of the acid formed − pKa of the acid consumed)

Subtract in that order and the sign takes care of itself: a positive exponent means the reaction runs forward, a negative one means it does not. Acetic acid (4.76) plus hydroxide makes water (15.7), so K ≈ 1015.7−4.76 = 1011 — complete. Ethanol (16) plus hydroxide makes water (15.7), so K ≈ 10−0.3 — about one half, which is why hydroxide in water gives you an alcohol and an alkoxide in comparable amounts rather than clean deprotonation. And a rough calibration for reading the exponent: 10⁶ or better is "goes to completion", 10⁻⁶ or worse is "does not happen", and the narrow band between them is where equilibrium arrows earn their keep.

Worked example — can hydroxide deprotonate a terminal alkyne?

Left-hand acid: the alkyne C–H, pKa ≈ 25. Right-hand acid: water, formed when hydroxide takes the proton, pKa 15.7.

Water is the stronger acid (lower pKa), so the equilibrium favors the left — the side with the weaker acid. The gap is about 9 units, so the equilibrium lies roughly 10⁹ to one against the product. Hydroxide will not do this.

Now try sodium amide, NaNH₂. Right-hand acid: ammonia, pKa 38. The alkyne at 25 is the stronger acid, so the equilibrium favors the right by about 10¹³. This works, and it is exactly why NaNH₂ is the reagent specified for generating acetylides in Alkenes & Alkynes.

Worked example — separating a carboxylic acid from a phenol

Both dissolve in NaOH, so that does not separate them. Use sodium bicarbonate instead: the relevant acid on the right is carbonic acid, pKa 6.4.

The carboxylic acid (pKa 4.76) is the stronger acid, so it is deprotonated and dissolves into the aqueous layer as its carboxylate salt. The phenol (pKa 10) is the weaker acid, is not deprotonated, and stays in the organic layer. Run the separation in ether and that organic layer is the upper one, because ether is less dense than water — so the aqueous layer carrying the carboxylate is the one you run off the bottom.

This is a real laboratory separation, and it rests on nothing but two pKa comparisons.

Compare acids, not bases. The most common mistake is to reason about which base is stronger and get tangled in the double negative. Convert everything to acids and compare pKa values directly — it is the same question, asked in a form where the number points the right way.

The base's own pKa is its conjugate acid's

Bases do not have pKa values of their own; they inherit one from their conjugate acid, sometimes written pKaH. A base can remove a proton whose pKa is lower than its own pKaH. Hydroxide (pKaH 15.7) can deprotonate anything below about 15.7 and nothing much above it. Amide ion (pKaH 38) can deprotonate almost everything organic. LDA, a bulky lithium amide base (pKaH ≈ 36), is chosen precisely because it is strong enough for ketone alpha-hydrogens at pKa 20 while being too bulky to attack the carbonyl itself.

Once you see this, choosing a base stops being memorization. You look up what you need to deprotonate, and you pick a base whose pKaH is comfortably higher.

Worked example — which base will do the job?

You have three substrates to deprotonate completely: ethanol (O–H, pKa 16), a ketone alpha hydrogen (pKa 20), and a terminal alkyne (C–H, pKa 25). Four bases are on the shelf: NaOH (pKaH 15.7), NaOEt (pKaH 16), NaH (pKaH 36, for H₂), NaNH₂ (pKaH 38).

Ethanol. NaOH gives K ≈ 1015.7−16, roughly 1:2 — an equilibrium mixture, not a reaction. NaOEt is the same molecule's own conjugate base, so K = 1 and nothing is achieved. NaH works: K ≈ 1020, and because the by-product is hydrogen gas that bubbles out of the flask, it is irreversible as well as favorable. That is the standard way to make an alkoxide.

The ketone. NaOH and NaOEt are four units short, so both give a trace of enolate at equilibrium — useful when a trace is what you want, useless when you need all of it. NaH and NaNH₂ are both sixteen-plus units above and both work thermodynamically; NaNH₂ is nucleophilic enough to attack the carbonyl as well, which is why a hindered amide base is preferred in practice.

The alkyne. Hydroxide and ethoxide are nine units short and do nothing. NaNH₂ is thirteen units above: K ≈ 1013, complete, and it is the reagent every textbook specifies for making acetylides.

The rule extracted: aim for a pKaH at least three or four units above the proton you are removing. Two units is an equilibrium; ten units is a reaction.

pH decides which form you are holding

pKa is a property of the molecule. pH is a property of the solution it is sitting in, and the two together decide whether you have the acid or its conjugate base in the flask. The rule is short: below its pKa an acid is mostly protonated; above its pKa it is mostly deprotonated; at pH = pKa the two forms are present in equal amounts.

The crossover is sharp, because the underlying scale is logarithmic — one pH unit either side of the pKa is already a 10:1 ratio, and two units is 100:1. So acetic acid (pKa 4.76) is a neutral molecule in stomach acid and a carboxylate anion in blood at pH 7.4, and an amine with a conjugate acid pKa of 10 is an ammonium cation in both. That single fact explains why carboxylic acids are extracted into aqueous base and recovered by acidifying, and why nearly every amine-containing drug is sold as its hydrochloride salt: at the pH of the body it is charged anyway, and the salt is what dissolves.

Two cautions on the numbers

pKa is solvent-dependent. The values in every table are measured in water (or extrapolated to it), and they shift in other solvents — sometimes by many units. The relative ordering usually survives, which is what you rely on, but absolute values in DMSO can differ substantially from the aqueous ones.

Strong acids are all leveled in water. HCl, HBr, HI and H₂SO₄ are all completely dissociated in aqueous solution, so water cannot distinguish between them — the strongest acid that can exist in water is H₃O⁺. Their quoted pKa values come from measurements in other solvents. The same levelling applies at the other end: the strongest base that survives in water is hydroxide, which is why reagents like NaNH₂ and LDA must be used in aprotic solvents.

What carries forward

pKa reasoning runs through the whole course. It ranks leaving groups (Organic Structure & Electron Movement and Substitution & Elimination), it decides whether a base will do the job in every elimination and enolate reaction, it explains the relative reactivity of carboxylic acid derivatives in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives, it governs which nitrogen gets protonated in Amines, and it is the framework behind every extraction you will run in the lab.
