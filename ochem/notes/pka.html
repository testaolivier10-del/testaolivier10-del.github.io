Ka and pKa

For an acid HA dissociating in solution, HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, the equilibrium constant is Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]. A larger Ka means the equilibrium sits further toward the dissociated (ionized) side — a stronger acid. Because Ka values span many orders of magnitude, chemists use pKa = −log₁₀(Ka) instead, the same trick used for pH. This means lower pKa = stronger acid — the negative sign flips the direction.

A pKa table worth memorizing the shape of

HCl ≈ −7 (essentially fully dissociated in water), H₃O⁺ ≈ −1.7, HF ≈ 3.2, acetic acid (CH₃COOH) ≈ 4.76, phenol (aromatic OH) ≈ 10, water ≈ 15.7, ethanol (and most alcohols) ≈ 16, terminal alkyne C–H ≈ 25, ammonia (N–H) ≈ 38, alkane C–H ≈ 50. Each step down this list is a weaker acid — and a correspondingly stronger conjugate base.

Every 1 unit of pKa is a 10-fold change in acid strength, because of the logarithm. Acetic acid (pKa 4.76) is roughly 10¹¹ times more acidic than ethanol (pKa 16) — an almost unimaginable difference that a bare number easily hides.

Predicting which side an equilibrium favors

In a proton transfer, the equilibrium favors the side with the weaker acid — equivalently, the side where the acid has the higher pKa. Compare the pKa of the acid on the left to the pKa of the acid that would form on the right (the conjugate acid of the base); the reaction favors whichever side has the acid with the higher pKa, because that's the more stable, lower-energy species.
