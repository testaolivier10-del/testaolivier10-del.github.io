Amino acids

Practice this — interactive lesson

An α-amino acid carries an amino group and a carboxylic acid on the same carbon. Twenty of them are used to build proteins, they differ only in the side chain, and essentially all of their distinctive behavior comes from having an acid and a base in one small molecule.

They are never neutral molecules

Drawn with a free NH₂ and a free COOH, an amino acid looks like a compound with two untouched groups. It is not. The carboxylic acid (pKa ≈ 2) is a far stronger acid than the ammonium ion (pKa ≈ 9–10) is, so the proton moves internally and the molecule exists as a zwitterion: −COO⁻ and −NH₃⁺ at the same time, overall neutral.

This is not a curiosity; it explains the physical properties. Amino acids are high-melting crystalline solids that dissolve in water and not in ether — they behave like salts, because at ordinary pH they are one.

Three species, depending on pH: at low pH everything is protonated and the molecule is a cation (COOH, NH₃⁺). At high pH everything is deprotonated and it is an anion (COO⁻, NH₂). In between sits the zwitterion. Sketching that three-species ladder is how nearly every amino acid question is solved.

The isoelectric point

The isoelectric point, pI, is the pH at which the molecule carries no net charge — the pH where the zwitterion dominates. For an amino acid with no ionizable side chain it is the average of the two pKa values on either side of the zwitterion:

pI = ½(pKa1 + pKa2)

Glycine, with pKa values near 2.3 and 9.6, has a pI near 6.0 — slightly acidic, not 7, which is worth noticing because the intuition that "neutral means pH 7" is wrong here.

When the side chain ionizes, you average the two pKa values that flank the neutral form rather than simply the first two. For an acidic side chain such as aspartate that means the two acidic pKa values, giving a low pI; for a basic side chain such as lysine it means the two basic ones, giving a high pI.

Worked example — pI when the side chain ionizes

Lysine has pKa values 2.18 (COOH), 8.95 (α-NH₃⁺) and 10.53 (side-chain NH₃⁺). Find its pI.

Write the species out in order of pH, with their charges. Below 2.18: COOH intact and both ammoniums on, net +2. Between 2.18 and 8.95: COO⁻ and two ammoniums, net +1. Between 8.95 and 10.53: COO⁻, the α-amine now neutral, the side chain still protonated, net 0. Above 10.53: net −1.

Find the zero and average its two neighbors. The uncharged form is bracketed by 8.95 and 10.53, so pI = ½(8.95 + 10.53) = 9.74.

Check that the answer points the right way. A basic side chain should push the pI up, and 9.74 sits well above the ~6 of an amino acid with an inert side chain. Averaging the lowest two values out of habit gives 5.57, which is the standard wrong answer.

Now aspartic acid: pKa 1.88 (α-COOH), 3.65 (side-chain COOH), 9.60 (NH₃⁺). The ladder runs +1 → 0 → −1 → −2, and the uncharged form is bracketed by 1.88 and 3.65, so pI = ½(1.88 + 3.65) = 2.77. Two acids drag the balance point low. The procedure never changes: build the ladder, find the zero, average the two pKa values on either side of it.

Stereochemistry: one carbon, one answer, two exceptions

The α carbon of an amino acid bears four different groups — the amino group, the carboxyl, an H and the side chain — so it is a stereocenter. Which of the two biology uses is read exactly as a sugar's D or L is read: draw the Fischer projection with the carboxyl at the top and the side chain at the bottom, and look at the horizontal amino group. On the left it is L; on the right it is D. Essentially every amino acid in every protein is L, and the mnemonic that survives an exam is CORN, and its viewing direction is the half people drop: hold the α carbon with the H pointing toward you and read CO–R–N clockwise — that is L. Turn the molecule so the H points away instead and the same three groups read counterclockwise for L, because looking from the other side reverses every rotation you see. The mnemonic is a piece of geometry rather than a CIP assignment, which is why it still returns L for cysteine even though cysteine is the one amino acid labeled R.

  • Glycine is the exception that is not a stereocenter at all. Its side chain is a hydrogen, so the α carbon has two identical groups and glycine is achiral.
  • Cysteine is the exception that trips people. It is L like the others, but because sulfur outranks the carboxyl carbon in the CIP priority rules, it is assigned R while the other eighteen chiral ones are S — glycine has no configuration to assign. Nothing about its shape is unusual; the label changed because the priority order did.
L and S are not synonyms, and cysteine is the standing proof. D/L is a relational classification, like the sugars' D/L; R/S is computed from CIP priorities. Two systems, two answers, and they agree only by coincidence.

Grouping the twenty by side chain

Memorizing twenty structures is a poor use of effort compared with grouping them by what the side chain does, which is what determines where each one sits in a folded protein:

  • Nonpolar — alkyl or aromatic side chains (alanine, valine, leucine, phenylalanine). Hydrophobic, so they end up buried in the protein's interior.
  • Polar, uncharged — OH, SH or amide side chains (serine, threonine, cysteine, asparagine). Hydrogen bond, often found at the surface or in active sites.
  • Acidic — a second carboxylic acid (aspartate, glutamate). Negatively charged at physiological pH, low pI.
  • Basic — an amine or a nitrogen-containing ring (lysine, arginine, histidine). Positively charged at physiological pH, high pI.

Two more are worth singling out. Proline's side chain loops back to its own nitrogen, making a ring and a secondary amine — which stops it adopting the shapes the others can and gives it a special role in protein folding. Cysteine's thiol can be oxidized to form a disulfide bond with another cysteine, the only covalent cross-link in ordinary protein structure.

Ninhydrin is the standard way to see an amino acid that is otherwise invisible: it reacts with a free α-amino group to give a deep purple dye, which is why chromatography plates and fingerprint work both use it. Proline is the exception again — its nitrogen is secondary and locked in a ring, so it gives a yellow product rather than purple.
Worked example — predicting direction of movement in an electric field

Alanine (pI ≈ 6.0) is placed in a buffer at pH 9. Which electrode does it move toward?

Compare pH with pI. The pH is above the pI, so the molecule has lost more protons than it has at its isoelectric point: it carries a net negative charge.

Negative moves toward the positive electrode, the anode.

The general rule is worth holding: pH above pI means negative; pH below pI means positive; pH equal to pI means no movement. That single comparison answers every electrophoresis question in this chapter, and it is also the principle by which proteins are separated in a lab.

Where amino acids come from in a flask

Three classical routes, and the course already owns all three. HVZ then ammonia: brominate a carboxylic acid at the α position and displace the bromide with excess NH3 by SN2, which Enolate Chemistry did explicitly. The Gabriel–malonic ester sequence alkylates a phthalimidomalonate, then hydrolyzes and decarboxylates it. The Strecker synthesis treats an aldehyde with NH3 and HCN to give an α-amino nitrile — an imine formed and then captured by cyanide, both from Carbonyl Chemistry — and hydrolyzes the nitrile to the acid.

All three deliver the amino acid as a racemate, because in each one the new stereocenter is formed at a planar intermediate with no chiral influence anywhere near it. Biology's amino acids are single enantiomers, so a laboratory route has to be followed by a resolution — the point at which Stereochemistry's resolution stops being a puzzle and becomes a manufacturing cost.

What carries forward

The acid–base reasoning here is the same as Acids & Bases’s, applied to a molecule with two ionizable groups instead of one. The stereochemistry is Stereochemistry’s. What is new is only the biological vocabulary — and the habit of asking, of any amino acid at any pH, which of the three species is present.
