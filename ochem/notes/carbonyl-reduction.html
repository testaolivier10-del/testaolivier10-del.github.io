Reducing carbonyls

Practice this — interactive lesson

Reducing a carbonyl is the reverse of the last section, and mechanically it is one idea: a hydride — H with two electrons — attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon, the π electrons go up onto oxygen, and the resulting alkoxide is protonated on workup to give an alcohol. The whole subject is which reagent delivers that hydride, and to what.

Two reagents, very different appetites

SubstrateNaBH₄LiAlH₄
Aldehyde1° alcohol1° alcohol
Ketone2° alcohol2° alcohol
Estervery slow / no1° alcohol
Acid chloride1° alcohol1° alcohol
Carboxylic acidno reaction1° alcohol
Amideno reactionamine
Nitrileno reaction1° amine
Alkene, alkyneno reactionno reaction

Two rows in that table are worth reading twice. An amide reduced by LiAlH₄ gives an amine, not an alcohol — the nitrogen stays attached and the oxygen leaves entirely, which makes it the standard route from an amide to an amine. And neither reagent touches an isolated alkene or alkyne: hydride reduction needs a polarized π bond to attack, and C=C has no electrophilic end. Reducing an alkene is catalytic hydrogenation's job, in the next section. The acid chloride row is the one exception to "NaBH₄ only touches aldehydes and ketones": an acid chloride is so much more electrophilic than an ester that even the mild reagent reduces it, and it goes all the way to the primary alcohol for the same reason the ester does — chloride is an excellent leaving group, so the tetrahedral intermediate collapses to an aldehyde and the aldehyde is reduced again.

Why the difference, and how to use it

Aluminum is less electronegative than boron, so the Al–H bond is more polarized and LiAlH₄ is by far the more reactive hydride source. That single fact produces the practical differences:

  • NaBH₄ is mild enough to use in methanol or ethanol. It reacts with protic solvents only slowly, so you can simply dissolve it and go. That convenience is real and is part of why it is the default for a simple ketone.
  • LiAlH₄ reacts violently with water, including atmospheric moisture, and must be used in an aprotic ether solvent — dry THF or diethyl ether — with a separate aqueous workup afterwards to protonate the alkoxide.
  • Selectivity comes from choosing the weaker reagent. A molecule containing both a ketone and an ester is reduced at the ketone alone by NaBH₄, while LiAlH₄ would reduce both. Deliberately reaching for the less powerful reagent is the commonest way this material appears in a synthesis question.
Hydride reagents are nucleophiles, not acids or bases in the working sense, and they attack the carbonyl carbon. That is the same electrophilic carbon the nucleophilic addition chapter is about — so this is not a new mechanism, it is the general carbonyl addition with H⁻ as the nucleophile.

Reducing a ketone usually makes a racemic mixture

The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar — that is the figure from Carbonyl Chemistry, and it has two faces. A hydride arriving from the top and a hydride arriving from the bottom give products that are mirror images, and nothing in a flask of NaBH₄ and butan-2-one prefers one face over the other. So reducing a ketone whose two substituents differ gives the alcohol as a racemic mixture: exactly 50:50, optically inactive.

Say it as a rule you can apply without thinking: a flat, achiral starting material plus an achiral reagent cannot give a single enantiomer. Butan-2-one plus NaBH₄ gives (±)-butan-2-ol, not (R)- and not (S)-. If an exam question asks for a single enantiomer from a plain ketone reduction, the answer it wants is "you cannot get one this way" — that needs a chiral reagent or an enzyme, which is a later course.

This is the same argument as the racemization in an SN1 reaction from Substitution & Elimination: a flat intermediate attacked equally from both faces. Only here the flat thing is the starting material rather than an intermediate.

Esters and acids take two hydrides

A preview. The next chapter, Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives, is built on one question about the tetrahedral intermediate: does it keep everything and protonate, or does it throw something out and re-form the C=O? You need the answer one chapter early here, and it is short. A group leaves if it is a weak base: chloride and alkoxide go, an amide nitrogen (R₂N⁻) does not, and a carbanion certainly does not. That one line is all this section borrows.

Reducing a ketone delivers one hydride and stops, because the alkoxide formed has nothing to expel. An ester is different: the first hydride gives a tetrahedral intermediate that does have a leaving group, the alkoxide of the alcohol half. It collapses, expelling that alkoxide and giving an aldehyde — which is more electrophilic than the ester was, and is immediately reduced again by a second hydride.

So an ester goes to a primary alcohol and you cannot normally stop at the aldehyde, because the intermediate is more reactive than the starting material. Stopping there requires a deliberately weakened reagent (DIBAL-H at low temperature), which is worth knowing exists even if the details belong to a later course.

Worked example — one molecule, three reagents

Take ethyl 4-oxopentanoate, CH₃CO–CH₂CH₂–CO₂CH₂CH₃: a methyl ketone at one end, an ethyl ester at the other. Give the product of each reagent.

Step 1 — inventory the electrophiles. Two carbonyls. Rank them: a ketone is more electrophilic than an ester, because the ester's oxygen donates its lone pair into the C=O and takes some of the positive character away. So whatever reagent you use attacks the ketone first.

Step 2 — NaBH₄ in methanol, then workup. NaBH₄ is strong enough for the ketone and not for the ester. One hydride adds to the ketone carbon, the alkoxide is protonated on workup, and the ester is untouched. Product: ethyl 4-hydroxypentanoate, CH₃CH(OH)–CH₂CH₂–CO₂CH₂CH₃. (In practice that hydroxy-ester will close to a lactone, but the reduction itself stops here.)

Step 3 — LiAlH₄ in dry THF, then aqueous workup. Now both react. The ketone takes one hydride and gives a secondary alcohol. The ester takes two: the first gives a tetrahedral intermediate that expels ethoxide to leave an aldehyde, and that aldehyde — more electrophilic than the ester it came from — is reduced at once. Product: the diol, CH₃CH(OH)–CH₂CH₂–CH₂OH, plus ethanol from the expelled ethoxide.

Step 4 — H₂ over Pd/C at one atmosphere. Neither carbonyl is touched; hydrogenation at one atmosphere is for C=C and C≡C. Product: starting material, recovered.

The point of the exercise: three reagents, three answers, and the molecule never changed. Every "which reagent" question in synthesis is this question.

Reduction to a methylene

Hydride reduction takes a ketone down one rung, to an alcohol. Two named reactions take it down two, replacing C=O with CH₂ entirely, and they exist as a pair because they tolerate opposite conditions:

  • Clemmensen reduction — Zn(Hg), concentrated HCl. Strongly acidic, so use it when the rest of the molecule can survive acid but not base.
  • Wolff–Kishner reduction — hydrazine then hot KOH. Strongly basic, for molecules that cannot survive acid.

Two things bound them both. They only work on aldehydes and ketones. An ester or an amide is not reduced to a methylene by either one — both reactions start by attacking the C=O, and a carbonyl flanked by an oxygen or a nitrogen is too unreactive and has a leaving group to expel instead. So "C=O to CH₂" is a ketone-and-aldehyde operation, full stop.

And the Wolff–Kishner's driving force is worth naming, because it is the usual follow-up question: hydrazine H₂N–NH₂ first condenses with the ketone to give a hydrazone, C=N–NH₂ (the same addition-then-lose-water pattern as imine formation in Carbonyl Chemistry). Hot hydroxide then deprotonates it, and the carbanion that forms expels nitrogen gas. N₂ leaving is irreversible and enormously favorable, and it is what drags the whole sequence forward.

The pairing is the point: whichever one your substrate tolerates, the other is available. Both come up again in aromatic chemistry, where a Friedel–Crafts acylation followed by one of these is the standard way to attach an unrearranged alkyl chain to a ring.

What carries forward

Hydride addition to a carbonyl is the same mechanism as every other nucleophilic addition in the carbonyl chapters, with H⁻ standing in for the nucleophile — so if you can draw a Grignard addition, you can already draw this. The selectivity table is the part to hold: it is the source of most "which reagent" questions in synthesis, and the amide-to-amine row is the one most often missed.
