The two rules

A curved arrow represents two electrons moving together. The tail starts wherever those electrons currently are — a lone pair, or an existing bond. The head points to wherever they end up — a new bond, or a new lone pair. The tail can never start on a bare atom or a positive charge, because there's nothing there to give away — it must start on an actual pair of electrons.

Worked example: ammonia + H⁺

Tail on nitrogen's lone pair (the electron source), head on H⁺ (where the new N–H bond forms). Order matters: the arrow always starts at the electrons and points to where they're going, never the reverse.

Spotting invalid arrows

An arrow drawn starting from a positive charge is always wrong — a positively charged atom is electron-deficient, so it can only ever be a destination (a head), never a source (a tail).

Every SN1, SN2, E1, E2, addition, and carbonyl mechanism for the rest of the course is built from these same two rules: tail on the electrons, head on the destination.

Fishhook arrows

A full curved arrow (double-barbed head) moves 2 electrons — that's everything in this course. A fishhook arrow (single-barbed head) moves just 1 electron, used for radical mechanisms. Just recognize the visual difference; you won't need to draw these for SN1/SN2/E1/E2.
