Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons · Section 12 of 119

Curved arrows

Practice this — interactive lesson

A curved arrow is the notation organic chemists use to say where electrons went. Every mechanism in this course — every substitution, elimination, addition, and carbonyl reaction — is written in this one symbol. It has exactly two rules, and almost every mechanism error you will make for the next several months is a violation of one of them.

Four words appear below before their own sections arrive, so here they are in one line each. A nucleophile is the species that supplies the electron pair — the tail end of an arrow. An electrophile is the electron-poor atom that receives it — the head end. A leaving group is a group that departs taking the bonding pair with it. SN2 is the name of the one-step reaction in which a nucleophile attacks a carbon at the same instant a leaving group departs from it; the next few sections cover the first three properly, and Substitution & Elimination covers the reaction itself. Nothing on this page needs more than these sentences.

The two rules

A curved arrow represents two electrons moving together. The tail starts wherever those electrons currently are. The head points at wherever they end up.

A tail may start on exactly two things: a lone pair, or an existing bond. Nothing else. A tail can never start on a bare atom, on a positive charge, or on empty space, because there are no electrons there to move.

One piece of accepted shorthand sits inside that rule rather than beside it. Because lone pairs are usually left off a drawing, chemists often start a tail at a drawn negative charge — the minus on an alkoxide oxygen, say. That is legal because the charge implies the lone pair: a species carrying a formal negative charge has unshared electrons at that atom by definition, so the tail is still starting on a lone pair, just one you have not troubled to draw. It is not a third kind of tail, and the test never changes: name the pair of electrons the tail is standing on. If you cannot, the arrow is wrong.

A head may point at two things, and the first of them is ambiguous in a way worth naming. A head pointing at an atom means one of two outcomes: a new sigma bond between the source and that atom, if the electrons came from somewhere else in the drawing; or a new lone pair on that atom, if the electrons came from a bond that atom was already part of. The C=O pi bond collapsing onto its own oxygen is the second case — nothing new is bonded, oxygen simply gains a pair — and it is the single commonest arrow in the course. A head pointing at the middle of a bond means a new pi bond forms there, between the two atoms that bond already joins. A head pointing at an atom that already has a full octet is a signal that something else must break simultaneously — which usually means you need a second arrow.

Worked example 1 — ammonia protonated by a bare H⁺

Ammonia's nitrogen has a lone pair; H⁺ has an empty 1s orbital and no electrons at all.

One arrow: tail on nitrogen's lone pair, head on the hydrogen. The lone pair becomes the new N–H bond.

Check the result: nitrogen now has 4 bonds and no lone pairs, so FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. The proton, now sharing a pair, is neutral. Charge in: +1. Charge out: +1. ✓

Drawing this arrow the other way round — from the H⁺ to the nitrogen — is wrong even though it "shows what connects to what," because H⁺ has no electrons to donate. Arrows track electrons, not atoms.

That is the clean case, and it is clean because H⁺ was handed to you with nothing attached. Real flasks do not contain bare protons: a proton in solution is always bonded to something — H–Cl, H₃O⁺, H₂SO₄ — and prising it off is a second event needing a second arrow. The next example is the same reaction done honestly.

Worked example 2 — the same protonation from H–Cl, which is how it really happens

Now react ammonia with hydrogen chloride. The nitrogen still wants the proton, but the proton is not free: it is held by a covalent bond to chlorine, and that bond has to break in the same step.

Arrow 1: tail on nitrogen's lone pair, head on the hydrogen of H–Cl. That would give the hydrogen two bonds, which hydrogen cannot have.

Arrow 2, fired at the same instant: tail on the H–Cl bond, head on the chlorine. Chlorine leaves with both electrons as chloride.

Check the charges. Nitrogen: 4 bonds, no lone pair, FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Chlorine: 3 lone pairs became 4, no bonds, FC = 7 − 8 − 0 = −1. Charge in: 0 and 0. Charge out: +1 and −1, which sums to 0. ✓

Every proton transfer you will draw for the rest of the course has this two-arrow shape, because the proton always comes from somewhere. The one-arrow version above is a legitimate simplification only when the question hands you H⁺ on its own.

Worked example 3 — deprotonation, which is the same step seen from the other side

Hydroxide removing the O–H proton of ethanol, CH₃CH₂–O–H + HO⁻.

Arrow 1: tail on a lone pair of the hydroxide oxygen, head on the ethanol's hydrogen. Arrow 2: tail on the ethanol O–H bond, head on that oxygen, which keeps the pair.

Check the charges. The hydroxide oxygen goes from 1 bond and 3 lone pairs (6 − 6 − 1 = −1) to 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs (6 − 4 − 2 = 0): it has become water. The ethanol oxygen goes from 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs (neutral) to 1 bond and 3 lone pairs (6 − 6 − 1 = −1): it has become ethoxide, an alkoxide, the anion you get by taking the proton off an alcohol. Charge in −1, charge out −1. ✓

Protonation and deprotonation are not two skills. They are one pattern read in two directions, and in both of them the base supplies the electrons and the departing partner keeps the bond.

Worked example 4 — two arrows at once

Hydroxide attacking bromomethane, the canonical SN2. If you drew only the arrow from hydroxide's lone pair to the carbon, you would produce a carbon with five bonds, which does not exist.

So a second arrow is required, and it must fire at the same instant: tail on the C–Br bond, head onto the bromine, which leaves with both electrons as bromide.

Every arrow that would overfill a second-row atom demands a partner arrow that empties it again. That pairing is the shape of most mechanism steps.

The four patterns, and that is the whole list

Every mechanism in this course is assembled from four arrow-pushing patterns. They are not four things to memorize; they are the only four things two electrons can usefully do to an organic molecule, and naming them turns an unfamiliar mechanism into a sequence you have already drawn.

1. Nucleophilic attack. One arrow, tail on a lone pair or a pi bond, head on an electron-poor atom. A new bond forms. If the target already has a full valence shell, this arrow cannot stand alone and needs pattern 2 beside it; if the target has an empty orbital — a carbocation, a proton, BF₃ — then one arrow is the whole step and nothing has to break, because there was room.

2. Loss of a leaving group. One arrow, tail on the bond between carbon and the departing group, head on the departing group. Nothing attacks; the bond simply breaks with both electrons going one way. Push Br⁻ off a tertiary bromide this way and the product is a carbocation: carbon had four bonds and now has three, so it is +1, and the bromine had no charge and now carries the pair, so it is −1. Charge in 0, charge out 0. This is the pattern students under-use, because it feels like nothing is driving it, and it is exactly the first step of an SN1 or E1 reaction.

3. Proton transfer. Two arrows, as in the worked examples above: base's lone pair to the hydrogen, and the H–X bond to X. Half the steps in acid-catalyzed mechanisms are this and nothing else.

4. Rearrangement. One arrow, tail on a C–H or C–C sigma bond next door to a carbocation, head on the cation's carbon. The hydrogen or the alkyl group migrates with its bonding pair, and the positive charge moves to the carbon the pair left behind. This is a 1,2-hydride shift or 1,2-alkyl shift, and it happens whenever the new cation would be more stable than the old one. You will meet it properly in Substitution & Elimination; what matters here is recognizing a sigma bond as a legitimate electron source, which it is, and which rules 1 and 2 already allowed.

Almost every multi-step mechanism you will be asked to draw is these four in some order, with pattern 3 appearing wherever the conditions are acidic or basic. When a question gives you reactants and a product and asks for the mechanism, the productive move is not to recall the reaction — it is to ask which of the four could get you one step closer.

Spotting invalid arrows

Four checks catch nearly everything.

Does the tail start on electrons? An arrow starting from a positive charge is always wrong. A positively charged atom is electron-deficient: it can only be a destination, never a source. This is the single most common beginner's error, and it comes from thinking of arrows as showing attack direction rather than electron flow.

Does any atom end up with too many bonds? Carbon never exceeds four, nitrogen never exceeds four, oxygen never exceeds three. If your product violates one of these, you formed a bond without breaking one.

Does charge balance? Total charge on the left must equal total charge on the right, every single step. Recompute formal charges after each arrow until it becomes automatic.

Does the arrow go from rich to poor? Electrons flow from an electron-rich site — a lone pair, a pi bond, a negative charge — toward an electron-poor one. An arrow running from a δ+ carbon toward a δ− oxygen is going uphill and is almost certainly backwards.

Arrows are not "what attacks what." They are "where the electrons go." In practice the two often point the same way, since the nucleophile supplies the electrons — but not always, and the exceptions are exactly where people trip. When a leaving group departs, the arrow points away from the carbon and onto the leaving group, even though nothing is attacking anything.

Arrows in resonance versus arrows in mechanism

The same symbol does two related jobs, and it helps to keep them straight. In a resonance context, arrows show how to get from one drawn structure to another drawn structure of the same molecule; nothing physically moves, and the arrows are a bookkeeping device for generating valid contributors. In a mechanism context, arrows describe electrons genuinely moving as one species becomes another.

The rules for drawing them are identical, which is why learning them here pays twice. The difference is only in what you write between the structures: ↔ for resonance, → for a mechanism step.

Fishhook arrows

A full curved arrow with a double-barbed head moves two electrons, and that covers essentially everything in this course. A fishhook arrow, drawn with a single barb, moves just one electron, and is used for radical mechanisms — homolytic bond cleavage, radical halogenation, radical addition of HBr. You should recognize the visual difference; you will not need to draw one for SN1, SN2, E1 or E2.

Every mechanism in the rest of the course is built from these two rules: tail on the electrons, head on the destination. There is no second notation to learn and no special cases to memorize. Practising arrows until they are automatic is the highest-return investment available this early in the course — and the arrow-pushing tool is built for exactly this drill.

What carries forward

Immediately: generating resonance structures reliably, which is the previous section's payoff. Then every mechanism from Substitution & Elimination onward. When you meet a reaction you have never seen, the way in is always the same — find the electron-rich site, find the electron-poor site, and draw the arrow between them. Much of what looks like memorization in organic chemistry is really this one skill applied to new molecules.
