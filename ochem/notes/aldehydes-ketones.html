Structure: a polarized, planar, sp² carbon

The carbonyl carbon (C=O) is sp² hybridized, trigonal planar, with a pi bond to oxygen exactly like an alkene's pi bond (Alkene Structure lesson) — except oxygen is far more electronegative than carbon. That electronegativity difference polarizes the pi bond heavily: carbon carries a substantial partial positive charge (δ+) and oxygen a substantial partial negative charge (δ−). This is captured by two resonance structures (Resonance lesson): the neutral C=O form, and a charge-separated form with a full positive charge on carbon and a full negative charge (plus a lone pair) on oxygen — the second form is a real, significant contributor, not just a formality.

The carbonyl carbon is a strong electrophile

That partial (and resonance-supported, partial-full) positive charge makes the carbonyl carbon an excellent electrophile (Electrophiles lesson) — nucleophiles attack it directly, exactly the reaction class explored in the next lesson. This is a direct structural parallel to the alkene addition reactions of Module 7, except here the electrophile is built into the substrate itself rather than needing to be generated by protonation first.

Aldehydes are drawn RCHO (carbonyl carbon bonded to at least one H); ketones are drawn RCOR' (carbonyl carbon bonded to two carbon groups, no H). This structural difference is the entire basis for their different reactivity, covered next.

Aldehydes are more electrophilic — and more reactive — than ketones

Two effects both point the same direction. Sterics: a ketone's two alkyl/aryl groups crowd the carbonyl carbon more than an aldehyde's one alkyl group plus small H, making the ketone carbon harder for a nucleophile to approach (identical logic to SN2 steric hindrance, Module 6). Electronics: alkyl groups donate electron density (the same hyperconjugation/induction effect that stabilizes carbocations and alkenes), which partially cancels the carbonyl carbon's positive character — a ketone's two donating groups cancel more of that positive charge than an aldehyde's one.

Hydration equilibria prove the trend quantitatively

Water's reversible addition to a carbonyl (forming a geminal diol, a real quantitative test of electrophilicity) gives strikingly different equilibrium constants: formaldehyde (H₂C=O, no alkyl groups) has Keq ≈ 1000 (the hydrate dominates); acetaldehyde (CH₃CHO, one alkyl group) has Keq ≈ 1.4 (roughly 50/50); acetone ((CH₃)₂C=O, two alkyl groups) has Keq ≈ 0.0014 (the hydrate is negligible). Each additional alkyl group drops the equilibrium constant by roughly three orders of magnitude — hard, direct evidence for the steric-plus-electronic reactivity trend.
