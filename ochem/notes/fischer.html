The drawing convention

A Fischer projection draws a stereocenter as a simple cross: a horizontal line and a vertical line crossing at the carbon (the carbon itself is usually left off, implied at the intersection). The convention that makes this work in 3D: horizontal lines represent bonds pointing toward the viewer (out of the page, like a wedge), and vertical lines represent bonds pointing away from the viewer (into the page, like a dash).

Why this convention exists

This convention comes from drawing the molecule's carbon backbone in a specific curved, "C-shape" orientation — vertical bonds naturally point back and away, horizontal bonds naturally point forward. Chemists (originally Emil Fischer, working out carbohydrate structures) adopted this as a fast shorthand instead of drawing full 3D wedge-dash structures every time.

Because the vertical bonds point away from you by convention, a Fischer projection's vertical-chain carbon (usually the highest-priority chain direction, like toward the CHO group in a sugar) is already oriented correctly for the R/S "lowest priority away from you" rule — as long as the lowest-priority group happens to be on a vertical position. If the lowest-priority group is on a horizontal position instead, use the flip-the-answer trick from the R/S lesson.

Rules for manipulating a Fischer projection safely

Two operations preserve the actual stereochemistry (don't change what molecule you're looking at): (1) rotating the entire projection 180° in the plane of the page, and (2) holding one group fixed and rotating the other three amongst themselves. One operation that breaks it (silently converts the molecule into its enantiomer): swapping any two groups a single time, or rotating the whole projection 90°. Swapping two groups twice, however, gets you back to the original — an even number of swaps preserves the molecule, an odd number inverts it.

Why Fischer projections are still used today

Fischer projections remain the standard way to draw sugars (carbohydrates) specifically because sugars often have many stereocenters in a single chain (glucose has four), and the vertical stacked-cross format makes comparing multiple stereocenters at a glance far easier than drawing repeated wedge-dash structures. You'll see this format used constantly if you continue into biochemistry.
