The universal mechanism

Every nucleophilic addition to a carbonyl follows the same two-step skeleton.

Step 1: the nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon, and the pi electrons are pushed entirely onto oxygen. That leaves a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate — the carbon has gone from sp² trigonal planar to sp³ tetrahedral, a real geometric change you can draw with a curved arrow (Curved Arrows lesson).

Step 2: that alkoxide is protonated — by water, by an acid workup, or by any other proton source around — giving a neutral alcohol.

One branch is worth flagging now. If the nucleophile itself carries a leaving group, step 2 can go differently: instead of simply picking up a proton, the tetrahedral intermediate collapses back down and reforms a C=O. That is the whole difference between addition and substitution, and it is what the next lesson (hemiacetals and acetals) and Module 10 (acyl substitution) are built on.

Hydride reduction: NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄

Both reagents deliver a hydride ion (H⁻) as the nucleophile, attacking the carbonyl carbon and reducing it to an alcohol. NaBH₄ is milder and more selective — it reduces aldehydes and ketones but leaves carboxylic acid derivatives (esters, amides) alone. LiAlH₄ is a much stronger, less selective hydride source that reduces essentially everything with a carbonyl, including esters, amides, and even carboxylic acids, all the way to alcohols (or amines, from amides).

Reducing a ketone gives a secondary alcohol; reducing an aldehyde gives a primary alcohol — the exact reverse of the alcohol oxidation ladder from Module 8, run backward.

Grignard and organolithium addition: building new C–C bonds

Grignard reagents (RMgX, made from an alkyl halide and Mg metal) and organolithiums (RLi) are powerful carbon nucleophiles — the carbon itself, bearing a full negative-charge character, attacks the carbonyl carbon directly. This forms a brand-new carbon-carbon bond, a rare and valuable outcome (alongside the acetylide SN2 chemistry from the Alkynes lesson). Addition to formaldehyde gives a primary alcohol; to any other aldehyde, a secondary alcohol; to a ketone, a tertiary alcohol — the alcohol's substitution class is set directly by how many carbon groups were already on the carbonyl carbon before addition.

Imine and enamine formation with amines

A primary amine (R–NH₂) adds to a carbonyl exactly like any other nucleophile, forming a tetrahedral intermediate — but this one has a proton on nitrogen and a hydroxyl on carbon, both of which can be lost as water in a subsequent elimination step, forming a C=N double bond: an imine. A secondary amine (R₂NH) can't form a stable imine (no N–H left to lose after the second dehydration step) and instead forms an enamine (a C=C conjugated with nitrogen's lone pair) via a slightly different elimination pathway. This addition-then-elimination pattern — building a tetrahedral intermediate, then kicking out water to reform a pi bond — is the direct mechanistic bridge into Module 12 (Amines) and reappears throughout Module 11 (Enolate Chemistry).
