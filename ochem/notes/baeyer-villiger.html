Baeyer–Villiger oxidation

Practice this — interactive lesson

Treat acetone, CH3–CO–CH3, with the reagent mCPBA and you get methyl acetate, CH3–CO–O–CH3. One oxygen atom has slipped into a carbon–carbon bond, between the carbonyl carbon and one of its methyl groups. The ketone has become an ester.

This is the Baeyer–Villiger oxidation. A peroxyacid (defined below) turns a ketone into an ester by putting an oxygen next to the carbonyl carbon, on one of its two sides:

R–CO–R′  +  mCPBA  →  R–CO–O–R′

It is unlike the other ketone reactions in this course. In nucleophilic addition and everything built on it, a new group joins the carbonyl carbon and its C–C bonds stay as they were. Here one of those C–C bonds is cut, and an oxygen fills the gap.

It counts as an oxidation because the carbonyl carbon gains a bond to oxygen: two in the ketone, three in the ester (see Oxidation levels).

The usual reagent, mCPBA, is short for meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid. It is a peroxyacid: a carboxylic acid with one extra oxygen, so that the acid group reads C(=O)–O–O–H instead of C(=O)–O–H. It is the same reagent that turns an alkene into an epoxide (Oxidative cleavage & dihydroxylation).

Two parts of mCPBA do the work. The oxygen at the O–H end is the one that ends up in the product. The O–O bond beside it is weak, and when it breaks, the rest of the reagent leaves as a carboxylate ion, ArCO2−. Resonance spreads that negative charge over two oxygens, so the carboxylate is a good leaving group.

The mechanism: an addition, then a shift

Follow one ketone through. Butanone, CH3–CO–CH2CH3, reacts with mCPBA to give ethyl acetate, CH3–CO–O–CH2CH3. The oxygen goes in on the ethyl side. The mechanism shows how it gets there; the next section explains why it picks that side.

Step 1. An acid in the flask protonates the carbonyl oxygen. A positive oxygen pulls electrons away from the carbonyl carbon, which makes that carbon a better electrophile.

Step 2. The peroxyacid adds to the carbonyl carbon through its O–H oxygen, the terminal oxygen, not the one bonded to the C=O. A lone pair on that oxygen forms the new C–O bond. At the same time the C=O π electrons move onto the positive oxygen, which becomes an ordinary OH.

Step 3. The oxygen that just added now has three bonds and a positive charge. It loses its proton to a base in the flask.

The result is the Criegee intermediate. Its central carbon carries four single bonds: to the OH, to the two groups of the ketone, and to the O–O–C(=O)Ar group from the reagent. It has no charge.

Some courses draw steps 1 to 3 as a single step without the acid: the peroxyacid adds, and its own proton moves to the carbonyl oxygen as the new C–O bond forms. Both drawings reach the same Criegee intermediate. Use the one your course grades.

Step 4. The Criegee intermediate falls apart in one step, with three pairs of electrons moving at once:

  • A lone pair on the OH oxygen moves in to make a new C=O bond.
  • The ethyl group moves off the central carbon onto the nearer oxygen of the O–O bond. It takes its bonding pair with it.
  • The O–O bond breaks, and its electrons leave with the carboxylate.

The ethyl moves from one atom to the atom right next to it, a move called a 1,2-shift. You met the same move in Carbocations, where a hydride or a methyl shifts onto a neighboring positive carbon. There the group moves toward an empty p orbital. Here it moves toward the empty σ* orbital of the O–O bond, the antibonding orbital you met in SN2. Filling that orbital is what breaks the O–O bond.

Step 5. The new ester comes out with a proton on its C=O oxygen. The carboxylate takes that proton, and the products are ethyl acetate and the carboxylic acid ArCO2H.

The migrating group keeps its configuration

(R)-3-Methylpentan-2-one has a stereocenter right next to its C=O. With mCPBA that carbon migrates, and the product is (R)-butan-2-yl acetate. The CH3 that pointed toward you still points toward you, and the H that pointed away still points away.

The bond from the migrating carbon never breaks completely. It slides from the carbonyl carbon to the oxygen in one motion, so the carbon never becomes a free cation that could flatten out. Its other three bonds are never touched. A reaction that leaves a stereocenter’s arrangement unchanged like this goes with retention of configuration.

A stereocenter that migrates keeps its arrangement. So a Baeyer–Villiger on a single enantiomer gives a single enantiomer, and you can say which one.

In this example the R/S label also stays the same. The new O sits where the carbonyl carbon was, and both outrank every other group on the stereocenter. In other molecules the label can change even though the arrangement does not, because the priorities around the carbon change.

Which group migrates

Butanone has a methyl and an ethyl on its carbonyl carbon, and the ethyl moved. Acetophenone, which has a methyl and a phenyl ring (Ph, a benzene ring attached through one carbon), gives phenyl acetate. Methyl benzoate, which would need the methyl to move, does not form.

To read a product, find the inserted oxygen. The group on its far side, away from the C=O, is the group that moved.

A ketone has two groups and only one of them moves, so most Baeyer–Villiger questions come down to which. The groups fall into a fixed order, called their migratory aptitude, their tendency to migrate:

H > tertiary > cyclohexyl ≈ secondary ≈ benzyl ≈ aryl > primary > methyl

To place a group in that order, look at the carbon that attaches it to the C=O and count the carbons bonded to it, leaving out the carbonyl carbon itself. Ethyl’s CH2 has one other carbon, so ethyl is primary. Isopropyl’s CH has two, so it is secondary; so is cyclohexyl. tert-Butyl’s central carbon has three, so it is tertiary. Methyl has none. Aryl means a benzene ring attached directly, like the phenyl in acetophenone. Benzyl means a CH2 that carries a benzene ring.

The order has a reason. While the group is moving, its carbon is partly bonded to where it came from and partly bonded to where it is going, and in that transition state it runs short of electrons: it carries a partial positive charge. A group that is good at holding a positive charge makes that transition state easier to reach, so it moves faster. That is why the alkyl groups line up in the same order as carbocation stability: tertiary, then secondary, then primary, then methyl.

Aryl groups seem to break the pattern. A phenyl cation is very unstable, yet phenyl migrates about as well as a secondary group. The ring never has to hold the charge on its own, though. As it moves, its ipso carbon, the ring carbon bonded to the carbonyl carbon, is partly bonded to the carbon it is leaving and to the oxygen it is joining. For a moment the ring bridges the two atoms. The ring’s π electrons then spread the positive charge over its other five carbons, much as they do in a benzylic cation.

Hydrogen tops the list, and that gives the reaction a second use. An aldehyde has an H on its carbonyl carbon, and that H migrates ahead of any carbon group. So butanal and mCPBA give butanoic acid, not a formate ester. Tollens’ reagent, Jones reagent or KMnO4 give the same acid by a different mechanism (Oxidizing an aldehyde).

One exception is worth knowing. When an aromatic aldehyde carries an OH or OCH3 group on its ring, the ring can move instead of the H, and the product is then a formate ester.

Aptitude is not the only condition. The migrating group must sit anti-periplanar to the O–O bond: seen down the C–O bond, the two point in opposite directions, 180° apart, as in an E2. Only then does the filled orbital of the bond that moves line up with the empty σ* orbital of the O–O bond.

In an open-chain ketone, turning the C–O bond brings either group into that position, so aptitude alone decides. In a rigid ring system one of the groups sometimes cannot get there, and then the lower-ranked group migrates. That is why aptitude is a strong tendency rather than a law.

The most useful case is the methyl ketone. Methyl comes last in the order, so the other group moves every time, and the product is the acetate ester of that group. Butanone gives ethyl acetate and acetophenone gives phenyl acetate, as above.

Rings become lactones

Cyclohexanone and mCPBA give oxepan-2-one, a seven-membered ring that contains one oxygen. That product is a lactone: a cyclic ester, the ring-shaped relative of an ester in the way a lactam is of an amide (Esters & amides). Its common name is ε-caprolactone.

Both groups on the carbonyl carbon of a cyclic ketone are ring carbons. Whichever one moves stays in the ring and simply bonds to the new oxygen, so the ring does not open. It grows by one atom, and that atom is the oxygen. Cyclopentanone gives the six-membered lactone oxan-2-one (δ-valerolactone) in the same way.

This makes the Baeyer–Villiger one of the few reliable ways in this course to enlarge a ring by one atom.

Worked example — 2-methylcyclohexanone plus mCPBA

1. Find the two groups. C1 is the carbonyl carbon. On one side is C2, which carries a methyl. On the other side is C6, a CH2.
2. Classify them. Leave out C1 and count carbons. C2 is bonded to C3 and to the methyl, two carbons, so it is secondary. C6 is bonded only to C5, so it is primary.
3. Rank them. Secondary beats primary, so C2 migrates.
4. Insert the oxygen on that side. The new oxygen goes between C1 and C2, not between C1 and C6.
5. Count the ring. Six carbons plus one oxygen make a seven-membered lactone, 7-methyloxepan-2-one. The ring oxygen is bonded to the methyl-bearing carbon on one side and to the C=O carbon on the other.
6. Check the stereochemistry. If C2 started as a single enantiomer, it stays one. The migration keeps its arrangement, so the methyl stays on the wedge.

When the molecule also has an alkene

6-Methylhept-5-en-2-one has a methyl ketone at one end and a C=C at the other. Three carbon groups sit on that C=C. With one equivalent of mCPBA, the C=C becomes an epoxide and the ketone is left alone.

An alkene with alkyl groups on it epoxidizes much faster than a ketone undergoes the Baeyer–Villiger, so when both are present the alkene usually reacts first. A planned Baeyer–Villiger on such a molecule therefore needs the steps reordered, or the alkene protected: turned for a while into a group that mCPBA ignores, then turned back.

The balance can tip the other way. An alkene with an electron-withdrawing group on it, such as a C=O attached directly, epoxidizes slowly. A strained ketone, such as cyclobutanone, undergoes the Baeyer–Villiger unusually fast. So “the alkene reacts first” describes the usual case, not every case.

What carries forward

Very little here is new machinery. The reagent is the epoxidation reagent. The key step is the 1,2-shift from carbocation chemistry, and the migration order follows carbocation stability. The product is an ester, which you already know how to hydrolyze.

In short, a peroxyacid inserts an oxygen between the carbonyl carbon and the group best able to carry a positive charge. Methyl ketones give acetate esters of the other group, cyclic ketones give lactones one atom larger, and the migrating group keeps its configuration.

The reaction also helps when you plan a synthesis backward from the target (Synthesis & Retrosynthesis). Take tert-butyl acetate. Making it from acetic acid and tert-butyl alcohol by esterification is hard, because the tertiary alcohol is so crowded. But a tertiary group is the easiest group to migrate, so 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-one and mCPBA give it directly. When an ester looks awkward to make by esterification, check the ketone it could come from.
