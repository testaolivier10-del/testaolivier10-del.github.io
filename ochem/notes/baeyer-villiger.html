Baeyer–Villiger oxidation

Practice this — interactive lesson

Almost every reaction of a ketone in this course happens at the carbonyl carbon or at the α carbon. The Baeyer–Villiger oxidation does something else: it inserts an oxygen atom into a carbon–carbon bond, turning a ketone into an ester.

R–CO–R′  +  mCPBA  →  R–CO–O–R′

The reagent is a peroxyacid, most often mCPBA — the same reagent that turns alkenes into epoxides in Alkenes & Alkynes. A peroxyacid carries an O–O bond with a good leaving group on the far side, which is the structural feature both reactions exploit.

The mechanism, and where the interesting step is

  • The peroxyacid is itself an acid, and it protonates the ketone’s carbonyl oxygen. That makes the carbonyl carbon much more electrophilic, and the peroxyacid’s terminal oxygen — the OH end of the O–O, not the one attached to the acyl group — adds to it. After a proton shift you have a tetrahedral carbon bearing an OH and an O–O–C(=O)R group: the Criegee intermediate.
  • That intermediate collapses by migration: one of the two groups on the original carbonyl carbon moves, with its bonding pair, onto the adjacent oxygen. The O–O bond breaks at the same time and a carboxylate leaves.

The migration is the step worth understanding. A group leaves one carbon and arrives at an oxygen without ever being free, taking its electrons with it — a 1,2-shift, the same motion that rearranges a carbocation, running here on a neutral intermediate.

The migrating group keeps its configuration. Because the bond never breaks fully, a stereocenter that migrates arrives with its arrangement intact. That is the fact worth remembering, because it makes the reaction usable on a chiral substrate where almost nothing else is.

Which group migrates

A ketone has two groups and only one moves, so a question about a Baeyer–Villiger is nearly always a question about which. The order is:

H > tertiary > cyclohexyl ≈ secondary ≈ benzyl ≈ aryl > primary > methyl

The reason is that the migrating carbon carries partial positive character in the transition state, so the group best able to support a positive charge migrates. For the alkyl groups that is the carbocation stability order arriving from a different direction. Aryl is the case where the shortcut strains: a phenyl cation is badly unstable, yet aryl migrates well, because the transition state is bridged rather than a free cation and the ring stabilizes it at the ipso carbon.

Hydrogen at the head of that list is not a curiosity, it is a whole substrate class. An aldehyde has an H on the carbonyl carbon, so the H migrates and the product is a carboxylic acid — the same answer you would get from Tollens, Jones or KMnO4, reached by a completely different mechanism. Put mCPBA on an aldehyde and the answer is the acid, not an ester.

One geometric condition rides along with the ranking. The group that migrates has to be anti-periplanar to the O–O bond in the Criegee intermediate, because the filled C–C orbital and the empty O–O antibonding orbital have to overlap. In a flexible acyclic ketone any group can rotate into that alignment, so aptitude decides on its own. In a rigid ring one of them sometimes cannot, and then the “wrong” group migrates — which is why aptitude is a strong tendency rather than a law.

The practical consequence is a rule you can use without recalling the whole series: with a methyl ketone, the methyl essentially never migrates, so the other group does and the product is the acetate ester of that group. Methyl is the worst migrator in the list, which makes methyl ketones the most predictable substrates.

Rings become lactones

Run it on a cyclic ketone and the migration is intramolecular, so the ring does not open — it expands by one atom, and that atom is oxygen. A lactone is simply a cyclic ester, the C(=O)–O linkage closed into a ring and the ester counterpart of a lactam. A cyclohexanone gives a seven-membered lactone, a cyclopentanone gives a six-membered one.

This is one of the few reliable ring-expansion reactions at this level, and the only one that puts a heteroatom into the ring while doing it.

Worked example — 2-methylcyclohexanone plus mCPBA.
1. Identify the two groups on the carbonyl carbon. C1 is the carbonyl. On one side is C2, which carries a methyl, so that carbon is secondary. On the other is C6, a plain CH2, so that one is primary.
2. Rank them. Secondary beats primary, so C2 migrates.
3. Insert the oxygen on that side. The new oxygen goes between C1 and C2, not between C1 and C6.
4. Count the ring. Six carbons plus one oxygen: a seven-membered lactone, with the ring oxygen attached to the methyl-bearing carbon and the C=O on its other side.
5. Check the stereochemistry. If C2 was a single enantiomer it stays one, because the migration retains configuration — the spatial arrangement at that carbon is untouched, even though the R/S label can flip when the priorities around it change.

Selectivity against an alkene

mCPBA epoxidizes alkenes, so a substrate with both a ketone and an alkene has a competition. The alkene usually wins, because epoxidation is faster — which means a Baeyer–Villiger on such a substrate needs the alkene protected or the sequence reordered.

Note how much this section reuses. The reagent is the epoxidation reagent, the migration is the carbocation 1,2-shift, the migratory aptitude order is the carbocation stability order, and the product is an ester you already know how to hydrolyze. Nothing here is new machinery; it is familiar machinery pointed at a bond nobody else touches.

What carries forward

A peroxyacid inserts an oxygen between the carbonyl carbon and the group best able to carry positive charge. Methyl ketones give acetate esters of the other group, cyclic ketones give lactones one atom larger, and the migrating group keeps its stereochemistry. Retrosynthetically, an ester whose two halves look like an awkward esterification is worth checking against the ketone it would come from.
