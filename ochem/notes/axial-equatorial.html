Alkanes & Conformations · Section 25 of 121

Axial/equatorial

Practice this — interactive lesson

Draw methylcyclohexane as a chair and you have to decide where the methyl goes. Each ring carbon offers two positions, and they are not alike: one points straight up or down, and the other points out from the ring. The choice changes the molecule's energy by an amount you can measure. This section shows how to find both positions on a chair drawing, why one of them costs energy, and how to put a number on that cost. It builds on the chair from Cyclohexanes.

Two positions per carbon

Start with cyclohexane itself. Each of its six carbons bonds to two ring carbons and to two hydrogens, so a chair carries twelve hydrogens. They fall into two sets of six.

An axial bond points straight up or straight down. It runs parallel to the ring's axis, the imaginary line through the middle of the ring at right angles to it. An equatorial bond points outward around the rim of the ring, its "equator," and tilts slightly up or down.

The axial bonds alternate as you go round the ring. If C1's axial bond points up, C2's points down, C3's points up, and so on. So C1, C3 and C5 have their axial bonds on the top face of the ring, and C2, C4 and C6 have theirs on the bottom face.

Each carbon's equatorial bond tilts the opposite way from its own axial bond. On C1 the axial bond points up, so the equatorial bond angles slightly down. On C2 it is the reverse: the axial bond points down and the equatorial bond angles slightly up.

Two habits make a chair drawing come out right, and they are the reliable way to draw them. Draw every axial bond exactly vertical, alternating in direction. Then draw each equatorial bond parallel to the ring bonds one carbon along: C1's equatorial bond runs parallel to the C2–C3 and C5–C6 bonds, and every other carbon follows the same pattern.

1,3-diaxial interactions

Now put a methyl group on C1 in the axial position, pointing up. The axial bonds on C3 and C5 point up as well, and each carries a hydrogen. All three axial groups rise from the top face, close enough to crowd each other. That steric clash is a 1,3-diaxial interaction: steric strain between two axial groups on the same face, two carbons apart. (C5 is also two carbons from C1, counting the other way round.) An axial methyl has two of them, one with the axial hydrogen on C3 and one with the axial hydrogen on C5.

Put the methyl in the equatorial position instead and it points out, away from the ring. Nothing else on the ring reaches into that space, so an equatorial methyl has no 1,3-diaxial interactions. A hydrogen now takes C1's axial position, and the small hydrogen-to-hydrogen contacts it makes are present in plain cyclohexane too.

Each 1,3-diaxial contact between a methyl and a hydrogen costs about 0.9 kcal/mol. That is the gauche interaction in butane from Newman projections, and a Newman projection down a ring bond shows why. Sight down the C1–C2 bond. An axial methyl on C1 sits 60° from C3, the next ring carbon, just as the two methyls of gauche butane sit 60° apart. An equatorial methyl sits 180° from C3, which is the anti arrangement.

The C1–C6 bond gives the same picture with C5, so an axial methyl carries two gauche interactions, about 2 × 0.9 = 1.8 kcal/mol. Unlike butane's central bond, the ring bonds cannot turn freely, so the chair keeps an axial methyl gauche to C3 and C5.

A-values: putting a number on it

Methylcyclohexane can take either chair shape drawn above, with the methyl axial or with the methyl equatorial. Both have the same atoms bonded in the same order, so they are the same compound in two shapes. That makes them two conformations of methylcyclohexane, in the sense of Newman projections. Like the anti and gauche conformations of butane, both are present at once, and the lower-energy one is the more common.

The equatorial chair is lower in energy by 1.7 kcal/mol, close to the 1.8 that the two gauche interactions predict. At room temperature that gap puts about 95% of the molecules in the equatorial chair.

Looking ahead: how the ring gets from one chair to the other is the subject of the next section, Ring flips.

The A-value of a group is that energy gap: how much lower in energy, in kcal/mol, the chair with the group equatorial is than the chair with the group axial. A-values are measured, and each one converts directly into an equilibrium ratio.

The chart shows first that an A-value follows a group's width, not its mass. Chlorine is more than twice as heavy as a methyl group, yet its A-value is less than a third as large: 0.5 against 1.7. A chlorine atom sits at the end of a long C–Cl bond and presents a smooth sphere to the ring, while a methyl group fans three hydrogens out sideways.

The chart also shows a large jump from isopropyl (2.2) to tert-butyl (4.9). An axial isopropyl group can turn so that its one C–H bond points at the ring and its two methyls point away. A tert-butyl group has no C–H bond on that carbon, so whichever way it turns, a methyl points at the axial hydrogens.

Worked example — turning an A-value into a ratio

An energy gap and an equilibrium ratio carry the same information. The equation that converts one into the other is ΔG = −RT ln K, or K = e−ΔG/RT run the other way. Here K is the ratio of equatorial to axial molecules, and ΔG is the energy change on going from the axial chair to the equatorial chair, which is minus the A-value.

R is the gas constant and T the absolute temperature. At 298 K their product RT is about 0.59 kcal/mol, and that one number is all you need to carry. Because K depends exponentially on ΔG, each extra 0.59 kcal/mol multiplies the ratio by e, about 2.7.

For methylcyclohexane the A-value is 1.7 kcal/mol, so K = e1.7/0.59 ≈ 18. The equilibrium is about 18:1 in favor of the equatorial chair, or roughly 95:5.

For tert-butylcyclohexane the A-value is 4.9 kcal/mol, so K = e4.9/0.59 ≈ 4000, which is better than 99.97% equatorial. The energy gap is about three times larger, but the ratio is more than two hundred times larger, because the relationship is exponential.

A tert-butyl group is so strongly equatorial, over 99.9%, that chemists use it as a conformational anchor. The chair with the tert-butyl axial still exists, but it is so rare that it never matters, so the group effectively holds the ring in one chair. A chemist can then study a single, known conformation instead of a mixture of two. Much of what is known about how axial and equatorial groups react differently was measured this way.

Cis and trans on a ring

Two groups on a ring are cis when they sit on the same face and trans when they sit on opposite faces, as Rings & unsaturation showed. A flat hexagon shows the face with the wedge-and-hash notation from Molecular geometry: a wedge comes up out of the page toward you, and a hash goes down behind it. Two wedges, or two hashes, is cis; one of each is trans.

Cis or trans is a fact about the compound, not about the drawing or the conformation. Moving a group to the other face would mean breaking a bond and making a new one. So a cis ring and a trans ring are two different compounds that can be separated and bottled, while the two chairs of methylcyclohexane are one compound. Stereochemistry names these arrangements in full later; the up/down reading is all this chapter needs.

Now look at the chair in the figure. C1's axial bond points up, so on C1 the bond that points up is the axial one. C2's axial bond points down, so on C2 the bond that points up is the equatorial one. On neighboring carbons, the up bonds are always of different kinds.

"Up" and "axial" are not the same thing. Up or down tells you which face of the ring a group is on, and that is fixed for the compound. Axial or equatorial tells you which kind of bond the group sits on in one particular chair. A group that points up may be axial or equatorial, depending on which carbon it is on and which chair the ring is in.

When a group has to be axial

Take the two isomers of 1,2-dimethylcyclohexane. In the trans isomer one methyl is up and the other is down. C1's up bond and C2's down bond are the same kind, because the two carbons' axial bonds point opposite ways. So a trans-1,2 ring can take a chair with both methyls equatorial, or a chair with both methyls axial.

In the cis isomer both methyls are up. The up bonds on neighboring carbons are of different kinds, so one methyl is axial and the other is equatorial in every chair. A cis-1,2 ring cannot put both groups equatorial.

When one group has to be axial, the ring favors the chair that puts the smaller group there. In cis-1-tert-butyl-2-methylcyclohexane, an axial tert-butyl group would cost about 4.9 kcal/mol and an axial methyl about 1.7. So the chair with the methyl axial and the tert-butyl equatorial is by far the more common one.

What carries forward

Axial and equatorial are the vocabulary for the rest of this chapter. The next section, Ring flips, shows how a ring moves from one chair to the other and what that does to each position. The section after it, Conformational analysis, uses A-values to decide which chair wins on rings with several substituents.

Looking ahead: size is not the only factor. On a sugar ring, an electronegative group on the carbon next to the ring oxygen often prefers the axial position for electronic reasons. This is the anomeric effect, which Biomolecules covers. When you meet it, the A-value table is not wrong; a second effect is at work.

Further on, Substitution & Elimination meets a reaction, E2 elimination, that on a ring works only when the group being removed from the ring is axial. It is the clearest case of a conformation deciding whether a reaction can happen at all, not just the molecule's shape.
