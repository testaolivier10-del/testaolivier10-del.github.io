Two positions per carbon

Every carbon in a cyclohexane chair has two substituent positions: axial (pointing straight up or down, parallel to the ring's central axis) and equatorial (pointing outward, roughly along the "equator" of the ring). Around the ring, axial positions alternate up-down-up-down-up-down, and each carbon's equatorial position points slightly up or down to balance it.

1,3-diaxial interactions

Two axial substituents on the same face of the ring, three carbons apart (positions 1 and 3, or 1 and 5 — same thing by symmetry), point toward each other and can clash sterically. This steric clash is called a 1,3-diaxial interaction, and it's the direct ring version of the gauche-butane steric strain from the Newman Projections lesson.

Equatorial substituents point outward and away from the ring, so they almost never experience 1,3-diaxial clashes. This is why bulkier groups strongly prefer the equatorial position.

A-values: quantifying the preference

The A-value of a substituent is the energy (in kcal/mol) that group prefers equatorial over axial. Methyl has an A-value of about 1.7 kcal/mol (translating to roughly a 95:5 equatorial:axial ratio at room temperature). tert-Butyl has an A-value of about 4.7–5+ kcal/mol — so large that a tert-butyl-substituted cyclohexane is essentially locked with that group equatorial, over 99.9% of the time.
