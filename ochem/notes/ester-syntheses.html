Malonic and acetoacetic ester

Practice this — interactive lesson

Say you want butanoic acid, CH3CH2CH2COOH, and you plan to make it by putting an ethyl group on the α carbon of acetic acid. The α carbon is the carbon bonded to the C=O. The obvious starting ester is ethyl acetate, but its α hydrogens are hard to remove: their pKa is near 25. Only a very strong base, such as LDA (lithium diisopropylamide), removes them fully, and a strong base can attack the ester carbonyl instead.

Diethyl malonate gets round this. Its CH2 sits between two ester carbonyls, so sodium ethoxide removes one of its hydrogens easily. That carbon takes the ethyl group. Later, one of the two esters is removed completely, as CO2, and butanoic acid is left.

The carbonyl that leaves was never wanted in the product. It was there only to make one hydrogen acidic. A group put into a molecule to make one step possible, and removed once that step is done, is called an activating group. This is the clearest example of one in the course.

This section covers two sequences built on that idea. The malonic ester synthesis starts from diethyl malonate and makes carboxylic acids. The acetoacetic ester synthesis starts from ethyl acetoacetate and makes methyl ketones.

Why two carbonyls

Compare the three esters in the figure. In ethyl acetate the α carbon touches one carbonyl. In diethyl malonate and ethyl acetoacetate it sits between two. When a base removes a hydrogen from that carbon, the negative charge spreads onto both carbonyl oxygens instead of one. A more spread-out charge means a more stable anion, and so a more acidic hydrogen. Alpha hydrogens & enolates draws the resonance forms of this anion.

Each pKa unit is a factor of ten. So 13 against 25 is twelve units, which makes the malonate hydrogen about 1012 times more acidic. That is the difference between needing LDA at −78 °C and needing the alkoxide that matches your solvent.

Malonic ester synthesis → a carboxylic acid

Here is the whole route to butanoic acid, one step per panel of the figure below.

  • NaOEt removes one α hydrogen (panel 1). Use the alkoxide that matches the ester: ethoxide with an ethyl ester, methoxide with a methyl one. A different alkoxide swaps itself into the ester (transesterification) and turns the starting material into a mixture.
  • CH3CH2Br is attacked by the anion in an SN2 (panel 2). The lone pair on the α carbon forms a bond to the CH2 of the halide, and bromide leaves. The product, diethyl ethylmalonate, still has one α hydrogen (panel 3).
  • H3O+, heat hydrolyzes both esters to COOH groups (panel 4). The product, ethylmalonic acid, has two COOH groups on one carbon. It is a 1,3-diacid: number its three-carbon backbone and the two acid carbons are C1 and C3.
  • The same heating then removes one COOH as CO2 (panels 5 and 6). Losing CO2 this way is called decarboxylation. The next section explains how it works.

The figure draws the anion with its negative charge on carbon, because carbon is the atom that forms the new bond. In the real anion that charge is shared with both carbonyl oxygens.

Some courses write the hydrolysis as two operations: NaOH in water to saponify the esters, then H3O+ and heat. That comes to the same thing, because the acid has to be free before it can decarboxylate.

The general product is R–CH2–COOH, where R is the group from the halide. Malonate's CH2 has two acidic hydrogens, so you can repeat NaOEt and a second halide, R′–X, before the hydrolysis. That gives R(R′)CH–COOH. Two groups is the limit.

Why the decarboxylation works

Ordinary carboxylic acids survive heating. Ethylmalonic acid does not. Look at panel 5 above. The molecule curls so that six atoms form a ring: the H of one COOH, that COOH's oxygen and carbon, the α carbon, and the other carboxyl's carbon and C=O oxygen.

Three pairs of electrons then move at once, all the same way round that ring:

  • The C=O π electrons of the carboxyl that stays reach the H and make a new O–H bond.
  • The old O–H electrons become the second C=O bond of CO2.
  • The C–C bond from the α carbon to the leaving carboxyl becomes a C=C.

CO2 leaves as a gas. What remains is an enol: a C=C with an OH on one of its carbons. The enol quickly shifts to the more stable C=O form, and the α carbon picks up a hydrogen as it does (panel 6). That shift is the keto–enol tautomerization from Alpha hydrogens & enolates.

You met this ring for β-keto acids in Carboxylic acids. It needs a second carbonyl β to the carboxyl, which means bonded to the α carbon. That carbonyl can be a ketone or another carboxyl. The acetoacetic route passes through a true β-keto acid. The malonic route passes through a substituted malonic acid, which is a 1,3-diacid, not a keto acid. Same six-membered ring, same outcome, two different names.

A carbonyl in any other position does not work. The electrons of the breaking C–C bond must flow into a C=O right next to them. In an α-keto acid the ring would have only five atoms, and in a γ-keto acid seven. In neither is there a C=O beside the α carbon to take the electrons, and a plain carboxylic acid has none at all.

This is also why the esters must be hydrolyzed first. The ring closes through the carboxyl O–H, and an ester has no O–H.

Two alkylations, one dihalide: a ring

Treat diethyl malonate with two equivalents of NaOEt and one of 1,4-dibromobutane, Br–CH2CH2CH2CH2–Br.

The first alkylation uses one end of the chain (panel 1 below). The α carbon now carries a four-carbon chain with a bromine on its far end.

The second NaOEt removes the last α hydrogen. The new anion attacks the far end of its own chain, and bromide leaves (panel 2). Both ends of this SN2 are in one molecule, so it is intramolecular, and it closes a ring.

Hydrolysis and heat then give cyclopentanecarboxylic acid (panel 3).

Count the ring before you answer. It holds the malonate α carbon plus every carbon of the dihalide chain. So 1,4-dibromobutane gives a five-membered ring, and 1,5-dibromopentane gives a six.

Acetoacetic ester synthesis → a methyl ketone

Ethyl acetoacetate, CH3COCH2CO2Et, has a ketone on one side of its CH2 and an ester on the other. It is the product of the Claisen condensation of ethyl acetate (Claisen reactions).

Run the same steps on it with ethyl bromide. NaOEt, then CH3CH2Br, puts the ethyl on the CH2 (panel 1 below). H3O+ hydrolyzes the ester to a COOH, which makes a β-keto acid. Heat removes that COOH as CO2 through the same six-atom ring, but this time the ketone oxygen takes the H (panel 2). The enol then becomes pentan-2-one (panel 3).

The ketone survives because it cannot be hydrolyzed. Only an ester has an OR group that water can replace with OH, so only the ester side becomes a carboxyl and leaves.

The general product is a methyl ketone, R–CH2–CO–CH3: a ketone with a CH3 on one side. The CH3–CO and the CH2 come from acetoacetate, and R comes from the halide. As with malonate, the CH2 can take two groups.

One starting material decides the product class. Malonate has two esters, loses one carboxyl and keeps the other as the acid. Acetoacetate has one ester and one ketone, loses the ester's carboxyl and keeps the ketone. Learn which compound goes with which product, and the rest of both sequences is identical.

The SN2 limit, which is the real constraint

Try diethyl malonate with tert-butyl bromide, (CH3)3C–Br. The carbon that holds the bromine carries three methyl groups, which block the back-side approach an SN2 needs (SN2). So the anion takes a hydrogen from one of the methyls instead. That C–H bond becomes a C=C, and bromide leaves. This is an E2 elimination (E2). You get 2-methylpropene, and the malonate back unchanged.

The general rule follows. The alkylation is an SN2 by a stabilized anion, so the halide must be methyl, primary, allylic or benzylic. Allylic means the carbon next to a C=C; benzylic means the carbon next to a benzene ring. A secondary halide reacts slowly, and elimination competes. A tertiary halide gives only elimination, because the anion is a base as well as a nucleophile.

So a question asking for a carboxylic acid with a tertiary group on the α carbon cannot be answered with a malonic ester synthesis, however neat the disconnection looks. Check the halide, not the target.

Reading a target backwards

Worked example — make 4-methylpentanoic acid, (CH3)2CH–CH2–CH2–COOH

Find the α carbon. It is the CH2 next to the COOH. It carries two hydrogens and one substituent, so this needs one alkylation.

Cut the bond from it to its substituent (top of the figure). The piece that keeps the COOH, CH2–COOH, is what malonate supplies. The other piece, –CH2CH(CH3)2 (isobutyl), names the halide: 1-bromo-2-methylpropane.

Check the halide. It is primary. It branches at the next carbon along, so its SN2 is slower than an unbranched primary halide's. It is not secondary, though, and it will run.

Write the sequence. (1) NaOEt, EtOH; (2) (CH3)2CHCH2Br; (3) H3O+, heat.

Check the trap. 3,3-Dimethylbutanoic acid, (CH3)3C–CH2–COOH, differs only in where the branching sits. Its cut hands you a tert-butyl group (bottom of the figure), so it would need tert-butyl bromide. That halide is tertiary, so this target has no route. Two targets look almost identical, and only one can be made. That is why the check goes on the halide.

The general method: find the α carbon of the target acid. It must still carry at least one hydrogen, because the decarboxylation puts one there. Cut the bond from it to each substituent. Each cut names one alkyl halide, and the CH2–COOH left over is always malonate's. A methyl ketone reads the same way: cut the substituents off the carbon next to the C=O on the side away from the CH3, and CH2–CO–CH3 is left for acetoacetate.

What carries forward

Deprotonate, alkylate, hydrolyze, decarboxylate. Malonic ester gives a carboxylic acid; acetoacetic ester gives a methyl ketone; and the SN2 limits both. When a target is a carboxylic acid or a methyl ketone with a substituent on the α carbon, look for the halide that installed it.
