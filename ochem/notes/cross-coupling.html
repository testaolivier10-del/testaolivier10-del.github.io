Organometallics · Section 83 of 121

Palladium cross-coupling

Practice this — interactive lesson

Every carbon nucleophile so far has needed an electrophilic partner that could be attacked — a carbonyl, or an alkyl halide willing to do SN2. That leaves out the two partners organic chemists most often want to join: an aryl halide — a halogen attached directly to a benzene ring, whose chemistry proper belongs to Aromatic Chemistry, later — and another aryl group. Aryl halides do not do SN2 at all — the backside of that carbon is behind the ring — and an aryl cation is far too unstable for SN1. (There is one separate route by which an aryl halide can be substituted, an addition–elimination that needs strong electron-withdrawing groups on the ring; Aromatic Follow-Through covers it. It is not something the halide does on its own.) Aryl Grignards, meanwhile, are too indiscriminate to be pointed at one site in a complicated molecule.

Cross-coupling solves this with a transition metal — almost always palladium — that takes the two pieces in one at a time, holds both, and then pushes them together. The 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Heck, Negishi and Suzuki for it, and it is now the most-used carbon–carbon bond-forming reaction in the pharmaceutical industry.

One catalytic cycle, three steps

The reactions below differ in what the nucleophilic partner is, but the palladium does the same three things every time. Learn the cycle once and the named reactions stop needing to be memorized separately.

StepWhat happensPalladium goes
Oxidative additionPd inserts into the Ar–X bond, ending up bonded to both piecesPd(0) → Pd(II)
TransmetalationThe R group moves from its own metal onto the palladiumstays Pd(II)
Reductive eliminationThe two organic groups on Pd join and leave as Ar–RPd(II) → Pd(0)

The names describe the palladium's oxidation state, which is the easiest way to keep them straight. Oxidative addition oxidizes the metal by two; reductive elimination reduces it by two and hands back the Pd(0) the cycle started with. That is why palladium is a catalyst rather than a reagent: it ends each turn exactly as it began, and a few mole percent turns over thousands of times.

In practice the palladium arrives as Pd(PPh3)4, or as Pd(OAc)2 with a phosphine ligand added to it, at a loading of a few mole percent. Oxidative addition is the step that sets the scope of the halide: Ar–I > Ar–OTf > Ar–Br >> Ar–Cl, because it is the C–X bond the metal has to break, and the C–Cl bond is much the strongest of them. Iodides and triflates go easily, bromides go well, chlorides are slow and need a specially designed bulky ligand. If a question hands you a choice of halide on the same ring, it is asking about this step.

Notice what the metal is doing that no nucleophile could. It makes the aryl halide reactive by bonding to it directly, rather than waiting to be attacked. That is the whole reason an aryl halide — inert to SN1 and SN2 alike — becomes a usable partner.

The named reactions, by what the partner is

ReactionNucleophilic partnerForms
SuzukiBoronic acid, R–B(OH)2, with baseBiaryls, most often
StilleOrganostannane, R–SnBu3The same, with no base needed
NegishiOrganozinc, R–ZnXThe same, and tolerates esters
SonogashiraTerminal alkyne, with a copper cocatalyst and an amine baseAn aryl alkyne
HeckAn alkene — no organometallic at allA substituted alkene

The Suzuki coupling is the one to know properly. Boronic acids are air-stable, water-tolerant, low in toxicity and easy to store, which is why it displaced the alternatives industrially even though the Stille coupling works at least as well chemically — organotin reagents are seriously toxic. The base is not optional: it converts the boronic acid to a borate, which is what transfers the R group in the transmetalation step.

The Sonogashira is the one with two metals and a base, and the base is not decoration. Copper(I) cannot hand an alkynyl group to palladium until it has one, and it gets one by forming the copper acetylide from the terminal alkyne — a C–H of pKa 25, which takes a base, exactly as when an acetylide was made earlier in this chapter. That base is an amine, usually triethylamine, often in enough excess to be the solvent as well. So there are two cycles turning at once: a copper cycle that makes the acetylide, and the palladium cycle that the acetylide transmetalates into.

The Heck reaction is the odd one out and worth separating. There is no organometallic nucleophile, so there is no transmetalation. Instead the alkene coordinates side-on to the palladium, and then migratory insertion puts the aryl group and the palladium across the double bond — Ar on one carbon, Pd on the other, both delivered to the same face. A β-hydride elimination then releases the product alkene, which is usually the trans isomer, with the new bond at the less substituted carbon.

What is left on the metal once the alkene departs is H–Pd(II)–X, and that is not the catalyst. A base — triethylamine, or a carbonate — removes HX from it and hands back Pd(0). So the Heck needs a full stoichiometric equivalent of base even though nothing in the substrate is ever deprotonated, and the by-product is the ammonium or carbonate salt. Track the oxidation states and the cycle only closes because of that last step.

Why the partners are aryl and vinyl, and not alkyl

Every electrophile in this section is an aryl or a vinyl halide, and that is not a coincidence or a matter of taste. Put an sp3 alkyl group on palladium and a competing reaction opens up. If that group carries a hydrogen on the carbon next to the one bonded to the metal — the β carbon — the palladium can take that hydrogen and let the rest leave as an alkene. This is β-hydride elimination: Pd–CH2CH2R becomes H–Pd plus CH2=CHR. It is usually faster than the reductive elimination you were hoping for, so an attempted alkyl coupling returns an alkene and unreacted partner rather than a product.

An aryl or a vinyl group has no sp3 β hydrogen to lose — its neighbors are all part of the same flat π system — so it sits on the metal until reductive elimination gets to it. That, and not the halide's leaving-group ability, is why cross-coupling is an aryl and vinyl reaction.

Read the Heck the other way round and the same step is the point rather than the problem. There the alkyl–palladium intermediate is meant to eliminate, because that is how the product leaves the metal — which is exactly why the Heck gives you an alkene and the other four give you a single bond.
Worked example — 4-acetylbiphenyl by a Suzuki coupling

Step 1 — cut the bond you cannot otherwise make. The molecule is a biaryl: two benzene rings joined directly by a single bond, with an acetyl group para on one of them. Working backwards from a target by cutting a bond and asking what two pieces would form it is called a disconnection, and Synthesis & Retrosynthesis makes a method of it; here one cut is enough. Nothing in any earlier chapter joins two rings this way, so cut that bond: what you need is an aryl halide and an aryl metal.

Step 2 — decide which half carries which. Put the halide on the ring that already carries the ketone (4′-bromoacetophenone) and the boron on plain benzene (phenylboronic acid). Either assignment works chemically; this one uses the cheaper, commercially stocked boronic acid.

Step 3 — check the functional groups. There is a ketone in the molecule. A phenyl Grignard would add to it and you would isolate a tertiary alcohol; a boronic acid will not, because its aryl group is never a free carbanion at any point.

Step 4 — write the conditions. Pd(PPh3)4 (2 mol %), Na2CO3 (aq), toluene/ethanol, heat.

Step 5 — trace the cycle and the oxidation states. Pd(0) + Ar–Br → Ar–Pd(II)–Br (oxidative addition, 0 to II). Carbonate adds to the boron to give the borate [PhB(OH)3]−, and only that anionic boron hands its phenyl to palladium (transmetalation, still II). Ar–Pd(II)–Ph → Ar–Ph + Pd(0) (reductive elimination, II back to 0). The ketone is untouched from start to finish, and the palladium ends where it began.

Why this changed how molecules get made

Cross-coupling is convergent. Two halves are built separately and joined near the end, instead of one chain being extended step by step. Since yields multiply, joining two five-step pieces at the end beats a linear ten-step route badly — and both halves can be varied independently, which is exactly what a medicinal chemistry program needs when it wants fifty analogs of one scaffold.

The functional group tolerance is the other half of the argument. A Suzuki coupling runs in the presence of ketones, esters, free alcohols and amines, none of which a Grignard would survive. The reactive species is bound to a metal for its entire lifetime and never exists as a free carbanion.

What carries forward

A palladium catalyst joins an aryl or vinyl halide to a carbon partner by oxidative addition, transmetalation and reductive elimination, and the named reactions differ only in what the partner is. Suzuki with a boronic acid is the default; Heck is the one with no organometallic and a β-hydride elimination at the end. And when you reach Synthesis & Retrosynthesis, a bond between two rings is a cut you should see immediately, because this is the reaction that makes it.
