Carbonyl & Enolate Breadth · Section 105 of 117

Michael and Robinson

Practice this — interactive lesson

An α,β-unsaturated carbonyl has two electrophilic carbons, and which one a nucleophile picks is decided by the nucleophile rather than by the substrate. You met this with cuprates; here the nucleophile is an enolate, and the 1,4 version of the reaction has its own name.

The Michael reaction

A Michael addition is the conjugate (1,4) addition of a stabilized enolate to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl. Two names worth keeping straight:

  • The donor is the nucleophile — the compound that gets deprotonated.
  • The acceptor is the enone, also called a Michael acceptor.

The donor is almost always a compound with an α hydrogen flanked by two electron-withdrawing groups: a 1,3-diketone, a β-keto ester, a malonate. Those have pKa values around 9–13 rather than 20, so an alkoxide is enough to deprotonate them completely, and the resulting enolate is stabilized enough to be soft — meaning its charge is spread out and diffuse rather than piled on one carbon, so it is steered by orbital overlap rather than by electrostatics, and the largest LUMO coefficient of an enone sits on the β carbon. That is what sends it 1,4 rather than 1,2.

The pattern down the left is the same every time: a C–H flanked by groups that can take the charge. The pattern down the right is the same every time too: a C=C conjugated to something electron-withdrawing, which is the only reason the β carbon is electrophilic at all. Thiols and secondary amines are good donors as well — that version is how cysteine and lysine side chains get modified in biology — but for exam purposes the carbon donors are the list.

The mechanism is three moves and worth running once. The base takes the doubly activated hydrogen to give the stabilized enolate. That enolate’s α carbon attacks the β carbon of the acceptor, and the electrons run up through the C=C and onto the carbonyl oxygen — so what forms first is not a ketone but an enolate of one. Protonating that enolate on oxygen gives an enol, which tautomerizes to the 1,5-dicarbonyl; protonating it on carbon arrives there directly. The move that is easiest to draw wrong is the second: one arrow runs from the donor’s α carbon to the acceptor’s β carbon, and a second arrow has to carry the π electrons on to the oxygen, or you have left a carbon with five bonds.

Recognize the product by counting. Conjugate addition joins the donor's α carbon to the acceptor's β carbon, which puts the two carbonyls 1,5 to each other. A 1,5-dicarbonyl is the Michael retron: see one, and disconnect it back to an enolate and an enone.

Compare that with an aldol, which gives a β-hydroxy carbonyl, and a Claisen, which gives a 1,3-dicarbonyl. Three condensations, three spacings — and the spacing in the product tells you which reaction made it.

Why a doubly stabilized donor

Two reasons, and both matter in practice.

The first is control. A simple ketone enolate made with LDA at −78 °C is hard, charge-dense and irreversible, and it tends to add 1,2, giving an alkoxide and then an alcohol. A doubly stabilized enolate has its charge spread over two carbonyls, is softer, and adds 1,4. But the conditions matter as much as the donor does: under catalytic alkoxide at room temperature every step is reversible, so even a simple ketone enolate ends up adding 1,4, because the conjugate adduct is the more stable product and the reaction has time to find it. That is why the classic Robinson — cyclohexanone plus methyl vinyl ketone with NaOEt — works with an ordinary ketone as the donor. A doubly stabilized donor is what makes conjugate addition reliable, not what makes it possible.

The second is clean deprotonation. At pKa 9–13 the donor is fully deprotonated by a catalytic alkoxide, so you never have a strong base present that could attack the acceptor's carbonyl directly.

The Robinson annulation

Put a Michael addition and an intramolecular aldol condensation back to back and you build a ring. That is the Robinson annulation, and it is the standard way to build a new six-membered ring bearing an enone.

  • Step 1 — Michael. The donor's α carbon adds 1,4 to the acceptor, giving a 1,5-dicarbonyl.
  • Step 2 — intramolecular aldol. An enolate formed on one side attacks the other carbonyl. In a 1,5-dicarbonyl the geometry that reaches is the one that closes a six-membered ring.
  • Step 3 — dehydration. The β-hydroxy ketone loses water to give a conjugated enone, which is what makes the whole thing irreversible.

The product is a cyclohexenone in every case you will meet. Methyl vinyl ketone is the classic acceptor, because it is cheap and its product ring is the one steroid chemistry needed.

Why six? Count it rather than assume it. An aldol joins an α carbon to a carbonyl carbon — never two carbonyl carbons — so from a 1,5-dicarbonyl the productive closure runs from the α′ carbon beyond one carbonyl to the far carbonyl carbon. Number the chain from that far carbonyl and the new bond arrives at the sixth atom. The only other closure a 1,5-dicarbonyl can attempt runs from an inner α carbon to the carbonyl two positions away, and that would make a four-membered ring, which never competes with a six. The ring size was fixed by the spacing before the aldol had any say.

Working backwards from a cyclohexenone

The retrosynthesis runs in reverse order and is worth practicing as a drill:

  • Undo the dehydration: put the water back, giving a β-hydroxy ketone.
  • Undo the aldol: break the bond between the α carbon and the carbinol carbon, opening the ring to a 1,5-dicarbonyl.
  • Undo the Michael: disconnect at the bond β to one carbonyl, giving the donor and the enone.

Every cyclohexenone in an exam question should make you check for this. If the ring can be opened to a 1,5-dicarbonyl, a Robinson annulation made it.

Worked example — 2-methylcyclohexane-1,3-dione plus methyl vinyl ketone. This is the canonical Robinson, and its product, the Wieland–Miescher ketone, is where a great deal of steroid synthesis starts.
1. Find the donor carbon. C2 of the dione is the carbon flanked by both carbonyls, so it is the one an alkoxide deprotonates. It already carries the methyl, which is why that methyl ends up quaternary.
2. Michael. C2 adds to the β carbon of MVK — the terminal CH2 — giving 2-methyl-2-(3-oxobutyl)cyclohexane-1,3-dione: the ring, plus a –CH2CH2COCH3 arm on C2.
3. Choose the aldol partners. Two carbons could serve as the nucleophile, and counting alone does not separate them, because both close six atoms. The productive one is the methyl of the side-chain ketone, reaching round to one of the two ring carbonyls: count that loop — the methyl carbon, the side-chain carbonyl carbon, the two CH2 groups, C2, and the ring carbonyl carbon — six atoms, and the new ring is fused to the old one along the C2–carbonyl bond. The other candidate is a ring CH2 next to a ring carbonyl, attacking the side-chain ketone; that loop is six atoms as well, but it begins and ends inside the existing ring, so it builds a bridged bicyclo[2.2.2] skeleton whose aldol product cannot dehydrate — the alkene would have to sit at a bridgehead, which a small bicyclic cannot hold. Only the fused closure reaches a conjugated enone, so that is the one that runs.
4. Dehydrate. The β-hydroxy ketone loses water, and the new C=C lands between the old ring carbonyl carbon and the carbon next to it, in conjugation with the side-chain ketone. That C=C sits at the ring fusion.
5. Read the product. A bicyclic compound carrying an enone in the new ring, an untouched ketone in the old one, and an angular methyl at the quaternary carbon. Run the three retro steps above on it and the dione and MVK fall back out, which is the check that you built it right.

What carries forward

A Michael addition is conjugate addition with an enolate donor, and it leaves a 1,5-dicarbonyl. A Robinson annulation is a Michael followed by an intramolecular aldol condensation, and it leaves a cyclohexenone. Both are recognized by product spacing rather than by memorizing conditions, which is the way to hold every condensation in this chapter.
