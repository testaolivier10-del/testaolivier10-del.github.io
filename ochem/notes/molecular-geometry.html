The idea

VSEPR (valence shell electron pair repulsion) says electron groups — bonds and lone pairs — push each other as far apart as possible, since electrons repel each other. This is the same electron-group count from Hybridization, answering a different question: hybridization tells you which orbitals the central atom uses (sp³/sp²/sp), and VSEPR tells you the actual 3D shape those orbitals settle into.

The catch: the name of the shape only counts atoms, not lone pairs — lone pairs are invisible in the name even though they're still there, pushing.

4 groups (sp³) → tetrahedral base  ·  3 (sp²) → trigonal planar  ·  2 (sp) → linear

Worked examples

Methane (4 bonds, 0 lone pairs): all 4 groups are visible atoms → tetrahedral. Ammonia (3 bonds + 1 lone pair, 4 groups total): the lone pair is invisible in the name, so the 3 visible N–H bonds form a trigonal pyramidal shape. Water (2 bonds + 2 lone pairs): only the 2 O–H bonds are visible → bent. CO₂/BeF₂ (2 groups, 0 lone pairs): spread 180° apart → linear.

A lone pair, not shared between two nuclei, spreads out more than a bond does — pushing bonds closer together than the ideal angle. That's why NH₃'s H–N–H angle is 107° and H₂O's H–O–H angle is 104.5°, both less than a perfect tetrahedron's 109.5°.

Beyond 4 groups

5 groups, 0 lone pairs → trigonal bipyramidal (3 equatorial + 2 axial, e.g. PCl₅). 6 groups, 0 lone pairs → octahedral (4 equatorial + 2 axial, e.g. SF₆). Rare in day-to-day organic molecules, but good to recognize for S/P compounds. (The Hybridization lesson's note applies here too: these shapes are real and well-confirmed experimentally, but the old "sp³d/sp³d²" explanation for why period 3+ atoms can do this has been superseded by atomic-size and ionic-character arguments, not genuine d-orbital mixing.)
