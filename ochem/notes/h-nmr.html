Spectroscopy · Section 85 of 117

¹H NMR

Practice this — interactive lesson

Proton NMR is the single most informative technique in organic chemistry. Where IR identifies functional groups, ¹H NMR maps the carbon skeleton — how many distinct hydrogen environments there are, how many hydrogens are in each, and which environments sit next to which. Read together, those three pieces of information usually determine a structure outright.

What NMR measures

A hydrogen nucleus is a tiny magnet. Placed in a strong external field it can align with or against that field, and the energy gap between those two states depends on the field the nucleus actually experiences — which is the external field minus whatever its surrounding electrons shield it from. Radio waves at the matching frequency flip the nucleus, and that absorption is the signal.

The whole technique hangs on that shielding, because electron density around a nucleus varies with chemical environment. More electron density means more shielding, a smaller effective field, and a signal further upfield. Less means deshielding, and a signal further downfield.

Chemical shift: where a signal appears

Position is reported as chemical shift in parts per million, referenced against tetramethylsilane at 0. Using ppm rather than frequency makes the numbers independent of the instrument's field strength, so a shift measured on a 300 MHz machine matches one from a 600 MHz machine. Samples are dissolved in a deuterated solvent — usually CDCl₃ — for a related reason: deuterium is invisible to a ¹H experiment, so the solvent does not bury the sample under its own signal.

Electronegative neighbors deshield. So does an adjacent carbonyl, an adjacent pi system, and — most dramatically — an aromatic ring.

Three entries deserve a note, and they are all the same effect. A circulating pi system, sitting in the applied field, generates a small field of its own — and because that field has a direction, it adds to the external field in some places and subtracts from it in others. This is magnetic anisotropy, and it is why geometry, and not electronegativity alone, sets a chemical shift. An aromatic ring’s electrons circulate around the ring (the ring current) and its hydrogens sit on the outside edge, where the induced field reinforces the applied one — so they land unusually far downfield at 6.5–8.5, and that displacement is itself a test for aromaticity. An alkene’s pi cloud does the same on a smaller scale, pushing vinyl hydrogens to 4.5–6.5. An alkyne is the case that proves the rule: its pi system is a cylinder around the bond axis and the ≡C–H sits on that axis, in the region where the induced field opposes the applied one — so it is shielded and appears at 1.8–3.0, upfield of a vinyl hydrogen even though its carbon is the more electronegative of the two. And a carboxylic acid O–H is the most deshielded proton on the list, which is the same acidity you worked out in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives showing up as a chemical shift.

Integration: how many hydrogens

The area under a signal is proportional to the number of equivalent hydrogens producing it. Only relative areas are meaningful, so integration gives you ratios — 3:2:1 — which you then scale against the molecular formula to get absolute counts.

Chemically equivalent hydrogens give one signal. The three hydrogens of a freely rotating methyl group are exchanged by that rotation faster than NMR can resolve, so they always appear together. Symmetry does the same job: the four aromatic hydrogens of p-xylene appear as a single peak. This means a symmetric molecule gives a much simpler spectrum than its hydrogen count suggests — which is information, not a loss. The reverse case catches people out, so it is worth one sentence: two hydrogens on the same carbon are not automatically equivalent. If the molecule has a stereocenter elsewhere, no symmetry operation exchanges them — replacing one gives a different diastereomer than replacing the other — so they are diastereotopic, have different shifts, and split each other. That is why the C3 hydrogens of 2-bromobutane give a messier pattern than a quick n + 1 count predicts.

Splitting: which environments are adjacent

A hydrogen's signal is split by non-equivalent hydrogens on adjacent carbons, because each neighbor's spin adds or subtracts a little from the local field. The result is the n + 1 rule: a hydrogen with n equivalent neighbors appears as n + 1 peaks, with relative intensities following Pascal's triangle.

Two pieces of vocabulary come with it. Coupling is transmitted through the bonding electrons rather than through space, and it dies away fast. Vicinal coupling, between hydrogens on adjacent carbons and therefore three bonds apart (H–C–C–H), is the one the n + 1 rule is about, and it runs about 6–8 Hz in a freely rotating chain. Geminal coupling, between two hydrogens on the same carbon and only two bonds apart, exists but stays invisible whenever those two hydrogens are equivalent. Four bonds and beyond is usually too small to resolve, which is the practical reason you only ever look at the carbon next door.

No neighbors gives a singlet; one gives a doublet at 1:1; two give a triplet at 1:2:1; three give a quartet at 1:3:3:1. The spacing between the peaks of a multiplet is the coupling constant J, measured in Hz, and it is identical for both partners in a coupled pair — which is how you confirm that two multiplets are talking to each other rather than coincidentally similar.

The rule has a precondition that is easy to miss: the n neighbors have to be equivalent to each other. When a hydrogen has two different sets of neighbors the couplings happen one at a time. In CH₃–CH₂–CHBr–CH₃ the CHBr hydrogen has two neighbors on one side and three on the other, and it is not a case of "five neighbors, so a sextet". It is split into a quartet by the methyl, and each of those four lines is split again into a triplet by the CH₂ — a quartet of triplets, twelve lines. Here the two J values happen to be nearly equal, so the lines fall on top of each other and the multiplet collapses to what looks like a sextet; when the two J values differ, all the lines stay separate and you see them. The safe habit is to build the pattern one coupling at a time, as a tree, and only then ask what it looks like.

J values carry their own structural information. In an alkene, trans coupling runs 12–18 Hz and cis coupling 6–12, which distinguishes E from Z directly. On an aromatic ring, ortho coupling is 7–10 Hz and meta coupling 2–3, which locates substituents.

Worked example — ethanol, signal by signal

1.2 ppm, 3H, triplet. Three hydrogens means a methyl; a triplet means two neighbors; 1.2 ppm means nothing electronegative nearby. The CH₃.

3.6 ppm, 2H, quartet. Two hydrogens, three neighbors, and shifted well downfield by the adjacent oxygen. The CH₂.

2–5 ppm, 1H, broad singlet. The OH — a singlet despite having neighbors, because it exchanges with other OH protons faster than coupling can register, and broad and variable in position for the same reason.

O–H and N–H protons usually do not follow the n + 1 rule. They exchange rapidly with each other and with traces of water, which averages away the coupling and leaves a broad singlet at an unpredictable shift. If a spectrum has one broad, un-split, oddly placed peak, suspect an exchangeable proton — and confirm it by adding D₂O, which swaps it for deuterium and makes the peak vanish entirely.

Working a spectrum

A reliable order of operations. Count the signals — that is the number of distinct hydrogen environments, and a small number relative to the formula means symmetry. Read the integrations as a ratio and scale to the formula. Use the shifts to say what each environment is attached to. Use the splitting to work out what is adjacent to what. Then assemble the fragments into a structure consistent with the molecular formula and the degrees of unsaturation.

Certain patterns become recognizable with practice: a 3H triplet with a 2H quartet is an ethyl group; a 6H doublet with a 1H septet is an isopropyl; a 9H singlet is tert-butyl; and two 2H doublets in the aromatic region is a para-disubstituted ring.

Worked example — C₄H₈O₂ from three signals

Data: 1.3 ppm (3H, t), 2.0 ppm (3H, s), 4.1 ppm (2H, q). IR shows a strong peak at 1735.

DoU = (8 + 2 − 8)/2 = 1, and the 1735 IR band says that one degree is an ester carbonyl.

The triplet/quartet pair is an ethyl group, and the quartet at 4.1 puts that CH₂ on an oxygen — so it is OCH₂CH₃. The 3H singlet at 2.0 is a methyl with no neighbors, at a shift typical of alpha to a carbonyl.

Assemble: CH₃–CO–O–CH₂CH₃, ethyl acetate. Every piece of data accounted for.

What carries forward

¹H NMR is the centerpiece of every structure-determination problem, and it works best alongside the others: IR for functional groups, mass spectrometry for the formula, ¹³C for the carbon count.
