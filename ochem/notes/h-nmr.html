Chemical shift: where a signal appears, and what deshields it

A hydrogen nucleus in a strong external magnetic field resonates at a frequency that depends on the local electron density surrounding it — electrons shield the nucleus from the external field. An electronegative neighbor (like an adjacent O, N, or halogen, or an adjacent carbonyl carbon) pulls electron density away, deshielding the hydrogen and pushing its signal further downfield (to a higher chemical shift, reported in parts per million, ppm, referenced against a standard). Typical ranges worth recognizing, from upfield to downfield:

  • alkyl C–H, far from anything electronegative — 0.9-1.5 ppm
  • C–H next to a carbonyl (alpha hydrogens, Alpha Hydrogens lesson) — 2.0-2.5 ppm
  • C–H next to O or a halogen — 3.3-4.5 ppm
  • vinyl (alkene) C–H — 4.5-6.5 ppm
  • aromatic ring C–H — 6.5-8.5 ppm
  • aldehyde C–H — 9-10 ppm
  • carboxylic acid O–H — 10-13 ppm

Two of those deserve a note. Aromatic C–H sits unusually far downfield because the ring's pi electrons circulate in the magnetic field and generate a small field of their own, reinforcing the deshielding — an effect called ring current, specific to aromatic rings. And a carboxylic acid O–H is the most deshielded proton on the list, which is the same acidity you worked out in Module 10, showing up here as a chemical shift.

Integration: how many hydrogens produce each signal

The area under each signal (its integration) is directly proportional to the number of chemically equivalent hydrogens producing it. Comparing the relative integration values across all the signals in a spectrum (as ratios, since only relative — not absolute — hydrogen counts can be read off directly) tells you the ratio of each type of hydrogen environment present in the molecule.

Chemically equivalent hydrogens (like the three hydrogens of a freely-rotating CH₃ group) always show up as a single signal — free rotation exchanges their average local environment fast enough that NMR simply cannot tell them apart. Diastereotopic hydrogens (like each half of a CH₂ group next to a stereocenter, connecting back to the Stereocenters lesson) can appear as genuinely different signals, though this refinement is worth knowing exists even if it's not the everyday case.

Spin-spin splitting: the n+1 rule reveals neighboring hydrogens

A hydrogen's signal is split into multiple peaks by the influence of non-equivalent hydrogens on adjacent carbons (through three bonds, typically) — each unique combination of nearby spin states shifts the observed frequency slightly. The result follows the n+1 rule: a hydrogen with n equivalent neighboring hydrogens is split into n+1 peaks, with relative intensities following the binomial (Pascal's triangle) pattern — a singlet (no neighbors), a doublet (1 neighbor), a triplet (2 neighbors, e.g., a CH₂ flanked by a CH₃), a quartet (3 neighbors, e.g., a CH₃ flanked by a CH₂), and so on. The spacing between split peaks (the coupling constant, J, in Hz) reflects the strength of that neighboring interaction and is identical for both coupling partners — a useful cross-check for correctly pairing up which signals are talking to each other.

Putting it together: a worked example

Ethanol's spectrum, signal by signal. The CH₃ integrates for 3H and appears as a triplet near 1.2 ppm — a triplet because the adjacent CH₂ has 2 hydrogens, and n+1 = 3. The CH₂ integrates for 2H and appears as a quartet near 3.6 ppm — a quartet because the adjacent CH₃ has 3 hydrogens, and n+1 = 4, and shifted downfield by the nearby oxygen. The OH integrates for 1H and appears as a broad singlet somewhere around 2-5 ppm depending on conditions, not cleanly split because the proton exchanges too fast.

All three pieces of evidence — shift, integration, and splitting — point at the same structure. That mutual agreement is what makes an assignment convincing.
