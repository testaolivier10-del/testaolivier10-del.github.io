What "conjugate" means

A conjugate acid-base pair is two species that differ by exactly one proton (H⁺). Lose a proton from an acid, and what's left is its conjugate base. Gain a proton on a base, and what you get is its conjugate acid. HCl and Cl⁻ are a conjugate pair; NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are a conjugate pair; water can be either the acid (losing a proton to become OH⁻) or the base (gaining one to become H₃O⁺) — water is amphoteric, meaning it can act as either.

The inverse relationship

The stronger an acid is, the weaker its conjugate base is — and vice versa. This isn't a coincidence: an acid is "strong" precisely because it readily gives up its proton, which means what's left behind (the conjugate base) must be comfortable holding the negative charge (or lone pair) without wanting a proton back. HCl (pKa −7, very strong acid) has Cl⁻ as its conjugate base — an extremely weak, stable base. Water (pKa 15.7, weak acid) has OH⁻ as its conjugate base — a comparatively strong, reactive base.

This is the exact same idea as leaving-group ability from Module 2: a good leaving group is a weak base, which is the conjugate base of a strong acid. "Conjugate base of a strong acid" and "good leaving group" are two names for the same thing.

Reading a pKa table both directions

A pKa table tells you two things at once: reading down the acid pKa, acids get weaker; reading down the corresponding conjugate bases, bases get stronger. This is why the strongest bases used in organic chemistry (LDA, NaNH₂, NaH) are all conjugate bases of extremely weak acids (amines, pKa ≈ 38; H₂, pKa ≈ 36) — a weak-enough acid guarantees an aggressively strong conjugate base.
