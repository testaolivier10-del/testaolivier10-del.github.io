Acids & Bases · Section 19 of 121

Conjugate acids/bases

Practice this — interactive lesson

Acids and bases come in pairs, and the pairing is not a bookkeeping convenience — it is the reason a single table of acid strengths can be read in both directions. Every time you rank an acid, you have also ranked a base, and the two carry exactly the same information. The number that does the ranking, pKa, is the next section; this one establishes the pairing that makes that number do double duty. A few pKa values appear below as illustrations — read them for now as "smaller means stronger acid", which is all you need here.

What "conjugate" means

A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by exactly one proton. Remove a proton from an acid and what remains is its conjugate base. Add a proton to a base and what you get is its conjugate acid.

HCl and Cl⁻ are a conjugate pair. NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are a conjugate pair. So are CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻, and H₂O and HO⁻, and H₃O⁺ and H₂O. "Exactly one proton" is the whole definition: same atoms otherwise, differing by one H and one unit of charge.

Water appears in that list twice, as both a conjugate acid and a conjugate base, because it can lose a proton to become HO⁻ or gain one to become H₃O⁺. A species that can act as either is amphoteric, and several of the most important molecules in this course qualify: water, alcohols, amines, and — importantly for Biomolecules — amino acids.

Worked example — labelling all four species

CH₃COOH + NH₃ ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + NH₄⁺

Acetic acid loses a proton, so it is the acid, and acetate is its conjugate base. Ammonia gains a proton, so it is the base, and ammonium is its conjugate acid.

Every proton transfer has all four roles filled. The acid and its conjugate base are on opposite sides of the arrow, as are the base and its conjugate acid — that opposite-sides relationship is worth noticing, because it is what trips people up when labelling.

Which way does it go? By the rule from the previous section, toward the weaker acid. Acetic acid gives up its proton far more readily than ammonium does, so the equilibrium lies to the right, and lies there almost completely. (The next section turns "far more readily" into a number: pKa 4.76 against 9.2, a gap of about 10⁴.)

The inverse relationship

The stronger an acid is, the weaker its conjugate base — and the reverse. This is not a coincidence or a separate fact to memorize; it follows from what "strong acid" means. An acid is strong precisely because it gives up its proton readily, which means whatever is left behind must be comfortable holding the resulting electron pair without wanting the proton back. Comfortable holding it is exactly what "weak base" means.

Run the comparison in both directions. HCl has a pKa of −7, making it a very strong acid; chloride is correspondingly an extremely weak, stable, unreactive base — you can have sodium chloride in your food. Water has a pKa of 15.7, making it a weak acid; hydroxide is correspondingly a strong and reactive base. Ammonia has a pKa of 38, making it a vanishingly weak acid; amide ion (⁻NH₂) is correspondingly one of the strongest bases you will use.

Hold on to this one, because How Reactions Happen will build a whole section on it. A group that leaves a molecule easily — a leaving group, in the vocabulary of that chapter — is one that is comfortable as an anion, which is to say a weak base, which is to say the conjugate base of a strong acid. "Conjugate base of a strong acid," "weak base," "stable anion" and "good leaving group" turn out to be four names for one property, and moving between them fluently is most of what makes Substitution & Elimination tractable.

One ranking, read in both directions

This is what the pairing buys you, and it is why the next section's table is worth learning once rather than twice: read down the acid column and acids get weaker, read down the conjugate base column and bases get stronger. That is why the strongest bases in common use — LDA, NaNH₂, NaH, butyllithium — are all left behind by extraordinarily weak acids: an amine at pKa 38, hydrogen gas at 36, an alkane at 50. If you want a ferocious base, find the feeblest acid you can and take its proton off.

Worked example — the conjugates of an alcohol and an amine

Every conjugate in this section so far has been inorganic. Exams ask about organic ones, and they ask in both directions, so run ethanol and ethylamine through the definition.

Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH. Its conjugate base is made by removing the O–H proton: ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻, negatively charged, pKa of the parent 16. Its conjugate acid is made by adding a proton to one of oxygen's lone pairs: the oxonium ion CH₃CH₂OH₂⁺, positively charged, pKa about −2. Same molecule, one proton either side, and the two answers differ by eighteen orders of magnitude in acidity.

Ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂. Conjugate acid: the ammonium ion CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺, pKa about 10.7. Conjugate base: the amide anion CH₃CH₂NH⁻, from an N–H at pKa about 38. Notice which of the two you will actually meet — the ammonium ion forms whenever an amine meets any ordinary acid, while the amide anion needs butyllithium.

The same operation on the groups you will meet later: the conjugate acid of a ketone is the protonated carbonyl C=OH⁺; the conjugate base of a ketone is the enolate; the conjugate base of a terminal alkyne is the acetylide. In each case, count one H and one unit of charge, and change nothing else.

A conjugate base is not automatically negative. "One H and one unit of charge" means the charge goes down by one, not that it ends at −1. H₃O⁺ is an acid and its conjugate base is neutral water. NH₄⁺ is an acid and its conjugate base is neutral ammonia. Going the other way, the conjugate acid of a neutral base is a cation: water → H₃O⁺, an amine → an ammonium ion. Students habitually decorate every conjugate base with a minus sign and every conjugate acid with nothing; the reliable move is to write the charge you started with and subtract or add one.
"Conjugate" describes a relationship, not a property. Chloride is not "a conjugate base" in the abstract — it is the conjugate base of HCl. Every base is the conjugate base of something. Asking "is this a conjugate base?" is like asking "is this a sibling?"; the answer requires naming the other party.
Conjugate pairs differ by one proton, not by any other change. HSO₄⁻ is the conjugate base of H₂SO₄, and SO₄²⁻ is the conjugate base of HSO₄⁻ — but SO₄²⁻ is not the conjugate base of H₂SO₄, because they differ by two. Multi-proton acids have multiple pKa values, one per step, and each step is its own conjugate pair.

What carries forward

The conjugate relationship is what lets you use one table for everything: acid strength, base strength, leaving-group ability and anion stability are a single ranking read from different directions. The next section puts numbers on that ranking — the pKa scale — and the one after it explains what structural features decide where a species lands on it.
