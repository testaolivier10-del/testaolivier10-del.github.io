The Claisen condensation: enolate meets ester

The Claisen condensation reacts an ester's enolate (formed exactly as in the Alpha Hydrogens lesson, just on an ester's alpha carbon instead of a ketone's) with a second ester molecule as the electrophile. Because the electrophile is an ester (not a ketone or aldehyde), the enolate's attack on its carbonyl carbon proceeds by nucleophilic acyl substitution (the addition-elimination mechanism from the Acyl Substitution lesson), not simple addition — the alkoxy group leaves, regenerating a carbonyl, exactly as in ordinary ester chemistry.

Step by step

(1) Base (typically the ester's own conjugate-base alkoxide, like NaOEt for an ethyl ester) removes an alpha hydrogen, forming the ester enolate. (2) That enolate's carbon attacks a second ester molecule's carbonyl carbon, forming a tetrahedral intermediate. (3) The intermediate collapses, expelling the alkoxide leaving group (RO⁻) and reforming a carbonyl — exactly the acyl-substitution pattern, just with a carbon nucleophile in place of the amine or water seen previously. The product is a beta-ketoester: one carbonyl retained as a ketone, the other retained as an ester, separated by the new C–C bond, two carbons apart from each other — structurally the ester-chemistry parallel to the aldol reaction's beta-hydroxy carbonyl.

The alkoxide byproduct (RO⁻) released in step 3 is the exact same base used to form the enolate in step 1 (e.g., NaOEt released from an ethyl ester regenerates NaOEt) — the reaction is, in that sense, catalytic in base, at least until the final equilibrium-driving step below.

Why the Claisen condensation is driven to completion: a second, more acidic deprotonation

Unlike a simple ketone alpha-hydrogen (pKa ≈ 20), the beta-ketoester product has an alpha hydrogen flanked by two carbonyls at once (the ketone and the ester), and its resulting enolate is doubly resonance-stabilized — delocalized into both carbonyls rather than just one. This pushes the pKa down to around 11, acidic enough that the mild alkoxide base used in the reaction (conjugate acid an alcohol, pKa ≈16) can deprotonate it essentially completely. That final, irreversible deprotonation removes the beta-ketoester product from the equilibrium entirely, pulling the whole multi-step, otherwise-reversible sequence forward to completion. A final aqueous acid workup then simply reprotonates this stable anion to reveal the neutral beta-ketoester product.

Crossed and intramolecular Claisen (Dieckmann) reactions

Just like the aldol reaction, a crossed Claisen between two different esters needs the same kind of selectivity strategy (one ester with no alpha hydrogens, or careful stoichiometric control) to avoid a statistical mixture. When both ester groups are part of the same molecule (a diester), an intramolecular Claisen — called a Dieckmann condensation — can form a ring instead, and is especially favorable when the resulting ring size is five or six members (the same favorable ring sizes seen in the Cyclohexanes lesson's strain-energy trend).
