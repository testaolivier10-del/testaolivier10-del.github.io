Ether chemistry

Practice this — interactive lesson

Ethers are the functional group defined by what it does not do. They have no acidic proton, no good leaving group, and nothing electrophilic enough to attack — which makes them almost inert, and therefore invaluable as solvents. Understanding why they are unreactive is a good test of whether the last two modules have stuck.

Naming them, and what they are like to handle

Common names treat an ether as two groups plus the word: CH₃–O–CH₂CH₃ is ethyl methyl ether. IUPAC treats the smaller half as a substituent on the larger one — an alkoxy group — so the same compound is methoxyethane, and cyclohexyl methyl ether is methoxycyclohexane. Both appear in exam papers, so read both.

Physically, an ether is what an alcohol would be without its O–H. There is still a bent C–O–C unit and still a dipole, but no hydrogen on oxygen, so ether molecules cannot hydrogen-bond to each other: diethyl ether boils at 35 °C while butan-1-ol, the same formula, boils at 118 °C. Yet the lone pairs are still there, so an ether can accept a hydrogen bond even though it cannot donate one — which is why diethyl ether dissolves in water to about 6 g per 100 mL, far more than pentane does, and why an ether is a good solvent for polar and nonpolar things at once.

Why ethers are so unreactive

An ether, R–O–R′, has no O–H bond, so there is no acidic proton for a base to remove. And the group attached to carbon is an alkoxide, RO⁻ — the conjugate base of an alcohol at pKa 16, which makes it a strong base and just as poor a leaving group as hydroxide. So an ether has nothing to give a base and nothing to give a nucleophile.

The result is a functional group inert to bases, nucleophiles, reducing agents and mild oxidants. That inertness is precisely why diethyl ether and THF are the default solvents for reactions involving ferociously reactive species — Grignard reagents and LiAlH₄ among them — that would attack almost any other functional group present.

(Both of those are reagents you meet properly in Carbonyl Chemistry. A Grignard, RMgX, is a carbon nucleophile made from an alkyl halide and magnesium; LiAlH₄ is a powerful source of hydride, H⁻. All that matters here is that they are violently reactive and need a solvent that will not react back.)

Ethers do more than sit there passively. The oxygen's lone pairs coordinate to the magnesium of a Grignard reagent, and that coordination is what keeps it in solution and reactive — THF, with a less hindered oxygen and greater basicity, coordinates better than diethyl ether, which is why it is preferred for difficult Grignard formations. The "inert" solvent is quietly doing chemistry.

Williamson ether synthesis

The standard route to an ether. Deprotonate an alcohol with a strong base — NaH is common, and its byproduct, hydrogen gas, simply bubbles away — to give an alkoxide, then let that alkoxide do an SN2 attack on a primary or methyl alkyl halide or tosylate.

Because the key step is a plain SN2, everything you know about SN2 applies. It works best with an unhindered electrophile; a secondary electrophile gives poor yields, and a tertiary one gives no ether at all — the alkoxide acts as a base instead, and E2 takes over.

Choose the right disconnection. To make tert-butyl methyl ether, you have a choice: methoxide plus tert-butyl bromide, or tert-butoxide plus methyl iodide. The first fails completely — tertiary substrate, strong base, E2. The second works cleanly, because the methyl halide is an ideal SN2 electrophile and the bulk sits harmlessly on the nucleophile. Always put the bulk on the alkoxide and the simplicity on the halide. This is a standard exam question and a standard real-world consideration.
Worked example — planning an unsymmetrical ether

Target: cyclohexyl ethyl ether.

Route A: cyclohexanol → cyclohexoxide (NaH), then ethyl bromide. The electrophile is primary. ✓

Route B: ethanol → ethoxide (NaH), then cyclohexyl bromide. The electrophile is secondary and cyclic, so E2 competes badly and you get mostly cyclohexene. ✗

Same target, same two fragments, and only one order works.

Alkoxymercuration and other routes

Where a Williamson would fail, adding an alcohol across an alkene does the job. Alkoxymercuration–demercuration — Hg(OAc)₂ in the alcohol you want to install, then NaBH₄ — is oxymercuration from Alkenes & Alkynes with the alcohol in place of water. Mercury bridges the two alkene carbons as a mercurinium ion, and the alcohol opens that bridge at the carbon better able to carry positive charge — the more substituted one, so the selectivity is Markovnikov — attacking from the opposite face, so the addition is anti. NaBH₄ then replaces the mercury with hydrogen. Because the bridge never becomes a free carbocation, nothing rearranges, and because no SN2 is involved the crowded carbon is not a problem: this is how you put an ether on a tertiary carbon.

Ether cleavage: only strong acid gets through

Ethers resist bases and mild reagents, but hot concentrated HI or HBr will cleave the C–O bond, and the reason is the same activation trick as with alcohols. The ether oxygen is protonated first, which converts a hopeless leaving group (RO⁻) into a good one (a neutral alcohol, whose conjugate acid ROH₂⁺ has pKa about −2).

Then a halide attacks — iodide and bromide are excellent nucleophiles and weak bases, exactly right for the job. Which mechanism depends on the carbon, as always: a primary or methyl carbon is attacked by SN2, with inversion at that carbon; a tertiary or benzylic one ionizes instead and the halide captures the cation, SN1; and a secondary carbon does some of each, depending on the acid, the temperature and the halide. In an unsymmetrical ether that gives one rule worth memorizing in two halves — the halide goes to the less hindered carbon when both are primary or secondary, and to the tertiary carbon when there is one, because that carbon stops being a steric problem the moment it becomes a cation.

With excess HI the initially formed alcohol is itself converted to a second alkyl halide, so an ether ends up as two alkyl halides. HCl does not work well — chloride is too poor a nucleophile for the SN2 case.

Worked example — where does the iodide go?

tert-Butyl methyl ether plus excess HI.

Protonate the oxygen. Now there are two carbons to attack: a methyl and a tertiary. The tertiary one ionizes readily to a stable cation, so the ether cleaves there — giving tert-butyl iodide and methanol. Excess HI then converts the methanol to methyl iodide by SN2.

The tertiary carbon won because it supports the cation, not because it was more accessible — it is in fact far less accessible. Ask which mechanism operates before asking which carbon is attacked.

Epoxides: the reactive exception

Everything above describes ordinary, acyclic ethers. A three-membered cyclic ether — an epoxide — behaves entirely differently, and the next section is devoted to it. The same ring strain that destabilizes cyclopropane applies here, and releasing that strain by ring-opening turns the least reactive functional group in the chapter into one of the most useful.

A safety note worth knowing

Ethers left standing in air slowly form explosive peroxides, and they form them specifically at the carbon next to oxygen. The reason is the same resonance argument used for allylic and benzylic positions: pulling a hydrogen off that carbon leaves a radical whose singly occupied orbital sits next to an oxygen lone pair, and that overlap delocalizes the unpaired electron. The α C–H is therefore the weakest one in the molecule, so traces of oxygen abstract it first, and O₂ adds to the radical to give a hydroperoxide. This is why ether bottles carry dates, why old ether should never be distilled to dryness — the peroxides are less volatile, so they concentrate in the pot — and why THF is usually sold with an inhibitor. It is the one genuinely dangerous property of an otherwise placid functional group.

Ethers as Lewis bases, and crown ethers

An ether has nothing to offer a nucleophile but two lone pairs to offer an electron-poor atom, which makes it a decent Lewis base. That is the whole of the THF–magnesium story above, and it is why BF₃ is sold as BF₃·OEt₂, "BF₃ etherate": the ether oxygen donates into boron's empty p orbital, giving a stable, pourable liquid that still releases BF₃ to a better donor when one appears.

Take that idea and put six ether oxygens in one ring and you get a crown ether. 18-crown-6 — eighteen ring atoms, six of them oxygen — has a cavity about 2.7 Å across, and a potassium ion at 2.66 Å drops into it and is held by all six lone pairs at once. Sodium, at 1.9 Å, rattles around and binds far more weakly, so the binding is genuinely size-selective. The consequence is the useful part: KF is insoluble in benzene, but wrap the K⁺ in 18-crown-6 and the whole ion pair dissolves, leaving a fluoride that is neither solvated nor tightly paired to its cation. A "naked" anion like that is a dramatically better nucleophile than the same anion in water, where every lone pair is hydrogen-bonded to a solvent molecule.

What carries forward

The Williamson synthesis is a standard move in synthesis problems and an excellent test of SN2 judgement. Ether protection — installing an ether to keep an alcohol out of the way and removing it later — is a routine strategy in multi-step routes; the ethers actually used for it are silyl ethers, which come off with fluoride rather than with hot HI, and they are covered with the rest of the protecting groups in Synthesis & Retrosynthesis. And the protonation trick that allows ether cleavage is the same one that allows every reaction of alcohols, which is worth noticing as a general principle: a bad leaving group can usually be fixed with acid.
