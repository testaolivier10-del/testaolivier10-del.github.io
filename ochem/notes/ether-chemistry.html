Why ethers are so unreactive

An ether (R–O–R') has no O–H bond (so no acidic proton to remove) and no good leaving group directly attached under neutral or basic conditions (RO⁻ is just as bad a leaving group as OH⁻ — both are strong bases, conjugate bases of weak acids). This makes ethers chemically inert to bases, nucleophiles, and mild oxidants — which is exactly why they're the default solvent choice (diethyl ether, THF) for reactions involving highly reactive species like Grignard reagents or LiAlH₄ that would destroy almost any other functional group present. (Both are reagents you meet properly in Module 9: a Grignard, RMgX, is a carbon nucleophile made from an alkyl halide and magnesium metal, and LiAlH₄ is a powerful source of hydride, H⁻. All you need here is that both are ferociously reactive and have to be run in something that will not react back.)

Williamson ether synthesis

The standard way to build an ether: deprotonate an alcohol with a strong base (NaH is common — its byproduct, H₂ gas, is easy to remove) to form an alkoxide (RO⁻), then let that alkoxide perform an SN2 attack on a primary (or methyl) alkyl halide or tosylate. Because this is a clean SN2 step, it works best when the electrophile is unhindered — a bulky, hindered, or secondary/tertiary electrophile invites E2 elimination instead, competing with (or beating) the substitution.

The Williamson synthesis is really just Module 6's SN2 mechanism with an alkoxide standing in as the nucleophile — you already know exactly how to predict when it will and won't work well.

Ether cleavage: only strong acid gets through

Even though ethers resist bases and mild reagents, concentrated strong acid (HI or HBr, hot and concentrated) can still cleave the C–O bond, because the ether oxygen can be protonated first (just like an alcohol), converting a bad leaving group (RO⁻) into a good one — a neutral alcohol, ROH, which is the conjugate base of the protonated alcohol ROH₂⁺ (pKa ≈ −2), a strong acid. After protonation, a halide ion (I⁻ or Br⁻, an excellent, highly nucleophilic and non-basic nucleophile) attacks: at a primary carbon this is SN2 (backside attack, inversion), while at a secondary or tertiary carbon it proceeds SN1 (through a carbocation). Either mechanism ultimately breaks the ether into an alcohol (or a second alkyl halide, if excess acid is present) and an alkyl halide.

Epoxides: the reactive exception

Ordinary ethers are nearly inert, but a three-membered cyclic ether — an epoxide — is a dramatic exception, reactive enough to merit its own lesson next: the same ring strain that destabilizes small cycloalkanes (Cyclohexanes lesson) applies here too, and that strain is released by ring-opening, making epoxides far more reactive than any acyclic ether.
