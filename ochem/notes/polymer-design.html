Designing and unmaking a polymer

Practice this — interactive lesson

The previous sections asked what a structure does. This one runs the question backwards: given a material you need, what monomers make it — and given a polymer you no longer want, what happens to it.

Reading a polymer backwards

The disconnection is mechanical once you know which kind you are looking at.

  • Only carbons in the backbone? It came from an alkene. Find the repeat unit, put the double bond back, and you have the monomer.
  • An ester in the backbone? Cut the acyl C–O bond — the one between the carbonyl carbon and the oxygen — putting OH on the acid side and H on the alcohol side. What comes back is a diacid and a diol. Cutting the other C–O bond, the one to the CH2, strips the oxygen out of the alcohol fragment and leaves you with no diol at all.
  • An amide in the backbone? Cut every amide C–N bond — the one between the carbonyl carbon and the nitrogen, not the one to the CH2. What comes back is a diacid and a diamine.

The rule that makes this reliable is the one from the first section: every monomer must have two reactive sites. If a disconnection hands you a fragment with only one, you have cut in the wrong place.

Check the atom count as well. Cut a polyester and you get a diacid and a diol; add their formulas and subtract the repeat unit, and the difference should be water — one molecule per linkage in the repeat unit. For a diacid-plus-diol polyester that is two. PET: C8H6O4 (166) plus C2H6O2 (62) minus the C10H8O4 repeat unit (192) leaves 36, which is 2 H2O. If the difference is not a whole number of waters, the disconnection is wrong.
Worked example — identify an unknown polymer from its drawn backbone

Given. A repeat unit –[NH–CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2–NH–CO–C6H4(para)–CO]–.

Step 1 — what is in the backbone? Walk it and name every group you pass. There are two N–CO units, so they are amides: this is a polyamide, and it came from a diacid and a diamine.

Step 2 — cut every amide C–N bond. Put OH on the carbonyl side and H on the nitrogen side. Out come H2N(CH2)6NH2 and HO2C–C6H4–CO2H.

Step 3 — check each fragment has two sites. The diamine has two NH2; the diacid has two CO2H. Both pass, so the cut was in the right place.

Step 4 — name it. Hexamethylenediamine plus terephthalic acid, an aromatic nylon with one aromatic partner rather than two. This is nylon 6,T, and it is not Kevlar: Kevlar needs both monomers aromatic and para.

Step 5 — predict the material. One rigid aromatic ring per repeat unit plus amide hydrogen bonds between chains gives something higher-melting and stiffer than nylon 6,6, and the flexible (CH2)6 run that Kevlar has no equivalent of keeps it less rigid than Kevlar. Do not push that to “so it melt-processes”: the 6,T homopolymer melts near 370 °C, which is above where amides start to decompose, so the commercial 6T materials are copolymers (6T with 6,6 or with 6I) made deliberately irregular to bring Tm back into a processable window. Kevlar, higher still, is never melted at all — it is spun from solution.

The trap in step 2. Cutting the other C–N bond, the one to the CH2, hands you a fragment with an NH2 at one end and nothing at the other. Whenever a fragment comes back with a single site, you cut the wrong bond.

Notice that the last two rows point in opposite directions from the second. A property that makes a material durable is the same property that makes it persist in the environment, and you cannot have one without the other — which is the honest version of the recycling problem.

What happens at the end

Three fates, and the chemistry decides which is available.

  • Mechanical recycling. Melt and remold. Only works for thermoplastics, and each cycle shortens the chains a little, so the material degrades with reuse.
  • Chemical recycling. Depolymerize back to monomers and rebuild. Doing it by hydrolysis needs a backbone that can be cleaved, so it works for PET and for nylon and not for polyethylene — a C–C backbone has no bond that hydrolysis can select. Polyethylene can be cracked thermally — heated hard enough to break C–C bonds at random — back to a mixture of small alkenes and hydrocarbon oil. That is pyrolysis, and the distinction matters: it destroys the chain indiscriminately and returns a feedstock that has to be refined all over again, not the monomer you started from.
  • Degradation. Polyesters such as PLA hydrolyze, and given the right conditions organisms can finish the job. Polyolefins essentially do not, because there is nothing in a saturated hydrocarbon chain for water or an enzyme to attack.

"Depolymerize" is not a new reaction. It is acyl substitution run backwards, with the by-product now supplied in excess instead of being stripped out. For PET there are three ways to do it, and they differ only in the nucleophile:

  • Hydrolysis — water, acid- or base-catalyzed. Gives terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, the original monomers.
  • Methanolysis — methanol, so a transesterification. Gives dimethyl terephthalate and ethylene glycol, which is the other pair PET is made from industrially.
  • Glycolysis — excess ethylene glycol, also a transesterification. Cuts the chains back to the monomeric diester BHET, which can be polymerized straight back up.
Notice what makes all three work: Le Châtelier, not new chemistry. Polymerization drives the ester equilibrium one way by removing the small molecule; depolymerization drives it the other way by flooding the system with it. The same reversibility that forced the plant to strip water out continuously is what lets the bottle be unmade twenty years later.
This is why PET can be hydrolyzed back to its monomers and polyethylene cannot, and the reason is a single functional group. An ester in the backbone is a handle; a C–C backbone is not. The properties that make polyethylene cheap, inert and durable are exactly the ones that make it permanent.

Biodegradable by design

PLA — polylactic acid — is the clearest example of designing for the end. Lactic acid carries both an OH and a COOH, so it is its own diol-and-diacid and polymerizes with itself. The resulting polyester hydrolyzes back to lactic acid, which is a normal metabolite.

In practice it is made in two stages, and the reason is the arithmetic from the previous section. Condensing lactic acid directly stalls at an oligomer, because water cannot be pulled out of a melt that viscous and the equilibrium stops well short of the conversion a material needs. So the oligomer is cracked to lactide, the cyclic diester of two lactic acids, and that is polymerized by ring-opening instead — a chain-growth process that loses nothing and does reach fiber-grade molecular weight. The backbone is identical either way: a polyester of lactic acid.

The qualifier matters, though: PLA composts efficiently in an industrial facility, which runs hot and wet with the right microbes, and negligibly in a garden heap or in seawater — in seawater it shows essentially no measurable breakdown over years. "Biodegradable" is a claim about conditions as much as about structure.

What carries forward

Disconnect a polymer by finding the backbone functional group and cutting it, checking that every fragment has two reactive sites. Choose monomers by the property you need, knowing that rigidity, durability and persistence are the same property seen three ways. And when a question asks whether something can be recycled chemically, the answer is whether its backbone has a bond that hydrolysis can find.
