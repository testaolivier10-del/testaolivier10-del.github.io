Designing and unmaking a polymer

Practice this — interactive lesson

Pick up a clear drinks bottle made of PET. Three questions can be asked about it. What monomers was it made from? If you wanted a different material, such as a stiffer fiber or a clear sheet, which monomers would you choose instead? And once the bottle is empty, what can happen to it? This section answers all three, and each answer comes from the same place: the bonds in the polymer’s backbone.

Reading a polymer backwards

Finding the monomers is retrosynthesis applied to a chain. You look for the bonds that polymerization made and undo them on paper. Each of those cuts is a disconnection: a bond you break in your head to see what two pieces could have formed it.

Start with PET. Walk along its chain and you pass two esters in every repeat unit. Each ester was made by acyl substitution: an alcohol oxygen attacked a carbonyl carbon and an OH left (Nucleophilic acyl substitution). So the bond that formed is the one from the carbonyl carbon to that oxygen, the acyl C–O bond, and that is the bond to cut.

After each cut, give the carbonyl carbon back its OH and give the oxygen back its H. The piece between two carbonyl carbons becomes HO2C–C6H4–CO2H, terephthalic acid, a diacid. The piece between two oxygens becomes HO–CH2CH2–OH, ethylene glycol, a diol.

An ester has a second C–O bond, the one from the oxygen to the CH2, and cutting there is the classic mistake. Both oxygens then stay with the acid piece, and the CH2CH2 piece comes away with no oxygen at either end. A piece with nothing to react with could never have been a monomer.

The same reasoning covers every polymer in this chapter, and it sorts them by what sits in the backbone.

  • Only carbons in the backbone? The polymer came from an alkene. Find the repeat unit, put the double bond back, and you have the monomer.
  • An ester in the backbone? Cut every acyl C–O bond, as for PET. Out comes a diacid and a diol.
  • An amide in the backbone? Cut every amide C–N bond, the one from the carbonyl carbon to the nitrogen, not the one from the nitrogen to the CH2. Out comes a diacid and a diamine.

One check makes this reliable. It is the rule from What a polymer is: every monomer in a chain has two reactive sites, one for each neighbor it joins. If a disconnection hands you a piece with only one, you have cut in the wrong place.

A second check is to count atoms. Add the formulas of the two monomers and subtract the repeat unit. The difference should be water, one molecule for each linkage in the repeat unit. A diacid-plus-diol repeat unit holds two esters, so it has lost two waters. For PET: C8H6O4 (166) plus C2H6O2 (62), minus the C10H8O4 repeat unit (192), leaves 36, which is 2 H2O. If the difference is not a whole number of waters, the disconnection is wrong.
Worked example — identify an unknown polymer from its backbone

Given. A repeat unit –[NH–(CH2)6–NH–C(=O)–C6H4–C(=O)]–, with the ring joined at para positions. The drawing below shows a stretch of this chain.

Step 1 — what is in the backbone? Walk along it and name every group you pass. Twice you pass an N bonded to a C=O, so the backbone holds amides. This is a polyamide, made from a diacid and a diamine.

Step 2 — cut every amide C–N bond. Put OH on the carbonyl side and H on the nitrogen side. Out come H2N(CH2)6NH2 and HO2C–C6H4–CO2H.

Step 3 — check that each piece has two sites. The diamine has two NH2 groups and the diacid has two CO2H groups. Both pass, so the cuts were in the right place.

Step 4 — name it. Hexamethylenediamine plus terephthalic acid gives nylon 6,T. It is an aromatic nylon with only one aromatic partner. That is why it is not Kevlar: in Kevlar, both monomers are aromatic and para.

Step 5 — predict the material. Each repeat unit has one rigid ring, and amide hydrogen bonds hold neighboring chains together. So nylon 6,T should melt higher and be stiffer than nylon 6,6. Its flexible (CH2)6 run, which Kevlar lacks, keeps it less rigid than Kevlar.

The trap in step 2. Cutting the other C–N bond, the one from the nitrogen to the CH2, hands you a piece with an NH2 at one end and nothing at the other. Whenever a piece comes back with a single site, you cut the wrong bond.

Step 5 needs one caution. Do not stretch the prediction to “so it can be melted and molded.” Pure nylon 6,T melts near 370 °C (its Tm, the melting point of its crystalline regions), and amides start to decompose below that. The polymer would break down before it flowed.

The commercial 6T nylons get around this by being copolymers, chains built from two kinds of repeat unit. Some of the terephthalic acid is swapped for adipic acid (giving 6T/66) or for isophthalic acid, the meta isomer (giving 6T/6I). The mixed units stop the chains from packing so regularly, which brings Tm down into a range where the polymer can be melted safely. A meta ring helps most, because it puts a bend in the chain.

Kevlar melts higher still, so it is never melted at all. It is dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid and spun into fiber from that solution.

Choosing monomers for a property

Now run the question the other way. Suppose you need a clear, rigid sheet, such as a safety visor. A polymer turns cloudy when it has crystalline regions, because light scatters at every boundary between a crystalline region and the tangled material around it (Structure and properties). So the sheet must be amorphous, with no crystalline regions at all.

Its stiffness then has to come from somewhere other than packing. The answer is a glass transition temperature, Tg, well above room temperature, so the tangled chains stay frozen in use. PMMA gets its high Tg from bulky side groups. Polycarbonate gets it instead from the rigid, awkwardly shaped bisphenol A units in its backbone. Both are clear as glass.

Every property on the list below works the same way: name the structural feature that produces it, then choose monomers that build that feature in.

Notice that the last row pulls against the second. An ester that lets a material break down is also a weak point while you are using it, and an amide that resists water also resists breakdown. The feature that makes a material durable is the feature that makes it persist in the environment. That trade-off is the honest core of the plastics problem.

What happens at the end

Once the PET bottle is empty, three fates are open to a polymer in general, and its chemistry decides which ones are available.

  • Mechanical recycling. Melt it and mold it again. This works only for thermoplastics; a thermoset network cannot be melted. Each cycle also shortens the chains a little, so the material gets worse with reuse.
  • Chemical recycling. Break the polymer back down to monomers and build it again. Doing this by hydrolysis needs a backbone bond that water can break, so it works for PET and for nylon but not for polyethylene.
  • Degradation. Polyesters such as PLA hydrolyze, and under the right conditions microorganisms finish the job. Polyolefins, the polymers of simple alkenes such as polyethylene, essentially do not.

The reason is one functional group. A saturated C–C chain gives water and enzymes nothing to attack, because every bond in it looks like every other. An ester is different from all its neighbors, and that difference is a handle hydrolysis can grab.

Polyethylene can still be broken down by heat. Heated hard enough, its C–C bonds break at random, and it gives a mixture of small alkenes and hydrocarbon oil. This is pyrolysis, or thermal cracking. It is not the same as chemical recycling, because it destroys the chain without any selectivity. What comes back is a feedstock that has to be refined all over again, not the ethylene you started from.

Breaking PET down to monomers, called depolymerization, needs no new reaction. It is acyl substitution run backwards, with the small molecule now supplied in excess instead of being removed. There are three ways to do it for PET, and they differ only in the nucleophile.

  • Hydrolysis uses water, with acid or base as catalyst. It gives terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, the original monomers.
  • Methanolysis uses methanol, so it is a transesterification: one ester’s alkoxy group swapped for another (Esters & amides). It gives dimethyl terephthalate and ethylene glycol, which is the other pair of monomers PET is made from industrially.
  • Glycolysis uses excess ethylene glycol, also a transesterification. It cuts the chains down to BHET, bis(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate: terephthalic acid with an ethylene glycol ester on each end. BHET can be polymerized straight back to PET.
All three work by Le Châtelier’s principle, not by new chemistry. Every ester bond in PET sits at an equilibrium with the acid and alcohol it came from. Polymerization drives that equilibrium one way by removing the small molecule as it forms. Depolymerization drives it back by flooding the system with it. The reversibility that forced the factory to strip out water continuously is what lets the bottle be taken apart years later.

Biodegradable by design

PLA, polylactic acid, is the clearest example of a polymer designed for its end. Lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)CO2H, carries both an OH and a CO2H. That gives it two reactive sites of different kinds, so it is its own diol and diacid and can polymerize with itself. The polyester it forms hydrolyzes back to lactic acid, which is a normal product of metabolism.

In practice PLA is made in two stages, and the reason is the conversion arithmetic from Condensation polymers. Heating lactic acid on its own in the melt usually stalls at short chains, called oligomers. The melt is too thick for the water to escape, so the equilibrium stops well short of the conversion a useful material needs.

So the short chains are heated further and cracked into lactide, the cyclic diester of two lactic acids: a six-membered ring in which each lactic acid is esterified to the other. Lactide then polymerizes by ring-opening. The growing chain end attacks a carbonyl in the ring and breaks that ester’s C–O bond, so the ring opens onto the chain. This is chain growth, it loses no small molecule, and it reaches the molecular weight a fiber needs. The backbone is the same either way: a polyester of lactic acid.

“Biodegradable” needs a qualifier, though. PLA composts efficiently in an industrial facility, which runs hot and wet with the right microorganisms. In a garden heap it breaks down very little, and in seawater studies have found almost no measurable breakdown after more than a year. Whether a polymer biodegrades depends on the conditions as much as on its structure.

What carries forward

To find a polymer’s monomers, find the functional group in its backbone and cut at the bond that polymerization made, then check that every piece has two reactive sites. To choose monomers, start from the property you need, and expect that rigidity, durability and persistence come as a package. To decide whether a polymer can be recycled back to monomers, ask whether its backbone holds a bond that hydrolysis can pick out.
