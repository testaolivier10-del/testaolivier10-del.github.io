Three kinds of ring strain

Angle strain is the energy cost of forcing bond angles away from their ideal (tetrahedral, ~109.5° for sp³ carbon). Torsional strain is the cost of eclipsing bonds around the ring, exactly like the eclipsed conformations from the Newman Projections lesson. Steric strain is the cost of non-bonded atoms bumping into each other in space (van der Waals repulsion) — you'll see this precisely as 1,3-diaxial interactions in the next lesson.

Small rings pay these costs differently

Cyclopropane (a flat triangle, forced to 60° angles) has enormous angle strain plus torsional strain from being completely flat and eclipsed — total ring strain ≈ 27.5 kcal/mol. Cyclobutane (~26.3 kcal/mol) and cyclopentane (~6.2 kcal/mol) pucker slightly out of plane to relieve some torsional strain at the cost of a little angle strain, but still can't escape either type entirely.

Cyclohexane's total ring strain is essentially zero — not because it lacks the same geometric challenges, but because it isn't flat. A puckered, three-dimensional shape called the chair conformation lets cyclohexane satisfy both angle and torsional strain requirements at once.

Why the chair works

In a chair, every C–C–C bond angle is close to 111° (nearly ideal tetrahedral geometry — almost no angle strain), and every pair of adjacent C–H bonds around the ring is staggered relative to its neighbor (sighting down any ring C–C bond gives a perfectly staggered Newman projection — zero torsional strain). No small or flat ring can simultaneously satisfy both conditions; cyclohexane's six-membered, non-planar geometry is exactly what's needed to hit both targets at once.
