Carbonyl Chemistry · Section 65 of 121

Hydrates & cyanohydrins

Practice this — interactive lesson

Put some acetaldehyde, CH3CHO, in water and wait. About half of it turns into a new compound, CH3CH(OH)2, with two OH groups on the carbon that used to be the carbonyl carbon. Do the same with acetone and almost none of it changes.

Both flasks run the addition mechanism from the previous section, with water as the nucleophile. This section runs that mechanism with the two smallest nucleophiles there are, water and cyanide. Water lets you put a number on how reactive a carbonyl is. Cyanide makes a new carbon–carbon bond. An alcohol adds in the same way, but it adds twice, and that takes the next section.

Hydrates: addition of water

Water adds across the C=O. The product carries two OH groups on one carbon, which chemists call a gem-diol (gem is short for geminal, from the Latin for twins: both OH groups sit on the same carbon). Its everyday name is a hydrate.

R2C=O + H2O  ⇌  R2C(OH)2

Pure water adds slowly. A trace of base or of acid speeds it up a great deal, and the two work in different orders.

In base, hydroxide is the nucleophile. It attacks the carbonyl carbon, and the C=O π electrons move onto the oxygen. That leaves an alkoxide, an oxygen with a negative charge on a carbon. The alkoxide then takes a proton from a water molecule, and the water becomes a new hydroxide. So the base is not used up: it is a catalyst, something that speeds a reaction and comes out unchanged.

In acid, the proton goes on first. A lone pair on the carbonyl oxygen takes H+ from H3O+. The protonated carbonyl is far more electrophilic, so neutral water can now attack its carbon. The added oxygen then carries two hydrogens and a positive charge, and a second water molecule takes one of those protons away. The acid comes back out, so it too is a catalyst. This is the acid-catalyzed order from the previous section, with water as the nucleophile.

Every one of those steps runs backward as easily as forward. The hydrate can lose water and give the carbonyl back, so the reaction settles at an equilibrium. A catalyst changes how fast the equilibrium is reached. It does not change where it lies.

Where it lies depends on the carbonyl. For most carbonyls it lies far to the left:

CarbonylPercent hydrate at equilibrium
Acetone0.1%
Acetaldehyde~50%
Formaldehyde~99.9%
Chloral, CCl3CHO~100%, and the hydrate is isolable

From acetone to formaldehyde is three orders of magnitude in percent hydrate, and the only change is two methyl groups removed. In equilibrium constants the gap is closer to six orders. Percentages squash both ends of the scale: 99.9% and 99.99% look almost the same, yet the second equilibrium constant is ten times the first.

The spread has the same two causes that make aldehydes more reactive than ketones, which the Aldehydes & ketones section at the start of this chapter set out.

  • Sterics. Addition turns a flat sp² carbon, with its groups 120° apart, into a crowded sp³ one, with its groups about 109.5° apart. The groups are pushed closer together. Two methyls resist that; two hydrogens do not.
  • Electronics. Alkyl groups donate electron density into the carbonyl carbon, which stabilizes the starting material. Electron-withdrawing groups do the opposite. Chloral carries three chlorines that pull electron density away, so it is fully hydrated. Its hydrate is isolable: a crystalline solid you can weigh out.
This is the reactivity order of the whole chapter, made measurable. Aldehyde > ketone toward every nucleophile, for these two reasons together, and the hydration equilibrium is the easiest place to see the size of the effect.

A third cause appears when the carbonyl is attached to a ring. Compare benzaldehyde with acetaldehyde. Both are aldehydes with one group on the carbonyl carbon, yet benzaldehyde forms far less hydrate. Its C=O is conjugated with the benzene ring: the π electrons of the ring and of the C=O overlap into one shared system, and the sharing stabilizes the molecule. Addition turns the carbonyl carbon sp³ and cuts that link.

All three causes follow one rule. Anything that makes the starting carbonyl more stable holds the equilibrium back.

The two usual causes can also disagree, and then the larger one wins. Hexafluoroacetone, (CF3)2C=O, is more crowded than acetone, because each CF3 group is bulkier than a CH3. It is nonetheless essentially completely hydrated. Its six fluorines pull electron density away from the carbonyl carbon, and that pull outweighs the crowding.

In one case the steric argument runs backward. In cyclopropanone, the carbonyl carbon is a corner of a three-membered ring, so the ring angle at that carbon is about 60°. An sp² carbon wants 120°, so it is 60° off. After addition the carbon is sp³ and wants 109.5°. The same 60° angle is now only about 50° off.

So addition relieves angle strain here instead of creating it, and cyclopropanone is essentially fully hydrated even though it is a ketone. Ring strain, from Alkanes & Conformations, is doing the work the alkyl count usually does.

Why the hydrate matters even when it is the minor species

A compound present in small amounts can still decide the product, if it is the only form that can react further. The hydrate is the standard example.

Oxidizing a carbon means trading a C–H bond for a bond to oxygen. An oxidant needs a carbon that holds both an H and an OH, as Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry showed when it counted the C–H bonds on an alcohol's carbon. A primary alcohol has that arrangement. The aldehyde it is oxidized to does not: its carbonyl carbon has an H but no OH.

Two chromium(VI) reagents show what water does to that. Jones reagent is Cr(VI) in aqueous sulfuric acid. PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) is a Cr(VI) salt used in dry dichloromethane, CH2Cl2, with no water present. Both oxidize a primary alcohol to the aldehyde. Jones then carries on to the carboxylic acid. PCC stops at the aldehyde.

The difference is the hydrate. In the water of the Jones reagent, the aldehyde forms some hydrate, and the hydrate is an alcohol again: an H and an OH on one carbon. The oxidant attacks it a second time. In dry dichloromethane no hydrate can form, so the aldehyde gives the oxidant nothing to grip. Oxidation & Reduction treats both reagents in full, and the aldehyde oxidation section at the end of this chapter builds on this one.

The hydrate does not have to be the main species for this to work. As the oxidant uses up hydrate, more aldehyde adds water to replace it, until all of it has been oxidized. An equilibrium does not have to favor an intermediate for that intermediate to decide the product.

The effect gets more striking the smaller the fraction is. Glucose sits almost entirely as a ring with no aldehyde group; well under a tenth of a percent is the open-chain aldehyde at any moment. An oxidant that attacks aldehydes still reacts with all of it, because the ring keeps opening to replace the aldehyde used up. The aldehyde oxidation section returns to glucose.

Cyanohydrins: addition of cyanide

Mix acetone with HCN and a little sodium cyanide, and the product is acetone cyanohydrin, (CH3)2C(OH)CN. A cyanohydrin carries an OH and a C≡N (a nitrile group) on the same carbon. Unlike hydration, this addition makes a new carbon–carbon bond: the cyanide carbon ends up bonded to the old carbonyl carbon.

R2C=O + HCN → R2C(OH)CN

The reagent is written HCN with a catalytic amount of cyanide: HCN plus a little KCN or NaCN. Neither component works alone.

HCN by itself is a feeble nucleophile. The carbon that would do the attacking holds a hydrogen, not a lone pair, so it has no free electrons to attack with.

Cyanide by itself does attack, but the product is an alkoxide. With no proton source to put an H on that oxygen, the alkoxide simply pushes the cyanide back out, and the addition runs backward.

Together they work. CN− does the attacking. HCN then hands its proton to the alkoxide, and in doing so becomes a new CN−. The cyanide is made again on every turn, which is why a small amount is enough: it is genuinely catalytic.

The pH decides how much of each form is in the flask. The pKa of HCN is 9.2, and at a pH equal to the pKa an acid is half protonated and half not (Acids & Bases). So the reaction is run in a buffer near pH 8–10, which keeps both HCN and CN− present at once.

Strong base takes a cyanohydrin apart. Base removes the proton from the OH, and the alkoxide re-forms the C=O and pushes out cyanide, which is the last panel of the figure above. So cyanohydrins are made in a mildly basic buffer and destroyed by strong base. That is the reversibility of nucleophilic addition, showing up as a rule for handling the product.

What makes the reaction worth doing is what the nitrile can become. On acetone cyanohydrin:

  • Hydrolysis (aqueous acid, heat) turns C≡N into COOH, giving an α-hydroxy carboxylic acid.
  • Reduction with LiAlH4 turns C≡N into CH2NH2, giving a β-amino alcohol.
  • Dehydration removes the OH and an H from the neighboring carbon, putting a C=C there and giving an α,β-unsaturated nitrile.

Greek letters name a carbon by its distance from a reference group: α is the carbon next to it, β the one after that. The α and β labels on these products are often marked wrong, because the products count from different reference groups.

In the acid, the reference is the carboxyl group. The COOH carbon is C1, and the letters start after it. Its neighbor, the carbon holding the OH, is α. So the product is an α-hydroxy acid.

In the amino alcohol, the reference is the alcohol, and the letters start on the carbon holding the OH. That carbon is itself α, so the CH2NH2 beside it is β. So the product is a β-amino alcohol. Nothing about the skeleton moved between the two products; only the carbon the counting starts from did.

In the unsaturated nitrile, the nitrile carbon is C1. The old carbonyl carbon is α, the CH2 is β, and the new C=C joins those two.

Working backward from a target to its starting materials is called retrosynthesis, as in the Grignard worked example of the previous section. In those terms, a cyanohydrin is a one-carbon extension that arrives with a functional group already on it. When a target has a carbon holding an OH right next to a COOH, a CH2NH2 or a C≡N, cut the bond between those two carbons. One piece is an aldehyde or ketone; the other is cyanide.

Worked example — making 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanoic acid

Draw the target first: a carbon holding an OH, two methyls and a COOH, (CH3)2C(OH)COOH.

Step 1 — find the pattern. The carbon holding the OH is bonded straight to the COOH carbon. That is the cyanohydrin pattern.

Step 2 — cut the bond between them. The OH carbon, with its two methyls, becomes a C=O: acetone. The COOH carbon came from cyanide.

Step 3 — check it forward. Acetone with HCN and a little cyanide gives acetone cyanohydrin. Aqueous acid and heat turn its C≡N into COOH. That is the target.

Other ways of adding carbon lead to other groups, and the choice is about what you want at the end, not about the carbon count. All three use the carbon nucleophiles of Organometallics, where they are taught:

  • Formaldehyde plus a Grignard reagent adds one carbon and gives a primary alcohol. The previous section previewed this one.
  • CO2 plus a Grignard reagent adds one carbon and gives a carboxylic acid.
  • A nitrile plus a Grignard reagent gives a ketone. This adds the nitrile carbon and whatever that carbon carries, so it is a one-carbon extension only from HCN's point of view. Nitriles themselves belong to Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives.

Bisulfite adducts, and a use for a bad reaction

Sodium bisulfite, NaHSO3, adds to aldehydes and to unhindered ketones. The product is called an adduct, meaning two molecules joined with nothing lost. Here the sulfur forms the new bond to the carbonyl carbon, and the adduct is a sodium salt.

As a salt, the adduct dissolves in water and not in organic solvents. That switch in solubility is the useful part. Shake a mixture in an organic solvent with aqueous bisulfite, and the carbonyl compound moves into the water layer while everything else stays behind.

The addition is reversible with acid or with base, so the carbonyl compound can be released again afterward. That makes the whole sequence a purification. Where the adduct crystallizes out instead, it can simply be filtered off.

It also tells carbonyls apart. Aldehydes and methyl ketones form the adduct readily; hindered ketones do not. So bisulfite can separate them, and this is the same steric effect that sets the hydration equilibrium.

What carries forward

Water, cyanide and bisulfite all do the same thing: they add reversibly to a carbonyl. How far each goes is decided by sterics and electronics, in the same direction every time. That is why the hydration percentages are worth knowing as a ruler rather than as facts. And one of the three makes a C–C bond, which is why cyanohydrins appear in syntheses and hydrates do not.
