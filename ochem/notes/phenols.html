Aromatic Follow-Through · Section 100 of 121

Phenols

Practice this — interactive lesson

A phenol is an OH attached directly to a benzene ring. That one structural difference from an ordinary alcohol changes its acidity by about six orders of magnitude, and everything else in this section follows from the same cause.

The acidity, and where it comes from

CompoundpKa
Ethanol16
Phenol10
Carbonic acid6.4
Acetic acid4.8

Deprotonate an alcohol and the negative charge sits on one oxygen. Deprotonate a phenol and the phenoxide delocalizes that charge into the ring — onto the two ortho carbons and the para carbon. Spreading charge stabilizes it, the conjugate base is more stable, and the acid is stronger.

A phenol is acidic enough to be deprotonated by NaOH but not by NaHCO3. A carboxylic acid is deprotonated by both. That single difference is a standard separation: shake a mixture with bicarbonate and the carboxylic acid goes into the aqueous layer while the phenol stays behind.
Two things that look like this section and are not. A benzylic alcohol, PhCH2OH, has its OH one carbon out from the ring: pKa about 15, ordinary alcohol chemistry, oxidizes to an aldehyde, ionizes happily to a benzylic cation. None of that is true of a phenol. And “more acidic than an alcohol” does not mean “as acidic as an acid”: a phenol at pKa 10 is still five powers of ten weaker than acetic acid, which is the entire basis of the bicarbonate separation.

Substituents move it, and predictably

Anything that stabilizes the phenoxide makes the phenol more acidic, which means electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity and donating groups decrease it. The effect is much larger from the ortho and para positions, because only from there can a group reach the charge by resonance.

PhenolpKaWhy
4-Methylphenol10.3A weak donor, slightly less acidic
Phenol10.0The reference
3-Nitrophenol8.4From meta, nitro can only withdraw inductively
4-Nitrophenol7.2Nitro withdraws by resonance from para
2,4-Dinitrophenol4.1Two of them, and now as acidic as an acid
2,4,6-Trinitrophenol0.4Picric acid — stronger than most carboxylic acids

Three nitro groups turn an OH on a ring into something more acidic than most carboxylic acids. Nothing about the O–H bond changed; the stability of what is left behind did.

One warning about ortho. A nitro group there withdraws by resonance like a para one, but it also hydrogen-bonds to the OH inside the same molecule, which is why 2-nitrophenol is volatile enough to steam-distil and much less water-soluble than 4-nitrophenol, which hydrogen-bonds to water instead. Position changes more than acidity.

Worked example — a three-way separation run on nothing but pKa

An ether solution holds benzoic acid (pKa 4.8), phenol (10) and cyclohexanol (16). Shake it first with aqueous NaHCO3.

Step 1. Bicarbonate is the conjugate base of carbonic acid, pKa 6.4. It can deprotonate anything more acidic than that and nothing less. Only the benzoic acid qualifies, so benzoate goes into the aqueous layer; acidify that layer and the benzoic acid comes back out.

Step 2. Shake the remaining ether with aqueous NaOH. Hydroxide is the conjugate base of water, pKa 15.7, so it reaches the phenol at 10 comfortably. Sodium phenoxide is water-soluble and leaves; acidify and the phenol returns.

Step 3. Cyclohexanol at pKa 16 is untouched by both and stays in the ether. Three compounds, two shakes, and no chromatography — which is why the pKa table is a practical tool and not a list to memorize.

The ring is strongly activated

The same lone pair that delocalizes into the ring when the phenol is deprotonated also donates when it is not. So OH is a strong activator and an ortho/para director — one of the strongest there is.

The practical consequence is that phenols are often too reactive. Bromination needs no Lewis acid catalyst at all: phenol plus bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol immediately, as a precipitate, with all three activated positions substituted. Getting monobromination requires deliberately weaker conditions.

Reactions worth knowing

  • Williamson ether synthesis. Phenoxide plus a primary alkyl halide gives an aryl alkyl ether. The phenoxide is the nucleophile — never the aryl halide, since that carbon will not do SN2.
  • Kolbe–Schmitt carboxylation. Phenoxide with CO2 at about 125 °C under pressure, then an acid workup, gives salicylic acid — 2-hydroxybenzoic acid. The phenoxide attacks CO2 through a ring carbon rather than through its oxygen, which is the delocalization of this section made visible: the charge really does sit on the ortho and para carbons, so those carbons are nucleophilic. Ortho wins because the sodium ion bridges the phenoxide oxygen and the incoming CO2. Acetylate that phenol with acetic anhydride and you have aspirin.
  • Reimer–Tiemann formylation. Phenol with CHCl3 and aqueous NaOH gives salicylaldehyde, the ortho aldehyde. The electrophile is dichlorocarbene, :CCl2, made by hydroxide taking the proton off chloroform and chloride then leaving; the phenoxide’s ortho carbon attacks it, and the CHCl2 group that results is hydrolyzed to CHO under the reaction conditions. Same lesson as Kolbe–Schmitt: a phenoxide attacks electrophiles with its ring.
  • Oxidation to a quinone. A phenol oxidizes to a quinone, a conjugated cyclohexadienedione — Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4, or Fremy’s salt for the clean version, and the substrate that works best is a 1,4-dihydroxybenzene (hydroquinone), which is why the pair is usually written as an interconversion rather than a one-way oxidation. The ring is no longer aromatic, and the two carbonyls are cross-conjugated with the two remaining C=C bonds — a chromophore whose HOMO–LUMO gap is small enough to absorb visible light, which is why p-benzoquinone is yellow. Losing aromaticity makes that chromophore possible; the conjugation is what produces the color. The same easy two-electron accounting is why quinones serve as biological electron carriers: the quinone/hydroquinone couple moves two electrons and two protons reversibly, which is exactly what ubiquinone does in the respiratory chain.
  • Esterification. Phenols acylate normally with an acyl chloride or anhydride, giving aryl esters — aspirin is one.
A phenol cannot be made from an aryl halide by ordinary substitution, and it cannot be dehydrated or oxidized like an alcohol. The C–O bond is to an sp² ring carbon, so nothing that needs SN1 or SN2 at that carbon works. For a plain ring, phenols are made from diazonium salts — the last section of this chapter. For an activated ring you already have a route: hydroxide on 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene is an SNAr, two sections back. (Industrially, phenol comes from cumene and oxygen, which is neither.)

What carries forward

One structural change — OH on a ring rather than on a chain — and three consequences: pKa 10 instead of 16, because the phenoxide is delocalized; a strongly activated ring, because the same lone pair donates; and a C–O bond that no substitution reaction will touch. The bicarbonate test separating a phenol from a carboxylic acid is worth remembering as a practical fact, not just a number.
