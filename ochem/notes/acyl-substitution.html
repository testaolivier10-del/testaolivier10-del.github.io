Addition, then elimination — "addition-elimination"

Nucleophilic acyl substitution begins exactly like the nucleophilic addition from Module 9: a nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, and the pi electrons move to oxygen, forming a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate. But this substrate has a leaving group (X) already attached — so instead of simply protonating the alkoxide as the final step (as a ketone or aldehyde would), the electrons flow right back down from oxygen to reform the C=O pi bond, and X leaves, restoring a neutral, trigonal-planar carbonyl with the nucleophile now in place of X.

Why acyl substitution happens instead of simple addition

A ketone's tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate has no good leaving group attached (the two carbon substituents can't leave as carbanions), so it just gets protonated and stays as an alcohol — permanent addition. A carboxylic acid derivative's tetrahedral intermediate has a leaving group (Cl⁻, RCOO⁻, RO⁻, or, reluctantly, R₂N⁻) that can depart to reform a stable carbonyl — kicking that group out and regenerating the more stable, resonance-supported C=O is thermodynamically favorable, so substitution (not simple addition) is the outcome.

The Esters & Amides reactivity ladder is really a leaving-group-ability ranking in disguise: a better leaving group in the tetrahedral intermediate collapses back to product faster, which is exactly why acid chlorides (best leaving group, Cl⁻) react fastest and amides (worst leaving group, R₂N⁻) react slowest.

Worked example: converting an ester to an amide

An amine (nucleophile) attacks an ester's carbonyl carbon, forming a tetrahedral intermediate bearing both the incoming amine nitrogen and the original OR group. Comparing leaving-group ability, RO⁻ (conjugate base of an alcohol, pKa ≈16) is a much better leaving group than R₂N⁻ (conjugate base of an amine, pKa ≈ 38) would be, so the alkoxide leaves preferentially, regenerating the carbonyl — now as an amide. This is a direct, practical example of "down the reactivity ladder" conversion from the previous lesson.

Fischer esterification: acid catalysis makes the reverse direction work too

Making an ester directly from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol (Fischer esterification) needs acid catalysis specifically because a carboxylic acid's own leaving group, OH⁻, is a poor one on its own (Module 8's central lesson). Protonating the tetrahedral intermediate's leaving oxygen first converts OH⁻ into neutral water — a much better leaving group — letting the substitution proceed despite starting from one of the least reactive positions on the ladder in the "wrong" direction. This is the same protonation-as-activation strategy you've now seen in alcohol chemistry, ether cleavage, and hemiacetal/acetal formation.
