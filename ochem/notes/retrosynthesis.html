Synthesis & Retrosynthesis · Section 104 of 118

Thinking backwards

Practice this — interactive lesson

Every chapter so far has run forwards: here is a substrate, here is a reagent, what is the product. Synthesis runs the other way. You are given a target and asked how to build it, and the number of possible routes is far too large to search by trying reactions and seeing where they lead.

The technique that makes the problem tractable is retrosynthesis: start at the target and work backwards, one bond at a time, toward something you can buy.

The notation, and why it is different

A retrosynthetic step is written with a double-lined open arrow, ⇒, and it means "could be made from" — not "reacts to give". It is deliberately a different arrow from the ordinary reaction arrow, because it points in the opposite direction in time.

Breaking a bond in that backwards direction is a disconnection. It is a thought, not a reaction: nothing in a flask breaks the bond you just crossed out. You are asking which two pieces, joined by a reaction you know, would give this bond.

There is a second legal move backwards, and it is not a disconnection. A functional group interconversion, written FGI above the ⇒ arrow, rewrites one group into another without cutting the skeleton: "this ketone could be made from this alkyne", "this amine could be made from this nitrile". Nothing gets shorter, so it can feel like no progress — but the target often becomes one that a disconnection can reach, and most real analyses alternate: FGI to expose a pattern, disconnect, FGI again.

A disconnection is only legitimate if you can name the forward reaction that would make that bond. "Cut it in half and hope" is not a disconnection. If you cannot say what reagents would rejoin the pieces, you have drawn a picture rather than planned a synthesis.

Synthons and their synthetic equivalents

When you disconnect a bond you get two idealized fragments, usually charged, called synthons. Disconnecting the C–C bond of a secondary alcohol next to the carbinol carbon gives, say, a carbanion R⁻ and an aldehyde.

R⁻ is not a bottle on a shelf. The reagent that behaves like it is a synthetic equivalent — here, a Grignard reagent RMgBr. The synthon is the idea; the synthetic equivalent is what you actually order.

R⁻ (carbanion)RMgBr, RLi
R–C≡C⁻terminal alkyne + strong base (NaNH₂)

⁻CH₂–CO–R (enolate)the ketone + base

R–CO⁺ (acylium)acyl chloride + AlCl₃

Notice the pattern: nucleophilic synthons are things that attack and electrophilic ones are things that get attacked. Every disconnection produces one of each, because that is what a polar bond-forming reaction is.

Where to cut

Not all disconnections are equally useful. The ones worth trying first:

  • Next to a functional group. Functional groups are where reactivity lives, so bonds near them are the ones you have reactions for. A C–C bond to a carbinol carbon suggests a Grignard; a C–C bond β to a carbonyl suggests an aldol.
  • At a branch point. Branches are where a molecule was assembled, and disconnecting one usually splits the target into two comparably sized, simpler pieces.
  • At a ring, if the ring is six-membered and has an alkene in it. That is the retro-Diels–Alder pattern, and it turns one ring into two open-chain pieces in a single step.
  • Symmetrically. If the target is symmetric, a disconnection through the middle halves the problem.

A structural pattern that signals a particular disconnection is called a retron. An alcohol with a C–C bond to the carbinol carbon is the retron for a Grignard; a β-hydroxy carbonyl is the retron for an aldol; an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl is the retron for an aldol condensation; a 1,3-dicarbonyl is the retron for a Claisen; a 1,5-dicarbonyl is the retron for a Michael addition (the conjugate addition itself is taught in Enolate Chemistry); a cyclohexene is the retron for a Diels–Alder. Learning to see retrons is most of what makes an experienced chemist fast at this.

Check an aldol or Claisen disconnection for symmetry of reactivity before you accept it. If both fragments carry α hydrogens, either one can enolize and either can be attacked, so the forward reaction gives four products rather than one. The disconnection is clean only when one partner has no α hydrogen — benzaldehyde, formaldehyde, an ester of formic acid — or when one enolate is formed quantitatively first with a strong hindered base. A disconnection that looks fine on paper and gives a mixture in the flask is still a wrong answer.
Worked example — one disconnection, done properly

Target: 2-phenylbutan-2-ol, PhC(CH₃)(OH)CH₂CH₃.

Spot the retron. A tertiary alcohol with three different groups on the carbinol carbon. That is the Grignard retron: a C–C bond to a carbinol carbon can be disconnected.

Disconnect. Cut the bond to the ethyl group. The synthons are CH₃CH₂⁻ and the ketone PhCOCH₃ (acetophenone).

Name the equivalents. CH₃CH₂MgBr and acetophenone — both commercially available, so the route is one step.

Check forwards. CH₃CH₂MgBr attacks the ketone carbonyl; aqueous workup protonates the alkoxide; product is 2-phenylbutan-2-ol. ✓

Note there were three possible disconnections at that carbinol carbon — the ethyl, the methyl, or the phenyl. All three are legitimate Grignard disconnections, and the one to prefer is whichever leaves the most available starting materials. Having several valid answers is normal in synthesis, and a question asking for "a" synthesis rather than "the" synthesis is telling you so.

Worked example — two disconnections, and which one to do first

Target: hexan-3-one, CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₂CH₃. Permitted: acetylene and bromoethane.

Count first. Six carbons in the target, two in acetylene and two in bromoethane. At least two C–C bonds have to be made, so this is not a one-disconnection problem, and the first job is to find a move that makes those bonds visible.

Retro step 1 — the functional group is not where the bond is. A ketone in the middle of a chain is the retron for hydration of an internal alkyne: an FGI run backwards. Write hexan-3-one ⇒ hex-3-yne. No C–C bond has been cut yet, and that is the point — you have traded a target with a carbonyl for one whose skeleton is built by a reaction you know.

Retro step 2 — now disconnect. Hex-3-yne, CH₃CH₂C≡CCH₂CH₃, is an internal alkyne, which is the acetylide-alkylation retron. Cut one of the C–C bonds at the triple bond: the synthons are CH₃CH₂C≡C⁻ and CH₃CH₂⁺, whose equivalents are but-1-yne with NaNH₂ and bromoethane.

Retro step 3 — again. But-1-yne, CH₃CH₂C≡CH, is one more cut from acetylene: HC≡C⁻ and CH₃CH₂⁺, that is acetylene with NaNH₂ and bromoethane a second time. Every piece is now purchasable, so the analysis stops.

Write it forwards.
1. NaNH₂ in liquid NH₃, then CH₃CH₂Br → but-1-yne. The halide is primary, which the SN2 requires.
2. NaNH₂, then CH₃CH₂Br → hex-3-yne.
3. H₂O, H₂SO₄, HgSO₄ → hexan-3-one.

Check the last step. Hex-3-yne is symmetric, so Markovnikov hydration has only one answer to give: water can add either way round and both ways lead to the same ketone. That check matters, because an unsymmetrical internal alkyne would give a mixture of two ketones and the route would be worthless.

Notice the shape of the analysis. The first retro move changed a functional group rather than cutting a bond, and that is what made the C–C disconnections visible. Going the other way round — trying to cut C–C bonds next to the ketone — leads to aldol fragments that are harder to buy and, with α hydrogens on both sides, would not have been a clean disconnection anyway.

Knowing when to stop

You keep disconnecting until every piece is something you can reasonably buy: simple alkyl halides, small alcohols, benzene and its common derivatives, acetylene, simple ketones and esters. A question usually tells you the permitted starting materials, and that constraint is the real definition of "done".

The other stopping rule is practical. Each step loses material, so a route of ten steps at 80% each returns about 11% overall. Fewer steps is genuinely better, and a shorter route with a slightly awkward step usually beats a longer elegant one.

What carries forward

Retrosynthesis is a way of using everything else in the course rather than a new body of facts.
