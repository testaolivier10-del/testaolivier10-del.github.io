Ring strain makes epoxides reactive

An epoxide is a three-membered ring: two carbons and one oxygen, with bond angles forced down to about 60°, far from the ~109.5° an sp³ oxygen or carbon actually wants (recall cyclopropane's 27.5 kcal/mol of ring strain from the Cyclohexanes lesson — an epoxide carries a comparable strain penalty). Opening the ring releases that strain as a powerful thermodynamic driving force, which is why epoxides react readily with nucleophiles that would never touch an ordinary, strain-free ether.

Base-catalyzed opening: SN2 at the less hindered carbon

Under basic or neutral conditions, a strong nucleophile (RO⁻, CN⁻, etc.) directly attacks the epoxide in a textbook SN2 mechanism: backside attack with inversion, at whichever carbon is less hindered (less substituted) — exactly the same steric logic as any other SN2 reaction from Module 6. The strained C–O bond breaks as the nucleophile attacks. The oxygen, now an alkoxide, picks up a proton in a final workup step — "workup" just means the separate step at the end of a reaction where you add something, usually dilute aqueous acid, to neutralize a charged intermediate and get a handleable neutral product out. What you are left with is a 1,2-difunctionalized product: a diol if the nucleophile was hydroxide or water, or a new C–Nu bond otherwise.

Because it's a genuine backside SN2 attack, base-catalyzed epoxide opening always gives anti (opposite-face) stereochemistry at the two carbons — directly analogous to the anti addition you saw for halogenation of alkenes.

Acid-catalyzed opening: attack shifts to the more substituted carbon

Under acidic conditions, the epoxide oxygen is protonated first, which does two things: it makes the ring an even better electrophile, and it makes the C–O bonds heterolyze more easily toward a carbocation-like transition state. Because of this partial positive-charge buildup, the nucleophile (often just the solvent, like water or an alcohol) now attacks the more substituted carbon — the one that can better stabilize the positive character in the transition state — even though the mechanism is still concerted enough to remain backside (anti) with respect to the leaving oxygen.

Same substrate, opposite regiochemistry — this is not a new rule

This acid/base regiochemistry switch is the exact same underlying logic you've now seen three times: SN1-like character (carbocation stabilization matters, attack favors the more substituted carbon) versus pure SN2 character (sterics dominate, attack favors the less hindered carbon) — precisely the substrate/mechanism framework from the Substrate & Solvent Effects lesson, just applied to a strained ring instead of an open-chain leaving group.
