Epoxides

Practice this — interactive lesson

Take the least reactive functional group in the chapter and bend it into a three-membered ring, and it becomes one of the most useful electrophiles in organic synthesis. Epoxides are where ring strain, SN2 stereochemistry and the acid/base mechanism split from Substitution & Elimination all come together — and they give a 1,2-difunctionalized product with defined stereochemistry, which is hard to get any other way.

Ring strain makes epoxides reactive

An epoxide is a three-membered ring: two carbons and an oxygen, with bond angles forced down to about 60° against the roughly 109.5° that sp³ atoms want. The strain is comparable to cyclopropane's 27.5 kcal/mol, and it comes from both angle strain and the eclipsing of every substituent around the ring.

Opening the ring releases that strain, which is a powerful thermodynamic driving force. It is why a nucleophile that would never touch an ordinary ether will attack an epoxide readily, even though the leaving group is the same alkoxide in both cases. The strain is paying for the departure of a bad leaving group.

There is a kinetic contribution too, and it is worth unpacking rather than accepting. The orbitals on each ring atom point at roughly 109°, but the atoms they have to reach sit at 60°, so the bonds cannot form along the line between the nuclei: the electron density bulges outside the triangle in what are called bent or "banana" bonds. Off-axis overlap is poorer overlap, which makes those C–O bonds both weaker and more exposed to attack from outside the ring. So the strain does not only make the product favorable — it lowers the barrier to getting there.

Naming them

Three naming styles are in circulation and you will meet all three. As a ring, the parent is oxirane, and the oxygen is atom 1, so the carbons are C2 and C3: the epoxide of 2-methylpropene is 2,2-dimethyloxirane. As a substituent on a chain, the prefix is epoxy with the two carbons it bridges: 2-methyl-2,3-epoxybutane. And the two simplest members keep their old trivial names, ethylene oxide and propylene oxide. Nothing chemical turns on the choice, but "C1" of an oxirane is the oxygen, so an answer that puts a nucleophile on C1 is describing something that cannot happen.

Making an epoxide

Direct epoxidation of an alkene with a peroxyacid. This one is already done. meta-Chloroperoxybenzoic acid, mCPBA, hands a single oxygen to an alkene in one concerted step, and Alkenes & Alkynes worked through both the butterfly transition state and its stereochemical consequences. The two facts this section leans on are that the transfer is syn, to one face of the alkene, and that it is stereospecific: the alkene's geometry walks straight through into the ring, so a cis alkene gives the cis-substituted epoxide and a trans alkene the trans one.

Halohydrin cyclization. This route you have already built the pieces for. Take the halohydrin from Alkenes & Alkynes’s bromine-in-water reaction, deprotonate the OH with base, and let the resulting alkoxide displace the bromide by an intramolecular SN2. Backside attack means the oxygen and the halide must be anti across the two carbons — which they are, because the halohydrin was made by anti addition. Two anti steps in a row, and the ring closes.

Base-catalyzed opening: SN2 at the less hindered carbon

Under basic or neutral conditions a strong nucleophile — alkoxide, cyanide, acetylide, an amine, a thiolate (RS⁻), hydride from LiAlH₄ or a Grignard reagent, the last two previewed here and taught in Oxidation & Reduction and Organometallics — attacks the epoxide directly in a textbook SN2: backside attack, inversion at the carbon attacked, and the strained C–O bond breaking as the new bond forms.

The nucleophile attacks the less hindered carbon, exactly the steric logic of any SN2. The oxygen leaves as an alkoxide and picks up a proton during workup — "workup" meaning the separate final step where you add something, usually dilute aqueous acid, to neutralize charged intermediates and get a handleable neutral product.

What you get is a 1,2-difunctionalized compound: a diol if the nucleophile was hydroxide, or a new C–Nu bond with an adjacent OH otherwise. Opening an epoxide with a Grignard or an acetylide forms a carbon–carbon bond and installs an alcohol two carbons along, which makes it a valuable synthetic move: ethylene oxide plus the acetylide of propyne, then workup, gives pent-3-yn-1-ol — the chain grew by exactly two carbons and the OH landed where the ring oxygen was. When a synthesis problem asks you to add two carbons and an alcohol at once, an epoxide is the reagent it wants.

Because it is a genuine backside SN2, opening gives anti stereochemistry at the two carbons under acid as well as base — the nucleophile and the resulting OH end up on opposite faces. The clean test case is cyclohexene oxide: hydrolyze it under either set of conditions and you get trans-1,2-cyclohexanediol, never the cis diastereomer. On the ring the nucleophile has to arrive along the axis of the breaking C–O bond, so the product is formed trans-diaxial first and then relaxes by a ring flip; that is the same geometric argument as the anti addition of bromine to an alkene, and for the same reason — a three-membered ring blocks one face completely.

Acid-catalyzed opening: attack shifts to the more substituted carbon

Under acidic conditions the oxygen is protonated first, which does two things at once. It makes the ring a much better electrophile, and it makes the C–O bonds heterolyze more easily toward a carbocation-like transition state.

Because positive charge is developing on carbon in that transition state, the nucleophile — often just the solvent, water or an alcohol — attacks the more substituted carbon, the one better able to stabilize it. The mechanism remains concerted enough to stay backside, so the stereochemistry is still anti. Only the regiochemistry has flipped.

Note the size of the claim, though. Acid does not overrule sterics by decree; it adds a second, opposing effect, and which one wins depends on how much cation stabilization is actually on offer. With a tertiary (or benzylic) carbon on one side the electronic effect is decisive and the selectivity is clean. Between a secondary and a primary carbon it is weaker, and between two similar carbons sterics can still carry the day and give mixtures. Exam substrates are drawn to make the contrast obvious — one clearly more substituted carbon — so the rule holds there; a real molecule is under no such obligation.

Worked example — one epoxide, two products

2-Methyl-2,3-epoxybutane, with a fully substituted carbon on one side and a CH on the other.

With NaOCH₃ in methanol: SN2 conditions, so methoxide attacks the less substituted carbon. The methoxy group ends up there and the OH on the more substituted carbon.

With CH₃OH and catalytic H₂SO₄: the protonated epoxide develops cation character at the more substituted carbon, so methanol attacks there. The methoxy and the OH swap positions relative to the first case.

Same substrate, same nucleophile, opposite regiochemistry — set entirely by whether the conditions are acidic or basic. Both products are anti.

Do not memorize this as a separate rule. It is the SN1/SN2 spectrum from Substitution & Elimination applied to a strained ring. Basic conditions mean a strong nucleophile, an unassisted leaving group and pure SN2 character, so sterics decide. Acidic conditions mean a weak nucleophile, an activated leaving group and partial cation character, so cation stability decides. The same reasoning, the same answer, in a new setting.

Why this matters beyond the exam

Epoxides are electrophilic enough to react with the nucleophilic groups in DNA, which is why many epoxides are mutagens and why the body's own metabolism of aromatic compounds is a route to carcinogenicity: the liver oxidizes a benzene ring to an epoxide spanning two of its carbons — an arene oxide — precisely in order to make it water-soluble enough to excrete, and that intermediate is an electrophile looking for a nucleophile. It is also the basis of several anticancer drugs, which are deliberately designed to alkylate DNA. And one name worth recognizing now and meeting properly later: the Sharpless asymmetric epoxidation (a titanium alkoxide, a single enantiomer of diethyl tartrate, and tert-butyl hydroperoxide, on an allylic alcohol) delivers the oxygen to one chosen face and so makes a single enantiomer of an epoxide from an achiral alkene — a share of the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Treat it as a label for now; the asymmetric-catalysis machinery behind it is beyond this course.

What carries forward

Epoxide opening with a carbon nucleophile is a standard chain-extension move in synthesis, and the acid/base regiochemical switch is a favorite exam question. More generally this section closes Alcohols, Ethers & Related Chemistry by making the point that reactivity is often a matter of strain and activation rather than of functional group identity: the same C–O bond is inert in diethyl ether and eager in ethylene oxide.
