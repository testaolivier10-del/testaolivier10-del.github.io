The three particles

An atom is built from three particles. Protons: positive charge, sit in the nucleus, mass ≈ 1 atomic mass unit (amu). Neutrons: no charge, also in the nucleus, mass ≈ 1 amu. Electrons: negative charge, occupy the space around the nucleus, mass ≈ 1/1800 amu (negligible compared to protons/neutrons). The number of protons is an element's atomic number (Z) and is what defines which element it is — change the proton count and you have a different element entirely.

Isotopes and mass number

An element's neutron count can vary. Atoms of the same element with different neutron counts are isotopes. Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons, written as a superscript (e.g. carbon-12, ¹²C, has 6 protons + 6 neutrons; carbon-13, ¹³C, has 6 protons + 7 neutrons). Isotopes matter later in the course: ¹H and ¹³C are exactly what NMR spectroscopy detects, and mass spectrometry (Module 14) literally measures mass number to help determine a molecule's formula.

Shells and valence electrons

Electrons fill shells around the nucleus: the inner (K) shell holds up to 2, the next (L) shell up to 8. A neutral atom has exactly as many electrons as protons. The electrons in the outermost shell — the valence electrons — are the only ones that participate in bonding.

Building nitrogen (Z=7)

Nitrogen has 7 protons, so a neutral atom has 7 electrons: 2 fill the K shell first, leaving 5 for the L shell. Those 5 L-shell electrons are nitrogen's valence electrons.

Why valence electrons matter: the octet rule

Atoms are most stable when their outer shell is completely full — 8 electrons for almost everything you'll meet (2 for hydrogen, since its only shell is the K shell). An atom with a nearly-empty or nearly-full outer shell is unstable and will gain, lose, or share electrons with other atoms to reach a full octet. Sharing electrons between two atoms so both count them toward their own octet is exactly what a covalent bond is.

Ions: what "cation" and "carbocation" actually mean

An atom that loses an electron becomes positively charged overall — a cation. An atom that gains an electron becomes negatively charged overall — an anion. This vocabulary reappears constantly: a carbocation (Module 6, SN1/E1) is a carbon that's lost enough electron density to carry a positive charge; a carbanion is a carbon-centered anion. Same root idea as sodium losing an electron here, just applied to carbon later.

Sodium (Z=11): configuration 2, 8, 1 — just 1 valence electron, far from a full shell, so it readily gives that electron up (becoming Na⁺, a cation). That single fact predicts most of sodium's reactivity.
