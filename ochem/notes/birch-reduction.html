Aromatic Follow-Through · Section 111 of 117

Birch reduction

Practice this — interactive lesson

Benzene resists almost everything. Catalytic hydrogenation reduces alkenes and alkynes readily and leaves a benzene ring alone unless you force it with high pressure and heat — and when you do force it, you get the fully saturated cyclohexane. What you cannot get that way is the interesting product: the ring reduced partly, stopping at a cyclohexadiene.

The Birch reduction stops there, and it stops there because it works by a completely different mechanism.

The conditions and the mechanism

Sodium (or lithium) dissolved in liquid ammonia, with an alcohol present as a proton source. The ammonia turns deep blue, which is solvated electrons.

The alcohol is usually EtOH or t-BuOH, and the choice is not decoration. It has to be acidic enough to protonate a carbanion, yet far weaker an acid than those intermediates are basic, or it would simply consume the solvated electrons before they reached the ring. Ammonia itself, at pKa 38, is not acidic enough for the job, which is why a proton source has to be added at all.

  • An electron adds to the ring, giving a radical anion.
  • The alcohol protonates it.
  • A second electron adds, giving a carbanion.
  • The alcohol protonates that.

Two electrons, two protons, added one at a time in alternation. The product is a 1,4-cyclohexadiene — note, not the conjugated 1,3-isomer, which is more stable.

This is the same electron-then-proton pattern as the Na/NH3 reduction of an alkyne to a trans alkene. One mechanism, two chapters: dissolving metal reductions add electrons where catalytic hydrogenation adds a surface.

Getting the unconjugated product from a reaction that could have given the conjugated one is the thing to notice. The reaction is under kinetic control: the second protonation happens at the central carbon of the delocalized anion, and that lands the two double bonds 1,4 rather than 1,3. Stability never gets a vote.

It is worth naming the intermediate, because the answer is visible in it. After the first electron, the first proton and the second electron you have a cyclohexadienyl anion — the species one step earlier, after only the electron and the proton, is the cyclohexadienyl radical: one sp³ carbon and, running round the other five, a delocalized pentadienyl anion. Push the charge round and it lands on three of those five carbons — the two ends, which are the carbons flanking the sp³ one, and the middle one, directly across the ring. The alcohol protonates that middle carbon. Be careful how you justify it: in the pentadienyl anion the non-bonding orbital that carries the charge has coefficients of equal size at all three of those carbons, so the middle one is not the richest — it is simply the one that is protonated, and that is an observation about rate, not about charge density. Count what is left: two sp³ carbons directly opposite each other, and two double bonds between them. That is a 1,4-diene, and it fell out of the intermediate rather than out of a rule.

Substituents decide where the double bonds end up

This is what makes the Birch useful rather than a curiosity, and it is the most-tested part of the section. The rule inverts depending on the substituent:

Ring bearsThe substituted carbon ends upProduct
Electron-donating group (OCH3, CH3)On a double bond1-substituted cyclohexa-1,4-diene
Electron-withdrawing group (COOH, COR)On an sp³ carbon1-substituted cyclohexa-2,5-diene

The reason is where the carbanion can best sit. A withdrawing group stabilizes negative charge on its own carbon, so the intermediate puts the charge there and that carbon gets protonated — ending up sp³. A donating group destabilizes charge on its own carbon, so the charge goes elsewhere, that carbon is not protonated, and it stays part of a double bond.

With benzoic acid there is one wrinkle worth naming: under these conditions the COOH is deprotonated first, so what you are really reducing is the benzoate. The regiochemical conclusion is unchanged, because a carboxylate is still an acceptor.

The two rules are easy to swap, and the mnemonic people invent for them is backwards as often as not. Do not memorize “donor → 1,4”. Ask where the carbanion is, then remember that the carbanion is the carbon that gets protonated and therefore the carbon that comes out sp³. Second trap: the product is not the conjugated diene, whatever the substituent, and it is not cyclohexene or cyclohexane either — the Birch adds exactly two hydrogens.
Worked example — predict the product from 4-methylanisole

Step 1. Name the substituents by type. OCH3 is a strong donor. CH3 is a weak donor. They are para to each other, so there is no conflict: both want the carbanion to stay away from their own carbon.

Step 2. Apply the one question. The carbanion avoids C1, which bears the OCH3, and avoids C4, which bears the CH3. It sits, and is protonated, on a carbon that carries neither.

Step 3. Place the sp³ carbons. They are the two carbons that take protons, and they always come out para to each other. Here that is C3 and C6, both unsubstituted.

Step 4. Draw the product. 1-methoxy-4-methylcyclohexa-1,4-diene: double bonds C1=C2 and C4=C5, sp³ carbons at C3 and C6, with both substituents still sitting on double-bond carbons.

Step 5. Sanity check. Both substituted carbons stayed unsaturated, which is what donors do, and the two double bonds came out 1,4. Both boxes ticked.

The follow-on: that product is still an enol ether. Dilute acid hydrolyzes it to 4-methylcyclohex-3-en-1-one — unconjugated, as always.

Why you would want a 1,4-cyclohexadiene

Because it is a handle nothing else gives you. An unconjugated diene can be selectively epoxidized, hydrated, or hydrogenated at one double bond; it is also an enol ether if the substituent was a methoxy group — an OR sitting directly on a C=C, the oxygen analogue of the enamine from the previous chapter, and it hydrolyzes the same way, through the same protonated-at-carbon intermediate — so mild acid hydrolyzes it to a ketone. Be precise about which one: mild conditions give cyclohex-3-en-1-one, the β,γ-unsaturated isomer, and moving the double bond into conjugation takes a deliberate isomerization with stronger acid or base. Only after that step do you have the cyclohex-2-en-1-one the Robinson chapter was about — and only the conjugated isomer is a Michael acceptor. One more consequence of the same argument: on naphthalene the Birch reduces only one ring, to 1,4-dihydronaphthalene, because once that ring is done the molecule is an ordinary benzene attached to an ordinary diene, and an electron has no reason to go to either.

What carries forward

Na or Li in liquid ammonia with an alcohol, giving a 1,4-cyclohexadiene by alternating electron and proton additions. Donating groups end up on a double bond; withdrawing groups end up on an sp³ carbon; and both rules come from asking where the carbanion is most stable. It is the only way in this course to reduce a benzene ring partway rather than not at all or completely.
