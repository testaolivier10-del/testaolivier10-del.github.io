The definition

A stereocenter (also called a chirality center or stereogenic center) is most commonly an sp³ carbon bonded to four different groups. Because all four groups are different, swapping any two of them creates a different stereoisomer — this is exactly what generates chirality at that carbon.

Worked example

2-butanol, CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₂CH₃: the second carbon is bonded to OH, H, CH₃, and CH₂CH₃ — four genuinely different groups, so it's a stereocenter. Compare to 2-propanol, CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃: the central carbon is bonded to OH, H, and two identical CH₃ groups — only three different groups, so it is not a stereocenter, and 2-propanol is achiral.

A quick way to rule a carbon out fast: if it's attached to two identical groups (like two CH₃, or two H), it cannot be a stereocenter — you need four distinct groups, not three or two duplicated. Also watch for carbons with a double bond or in a ring with only 3 total attachments — those aren't sp³ centers with four groups and are never stereocenters this way (though ring carbons can still be stereocenters if they carry four different substituents through the ring and off it).

Counting stereoisomers: the 2ⁿ rule (a starting estimate)

For a molecule with n stereocenters, the maximum possible number of stereoisomers is 2ⁿ — each stereocenter can independently be one of two configurations. A molecule with 2 stereocenters has up to 4 possible stereoisomers; with 3 stereocenters, up to 8. This is a maximum, not always the actual count — the Meso Compounds lesson shows an important exception where internal symmetry reduces the real number below 2ⁿ.
