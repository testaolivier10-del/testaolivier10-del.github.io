The definition

Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other. This only becomes possible once a molecule has two or more stereocenters: with two stereocenters, there are up to 2² = 4 stereoisomers total, forming two enantiomeric pairs — and any molecule from one pair compared to any molecule from the other pair is a diastereomer relationship.

Same vs. different configuration, stereocenter by stereocenter

For a molecule with stereocenters labeled 1 and 2: flipping both stereocenters gives the enantiomer (the full mirror image). Flipping only one of the two (but not the other) gives a diastereomer — same configuration at one center, opposite at the other. In general, for n stereocenters, flipping all n gives the enantiomer; flipping any smaller, non-empty subset gives a diastereomer.

A useful shorthand: enantiomers are related by inverting every stereocenter; diastereomers are related by inverting some, but not all, stereocenters.

Why diastereomers have different physical properties

Unlike enantiomers, diastereomers are not mirror images of each other, so there's no symmetry reason for them to share physical properties. Diastereomers genuinely have different melting points, boiling points, densities, solubilities, and polarities — because they are, in every meaningful sense, different compounds, not mirror-image copies of the same one. This makes them separable by ordinary techniques like distillation, recrystallization, or column chromatography — unlike enantiomers, which require a chiral technique to separate.

Worked example: cis/trans as a special case of diastereomers

Cis and trans isomers of a disubstituted ring or alkene are actually a specific, common example of diastereomers — they are stereoisomers, not mirror images of each other, and (consistent with the diastereomer pattern) they have measurably different physical properties. cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene, for instance, have different boiling points (3.7°C vs. 0.9°C) and different dipole moments.
