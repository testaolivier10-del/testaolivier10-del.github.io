Benzylic reactivity

Practice this — interactive lesson

Start with toluene, C6H5–CH3. Its CH3 carbon is not part of the ring, but it is bonded straight to a ring carbon. A carbon in that spot is called benzylic: an sp³ carbon attached directly to a benzene ring. The CH2 of ethylbenzene and the CH of isopropylbenzene are benzylic too.

A benzylic carbon behaves unlike any other sp³ carbon in the molecule, and the reason is resonance. When a cation, a radical or an anion forms on the benzylic carbon, its empty orbital, odd electron or lone pair spreads into the ring. That one idea explains every reaction on this page.

Benzylic means beside the ring, never in it. The benzylic carbon sits outside the ring and is bonded to a ring carbon. The ring carbon itself is not benzylic, and neither is a chain carbon two bonds out from the ring: the stabilization does not run down the chain.

The same boundary separates two kinds of OH. An OH on the benzylic carbon makes a benzylic alcohol. It behaves like an ordinary alcohol but ionizes more easily, because the cation it leaves behind is benzylic. An OH on a ring carbon makes a phenol, which behaves like no ordinary alcohol and is the subject of the next section.

Why this one position gets special treatment

Take the cation first. When bromide leaves benzyl bromide, C6H5CH2Br, the CH2 carbon is left with a positive charge right next to the ring’s double bonds. Resonance can then move the charge off that carbon and into the ring.

The first curved arrow below moves the electrons of the nearest ring double bond toward the CH2 carbon. They become a C=CH2 double bond, and the plus moves onto an ortho carbon, the ring carbon next to the point of attachment. Two more arrows move it to the para carbon, across the ring, and then to the other ortho carbon.

So four carbons share the charge: the benzylic carbon, both ortho carbons and the para carbon. The two meta carbons never carry it. The phenoxide ion and the Meisenheimer anion from the SNAr section put their charge on the same ring positions.

This is the argument you met for allylic cations, with a benzene ring in place of the alkene. Count the carbons that carry the charge: an allyl cation has two, its two end carbons, while a benzylic cation has four.

The radical and the anion use the same four carbons. Pull a hydrogen atom off toluene’s CH3 and you get the benzyl radical, whose odd electron spreads into the ring. Pull off a proton instead and you get the benzyl anion, whose lone pair spreads the same way.

Substitution at a benzylic position

Benzyl chloride, C6H5CH2Cl, is a primary alkyl halide. An ordinary primary halide such as 1-chloropropane reacts only by SN2. Benzyl chloride reacts well by both SN1 and SN2.

SN1 is fast because the cation is benzylic, and so stabilized by the resonance above. A primary benzylic cation is about as stable as a tertiary alkyl cation, so a primary benzylic halide ionizes where an ordinary primary halide never would. Benzyl chloride even undergoes solvolysis in water or ethanol, where the solvent itself is the nucleophile.

SN2 is fast too, for a different reason. Halfway through an SN2 reaction, the carbon under attack is partly bonded to the nucleophile and partly bonded to the leaving group. At that moment it is flat, like an sp² carbon, with a p orbital along the line from nucleophile to leaving group. On a benzylic carbon that p orbital lies parallel to the ring’s p orbitals and overlaps with them. The overlap spreads out the charge that builds up in the transition state, and that lowers the barrier.

A benzylic CH2 is also unhindered. Besides the ring it carries only two hydrogens, so nothing blocks the nucleophile’s path to the back of the carbon. The rate numbers for both effects are in the SN2 section of Substitution & Elimination.

The conditions then decide the stereochemistry. (R)-1-Phenylethyl bromide, C6H5CH(Br)CH3, has a stereocenter at its benzylic carbon. In water, a weak nucleophile, it reacts by SN1. The bromide leaves first, and water can attack the flat cation from either face, so the 1-phenylethanol that forms is racemic. (The ion pair from the SN1 section can leave a slight excess of inversion here too.)

With a good nucleophile in acetone, the same bromide reacts by SN2 instead. The nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the bromine, and the product is inverted. One substrate gives two stereochemical outcomes, chosen by the conditions, which is possible only because it runs both mechanisms.

That double ability is rare. Most substrates are good at one mechanism and poor at the other, because what favors one disfavors the other. A benzylic halide is good at both, which is why benzyl bromide turns up so often as an alkylating agent, a reagent that attaches an alkyl group to a nucleophile. The malonic ester and enamine alkylations in Enolate Chemistry are two places it is used.

Radical bromination, and the reagent that makes it selective

Ethylbenzene has two kinds of C–H on its side chain: two on the benzylic CH2 and three on the CH3. Treat it with NBS and light, and bromine replaces a benzylic hydrogen almost exclusively. The product is (1-bromoethyl)benzene.

Radical halogenation usually gives mixtures, because a radical will take a hydrogen from almost anywhere in a molecule. A benzylic position changes that, and bond dissociation energies show why. These are the numbers the radical halogenation section used: the energy needed to break a bond into two radicals. A benzylic C–H takes about 90 kcal/mol, against 101 for a primary C–H and 96 for a tertiary one.

Those gaps come from the stability of the benzylic radical, and they are large. Bromination is already the selective halogenation, as that section showed. With the benzylic hydrogen 6 to 11 kcal/mol easier to remove than any other, the mixture problem disappears.

The reagent is NBS (N-bromosuccinimide) with light or a radical initiator. You met it for allylic bromination in How Reactions Happen. It releases Br2 slowly, so the Br2 concentration stays low, and that stops Br2 from adding across any C=C bond in the molecule by the ionic route.

The ring is not brominated either. Putting a bromine on a ring carbon needs Br2 with a Lewis acid such as FeBr3, which is electrophilic aromatic substitution from Aromatic Chemistry.

NBS brominates allylic and benzylic positions for the same reason: both give a radical that resonance stabilizes. So if a molecule has an allylic site and a benzylic site, NBS attacks both, and changing to another brominating reagent will not fix the selectivity.

One more benzylic bond that breaks easily

Hydrogen over a palladium catalyst normally reduces C=C and C≡C bonds and leaves single bonds alone. A benzylic C–O bond is the exception. Take a benzyl ether, R–O–CH2–C6H5. With H2 and Pd/C, the bond between the oxygen and the benzylic carbon breaks, and a hydrogen caps each end. The products are the free alcohol, R–OH, and toluene. Breaking a single bond with H2 this way is called hydrogenolysis.

The reason is the one running through this page. A benzylic bond is weaker than an ordinary one, because whatever is left on the benzylic carbon when the bond breaks is stabilized by the ring.

That makes a benzyl ether a useful protecting group for an alcohol, a group put on to hide the OH during other steps and taken off afterward. Benzyl bromide and a base put it on, by a plain SN2 at a benzylic carbon. It survives most acids and bases, including the aqueous acid that removes an acetal, and H2 with Pd/C takes it off under neutral conditions.

The cost is that H2/Pd reduces anything else it can reach in the same step. A C=C bond elsewhere in the molecule is hydrogenated while the benzyl group comes off, so the protecting group and the rest of the molecule have to be compatible.

Oxidation: the whole side chain becomes a carboxyl

Heat propylbenzene with KMnO4 in aqueous base, then add acid. The product is benzoic acid, C6H5COOH: the three-carbon chain has been cut back to one carbon, and that carbon is now a carboxyl. The rule is general. Any alkyl side chain with at least one benzylic hydrogen is cut back to a single carbon and oxidized to –COOH.

tert-Butylbenzene is the exception, and it shows how the reaction starts. Its benzylic carbon carries three methyl groups and no hydrogen. The oxidation begins by attacking a benzylic C–H, so with none to attack, tert-butylbenzene does not react.

Write the workup as its own step. Under the basic reaction conditions the product is the carboxylate ion, C6H5COO−, and the carboxylic acid appears only after H3O+ is added. Hot Na2Cr2O7 in H2SO4 does the same oxidation in one pot.

The ring itself survives. Hot permanganate cleaves an ordinary C=C bond, as in Alkenes & Alkynes, yet it leaves the benzene ring alone. Aromatic stabilization makes the ring inert to conditions that oxidize almost anything else.

Worked example — benzene to 4-nitrobenzoic acid

Step 1. Work backward from the carboxyl. Electrophilic aromatic substitution cannot put a COOH group on a ring, but oxidizing an alkyl side chain can. So the last step is hot KMnO4, then H3O+.

Step 2. When does the nitro group go on? Not last. By then the ring carries a carboxyl, which is a meta director, so nitration would give 3-nitrobenzoic acid, the wrong isomer. The nitro group has to go on while the ring still carries the methyl, which is an ortho/para director.

Step 3. When does the methyl go on? First. Friedel–Crafts alkylation fails on a ring that carries a nitro group, so the methyl cannot follow the nitration.

The sequence: CH3Cl with AlCl3; then HNO3 with H2SO4, which puts the nitro group para to the methyl (the ortho isomer also forms and is separated off); then hot KMnO4 and acidic workup. Only this order works, because each step would be blocked if it came any earlier or later.

To make the meta isomer, 3-nitrobenzoic acid, swap the last two steps. Oxidize toluene to benzoic acid first, and the carboxyl then directs the nitro group meta.

What carries forward

Benzylic is the position next to the ring, and everything about it follows from resonance into the ring. Cations, radicals and anions are all stabilized there. Substitution runs by either mechanism, NBS brominates there selectively, and hot permanganate cuts any side chain with a benzylic hydrogen back to a carboxylic acid.

When you plan a synthesis by working backward from the target, a benzoic acid should make you ask which alkylbenzene it could come from. Alkylating and then oxidizing is often how a carboxyl gets onto a ring, since no electrophilic substitution delivers one directly.
