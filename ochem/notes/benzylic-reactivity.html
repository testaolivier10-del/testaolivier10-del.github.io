Aromatic Follow-Through · Section 99 of 121

Benzylic reactivity

Practice this — interactive lesson

The benzylic position is the carbon attached directly to a benzene ring. It is not part of the ring, and it behaves unlike any other sp³ carbon in the molecule — because whatever reactive species forms there can be delocalized into the ring.

One idea covers the whole section: a benzylic cation, radical or anion is all stabilized by the same resonance. Put the empty orbital, the odd electron or the lone pair next to the ring and it spreads over four carbons instead of sitting on one.

Benzylic is beside the ring, never in it. The carbon has to be sp³ and attached to a ring carbon. An OH on the benzylic carbon is a benzylic alcohol and behaves like an ordinary alcohol, only more so; an OH on the ring carbon is a phenol and behaves like nothing else — that is the next section. And a carbon two bonds out from the ring is just an ordinary carbon: the stabilization does not run down the chain.

This is the same argument as allylic stabilization, with a benzene ring in place of a single alkene. Counted the same way — positions that actually carry the charge — an allyl system has two, its two end carbons, while a benzylic system has four: the benzylic carbon plus both ortho positions and the para one.

Substitution at a benzylic position

Benzylic halides are unusual in doing both SN1 and SN2 well.

  • SN1 is fast because the cation is stabilized. A primary benzylic halide will ionize where an ordinary primary halide never would.
  • SN2 is also fast, for a different reason: the ring's π system stabilizes the transition state, and a benzylic carbon is usually unhindered.
  • The stereochemistry then follows the mechanism, not the substrate. (R)-1-phenylethyl bromide solvolyzed in water gives racemic 1-phenylethanol, because the planar benzylic cation is attacked from both faces; the same halide with a good nucleophile in acetone inverts cleanly. One substrate, two stereochemical outcomes, chosen by the conditions — which is possible precisely because it runs both mechanisms.

That combination is rare. Most substrates are good at one mechanism and poor at the other; a benzylic halide is good at both, which is why benzyl bromide turns up constantly as an alkylating agent — including in the malonic ester and enamine chemistry of Enolate Chemistry.

Radical bromination, and the reagent that makes it selective

Radical halogenation normally gives mixtures, because a radical will abstract a hydrogen from anywhere. On a molecule with a benzylic position it does not: the benzylic radical is so much more stable that abstraction happens there almost exclusively. The numbers are the same kind you used for radical halogenation: a benzylic C–H runs about 90 kcal/mol against 101 for a primary alkane C–H and 96 for a tertiary one. Ten or eleven kcal/mol is an enormous margin at the transition state, which is why the mixture problem simply disappears.

The reagent is NBS — N-bromosuccinimide — with light or a radical initiator. NBS works by keeping the Br2 concentration very low, which suppresses the ionic addition that would otherwise attack any alkene present.

NBS is the same reagent used for allylic bromination, for exactly the same reason. One reagent, one mechanism, two names for the position — and if a molecule has both an allylic and a benzylic site, you have a selectivity problem rather than a reagent problem.

One more benzylic bond that breaks easily

Hydrogen over palladium normally reduces alkenes and alkynes and leaves single bonds alone. A benzylic C–O bond is the exception: H2 with Pd/C cleaves it cleanly — hydrogenolysis — giving toluene and the free alcohol. The reason is the one running through this whole section: the fragment left on the benzylic carbon is stabilized by the ring.

That makes a benzyl ether the one alcohol protecting group that comes off under neither acid nor base. Protect the alcohol with benzyl bromide and a base (a plain SN2 at a benzylic carbon), run chemistry an acetal would not survive, then hydrogenate the benzyl group off. The cost is that anything else in the molecule that H2/Pd would reduce comes off at the same time, so the protecting group and the substrate have to be compatible.

Oxidation: the whole side chain becomes a carboxyl

Hot KMnO4 in aqueous base, followed by an acidic workup, does something to an alkylbenzene that looks drastic and is completely predictable. Any alkyl side chain with at least one benzylic hydrogen is cut back to a single carbon and oxidized to –COOH.

  • Toluene → benzoic acid.
  • Propylbenzene → benzoic acid. The chain length does not matter.
  • tert-Butylbenzene → no reaction. There is no benzylic hydrogen to start on.
  • Write the workup. Under the basic reaction conditions what you have is the carboxylate; the carboxylic acid appears after H3O+. Hot Na2Cr2O7 in H2SO4 does the same job in one pot.

That last case is the one worth remembering, because it is the exception that shows the mechanism: the reaction begins by attacking the benzylic C–H, and a quaternary benzylic carbon has none.

Worked example — benzene to 4-nitrobenzoic acid

Step 1. Work backwards from the carboxyl. Electrophilic substitution cannot deliver a COOH group. The only route to one on a ring is to put an alkyl group there and oxidize it, so the last step is hot KMnO4 then H3O+.

Step 2. When does the nitro group go on? Not last: a carboxyl is a deactivating meta director, so nitrating the acid would work — and would indeed give the meta isomer, which is the wrong target. It has to go on while the ring still carries a methyl, which is an ortho/para director.

Step 3. When does the methyl go on? First. Friedel–Crafts alkylation fails on a ring carrying a nitro group, so the alkylation cannot follow the nitration.

The sequence: CH3Cl/AlCl3, then HNO3/H2SO4 (para to the methyl, separated from the ortho isomer), then hot KMnO4 and acidic workup. Three reactions, and only this order works — each step is blocked by the one that would otherwise precede it.

The ring itself survives. Permanganate will destroy an alkene without hesitation, and it leaves benzene alone — aromatic stabilization is worth enough to make the ring inert to conditions that oxidize almost anything else.

What carries forward

Benzylic is the position next to the ring, and everything about it follows from resonance into the ring. Cations, radicals and anions are all stabilized there; substitution runs by either mechanism; NBS brominates there selectively; and hot permanganate cuts any side chain with a benzylic hydrogen back to a carboxylic acid. Retrosynthetically, a benzoic acid should make you ask what alkylbenzene it was oxidized from — which is often how a group gets onto a ring that Friedel–Crafts could not deliver directly.
