Why substitution, not addition

Benzene's pi electrons can attack an electrophile exactly like an alkene's (Module 7 addition reactions) — but the resulting carbocation, called an arenium ion (or Wheland intermediate), has broken the ring's full conjugation at one carbon (now sp³), destroying the ~36 kcal/mol of aromatic stabilization energy (Aromaticity lesson). Rather than let a nucleophile trap that carbocation (which would give a non-aromatic addition product, permanently losing that stabilization), the ring instead loses a proton from the same carbon that the electrophile attacked, restoring full conjugation and regenerating the aromatic ring. Net result: a hydrogen has been replaced by the electrophile — substitution, not addition — because reforming the enormously stable aromatic ring is thermodynamically overwhelming.

The arenium ion is resonance-stabilized (but still not aromatic)

The positive charge in the arenium ion delocalizes over three ring carbons via resonance (ortho and para to the point of attack relative to the sp³ carbon) — real stabilization, but nowhere close to full aromatic stabilization, since the ring's continuous conjugation is still broken at the sp³ carbon. This partial stabilization is exactly why EAS reactions still require a genuinely reactive electrophile (usually generated with help from a Lewis acid catalyst) rather than proceeding as readily as, say, SN1 solvolysis.

Because deprotonation (loss of H⁺ to restore aromaticity) is thermodynamically so favorable, the arenium ion formation step — the electrophile actually attacking the ring — is essentially always the slow, rate-determining step of any EAS reaction.

The major EAS reactions and how each electrophile is generated

Halogenation (Br₂ or Cl₂ with a Lewis acid catalyst like FeBr₃ or AlCl₃): the Lewis acid polarizes the X–X bond, generating a strong enough electrophile (X⁺, effectively) to attack the ring. Nitration (HNO₃/H₂SO₄): sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, which then loses water to form the nitronium ion (NO₂⁺), a potent electrophile. Sulfonation (SO₃/H₂SO₄): SO₃ itself is directly electrophilic at sulfur, no further activation needed. Friedel-Crafts alkylation (RCl/AlCl₃): the Lewis acid helps ionize the alkyl halide to a carbocation (or a strongly polarized complex), which attacks the ring exactly like the carbocation electrophiles from Module 7. Friedel-Crafts acylation (RCOCl/AlCl₃): the Lewis acid similarly helps generate a resonance-stabilized acylium ion (RCO⁺), which attacks the ring to install a ketone directly onto it.

A key limitation: Friedel-Crafts alkylation carbocations can rearrange

Because Friedel-Crafts alkylation proceeds through a genuine carbocation intermediate, it inherits the exact same rearrangement risk from SN1/E1 chemistry (Module 6) — a less stable carbocation can shift (via hydride or alkyl shift) to a more stable one before ever attacking the ring, giving an unexpected substitution pattern. Friedel-Crafts acylation avoids this entirely: the acylium ion is already resonance-stabilized by the adjacent oxygen (similar to the oxocarbenium ion from the Acetals lesson) and has no driving force to rearrange, which is why acylation is often used deliberately as a workaround when a clean, unrearranged alkyl substituent pattern is needed (the resulting ketone can then be reduced back to a simple alkyl group in a separate step).
