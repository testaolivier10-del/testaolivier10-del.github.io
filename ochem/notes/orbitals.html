What an orbital is, and the four shapes

An orbital is a region of space where an electron is likely to be found; each one holds at most 2 electrons, and those two must have opposite spins (the Pauli exclusion principle — no two electrons in the same atom can be in the exact same state).

There are four orbital shapes you will hear about.

  • s orbitals are spherical. 1 per shell, starting at n=1.
  • p orbitals are dumbbell-shaped — two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, with a node (zero probability) at the center. 3 per shell, starting at n=2, pointing along the x, y, and z axes.
  • d orbitals are mostly four-lobed, cloverleaf-shaped. 5 per shell, starting at n=3.
  • f orbitals are more complex multi-lobed shapes. 7 per shell, starting at n=4.

Each added shape lets a shell hold more electrons: s alone = 2, s+p = 8, s+p+d = 18, s+p+d+f = 32.

This is why the periodic table has that stair-step shape

The periodic table's block shape directly reflects which orbital type was filled last for each element. The two leftmost columns (groups 1-2) are the s-block. The six columns on the right (groups 13-18) are the p-block — this is where essentially every element in organic chemistry lives: C, N, O, F, S, P, Cl, Br, I. The 10 columns in the middle (groups 3-12, the transition metals) are the d-block. The two rows pulled out at the bottom (lanthanides and actinides) are the f-block.

One payoff of knowing the blocks: sulfur, phosphorus, and the other period 3+ elements can hold more than 8 electrons around them (an "expanded octet," as in SF₆), while carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and fluorine never can. Why that is gets settled in the Lewis Structures and Hybridization lessons. You can look up any element's block with the periodic table tool (the ⚛ button) — its color-coded categories line up with these blocks.

Orbitals are the detailed picture of shells

The K shell from Atomic Structure is the single 1s orbital (2 electrons max, matching the K shell's max of 2). The L shell is the 2s orbital plus the three 2p orbitals: 1+3 = 4 orbitals, 8 electrons max — exactly the L shell's max of 8.

Why the fill order is what it is

Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first (the Aufbau principle). Within one shell, energy increases s < p < d < f. Why? An electron's energy depends on how strongly the nucleus pulls on it, and that depends on how much of the nucleus's pull is "screened out" by other electrons in the way — called shielding. An s orbital's shape lets its electron spend more time very close to the nucleus than a p or d electron of the same shell does (called penetration), so it feels less shielding, feels a stronger pull, and sits at lower energy.

This effect is strong enough that it eventually overtakes the shell number itself: by the time you reach period 4, the 4s orbital ends up lower energy than 3d, even though 4 > 3 — which is exactly why potassium and calcium fill 4s before any element starts filling 3d, and why the d-block starts at row 4 of the periodic table instead of row 3. When orbitals are exactly equal in energy (like the three 2p's, or the five 3d's), electrons spread out across them singly before any pairing up — Hund's rule, which minimizes electron-electron repulsion.

Carbon (6 electrons): 1s² 2s² 2p² — 2 electrons pair up in 1s, 2 pair up in 2s, and the last 2 go into separate 2p orbitals, unpaired.

The paradox: carbon's 1s² 2s² 2p² has only 2 unpaired electrons, which predicts just 2 bonds — but carbon almost always forms 4. Something has to happen before bonding to resolve this. That's the entire subject of the next lesson, Hybridization.
