Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions · Section 56 of 118

UV-Vis spectroscopy

Practice this — interactive lesson

Every other spectroscopy in this course reports on individual bonds or individual nuclei. UV-Vis reports on something more specific and more useful in this chapter: how far a conjugated π system extends. It is the experimental measurement of the thing the rest of this chapter has been reasoning about.

What the measurement is

Ultraviolet and visible light carry enough energy to promote an electron from an occupied molecular orbital to an empty one. In a molecule with a π system the cheapest such jump is from the HOMO to the LUMO — the two frontier orbitals from Conjugated systems. For a polyene both are π orbitals: the HOMO is the top filled bonding one and the LUMO is the bottom empty antibonding one, written π* and pronounced "pi star". So the transition is written π → π*, and what it measures is the size of the gap between those two rungs of the ladder.

A molecule absorbs the wavelength whose photon energy matches that gap. Since energy and wavelength are inversely related, the relationship you actually use is short and worth memorizing in this direction:

Bigger HOMO–LUMO gap → higher energy photon needed → shorter wavelength absorbed.
Smaller gap → lower energy → longer wavelength absorbed.

More conjugation, smaller gap, longer wavelength

The reason is the ladder drawn in the first section of this chapter. Adding a double bond to the conjugation adds two more p orbitals, which makes two more molecular orbitals — and the range they spread over grows far more slowly than their number does, so the rungs crowd closer together. The top filled one climbs, the bottom empty one drops, and the gap between them narrows with every double bond added. Ethene has one rung of each kind and the widest gap of all; β-carotene has eleven and the narrowest.

The numbers make the trend unmistakable, and they are worth knowing as a shape rather than as figures to recall exactly:

CompoundConjugated C=Cλmax (approx.)
Ethene1171 nm
Buta-1,3-diene2217 nm
Hexa-1,3,5-triene3258 nm
β-Carotene11~450 nm

The group responsible for the absorption is called a chromophore. An isolated C=C absorbs below about 200 nm, which is inconveniently far into the ultraviolet for ordinary instruments, so a simple alkene is effectively invisible. Conjugate two and the absorption moves into the accessible range — which is precisely why this technique is a conjugation detector rather than a general-purpose one.

Why conjugation eventually produces color

Keep extending the system and λmax keeps rising until it crosses about 400 nm and enters the visible region. At that point the compound is absorbing visible light, and what your eye sees is the light that was not absorbed — the complementary color.

β-Carotene, with eleven conjugated double bonds, absorbs blue light around 450 nm and therefore looks orange. That is the color of carrots, and the same reasoning covers the deep colors of dyes, indicators and the visual pigment retinal. Organic compounds are colorless unless they carry an extensively conjugated system; when you see a strongly colored organic compound, extended conjugation is the first thing to look for.

Not every band is a π → π* band

A carbonyl gives a second, quite different absorption, and knowing about it is what stops UV-Vis being misread. Oxygen's lone pair sits in a non-bonding orbital — an n orbital — which is higher in energy than the π bonding orbital, so promoting an electron out of it into the same π* costs less. Acetone therefore absorbs around 280 nm, far to the right of where its single C=O has any business being.

What gives it away is the strength. The lone pair points sideways, roughly perpendicular to the π* it has to reach, so the two orbitals barely overlap and the transition is nearly forbidden: ε for an n → π* band is in the range 10–100, against 10,000 and up for a π → π* band. So the reading is:

  • Long λmax with a large ε — an extended conjugated system.
  • Long λmax with a tiny ε — a lone pair, almost always a carbonyl.

Substituents shift λmax too, and by predictable amounts

Extra conjugation is the big lever, but it is not the only one. Anything that donates electron density into the π system raises the HOMO a little and moves the band to longer wavelength — alkyl groups weakly, heteroatom lone pairs (OR, NR2, SR) much more strongly. A group that does this without being a chromophore itself is called an auxochrome, and it is why a dye's color can be tuned by hanging an amino or a hydroxy group off the ring rather than by lengthening the chain.

The shifts are regular enough to be tabulated. The Woodward–Fieser rules start from a base value and add increments; for a diene the working version is short enough to carry:

ContributionIncrement
Base: acyclic or open (heteroannular) diene217 nm
Base: diene inside one ring (homoannular)253 nm
Each alkyl group or ring residue on the diene+5 nm
Each double bond extending the conjugation+30 nm
Each exocyclic double bond+5 nm

Two things are worth taking from the table even if you never use it. The first is what kind of number +30 for one more conjugated C=C is. Measured, the step from butadiene to hexatriene in the table above is 217 to 258 nm — +41 nm, not +30. The increment is not wrong; it is an empirical average, fitted to substituted dienes of the sort the rules were built for, and it is deliberately conservative. Use it to predict the shift between two related compounds, not to reproduce the parent hydrocarbons: a prediction of 247 nm for hexatriene is 11 nm short of the measurement, and that is the accuracy these rules claim. The second is that the +5 for a substituent is small, which is what makes UV-Vis a blunt instrument for anything except counting the length of the conjugation.

Beer–Lambert: how much is absorbed

Wavelength says what the molecule is; the amount of absorption says how much of it is there. The relationship is the Beer–Lambert law:

A = εcl

  • A is absorbance, a unitless number read off the instrument.
  • ε is the molar absorptivity, a constant for that compound at that wavelength — a measure of how strongly it absorbs. Conjugated systems have large ε, often 10,000 or more.
  • c is concentration, in mol L−1, and l is the path length through the sample in cm, conventionally 1 cm.
Because A has no units and c and l do, ε has to carry units that cancel them: L·mol−1·cm−1, often written M−1cm−1. That is worth remembering as a sentence rather than as a fact, because it is derived rather than memorized, and it is a standard exam question. "ε = 21,000" means 21,000 L·mol−1·cm−1.

Because absorbance is directly proportional to concentration, UV-Vis is a quantitative technique in a way NMR and IR usually are not in a teaching lab. It is the standard way to follow the concentration of a colored or conjugated species while a reaction runs.

Worked example — using Beer–Lambert on a number

A solution of a conjugated diene in a 1.00 cm cell gives an absorbance of 0.63 at its λmax, where ε = 21,000 L·mol−1·cm−1. What is the concentration?

Rearrange before substituting. A = εcl, so c = A / (εl). Doing the algebra first is worth the two seconds, because it makes the units check trivial.

Substitute. c = 0.63 / (21,000 × 1.00) = 3.0 × 10−5 mol L−1.

Check the units. L·mol−1·cm−1 × cm leaves L·mol−1, and dividing a unitless absorbance by that gives mol·L−1. If your answer comes out in anything else, you have the expression upside down.

Check the size. 30 micromolar — a few milligrams in a litre. That is the real point of a large ε: it makes the technique sensitive enough to follow a reaction at concentrations too low for NMR to see at all.

The trap: an absorbance much above about 1.5 is outside the linear range of most instruments, where the detector is receiving almost no light. If a reading comes back at 2.8, dilute the sample and measure again rather than trusting the arithmetic.

Worked example — reading a spectrum

Two isomeric dienes, C₆H₁₀. One absorbs at 227 nm, the other below 200 nm. Which is conjugated?

The one at 227 nm. A conjugated diene has the smaller HOMO–LUMO gap and therefore absorbs at the longer wavelength. The isolated diene behaves as two separate alkenes, each absorbing below 200 nm where a simple C=C does.

What the spectrum does not tell you is where the double bonds are, only that they are or are not in conjugation. Pair it with NMR to place them — UV-Vis answers one question, sharply, and says little about anything else.

What carries forward

UV-Vis sits in this chapter rather than with IR and NMR on purpose: it is the direct measurement of conjugation, and it makes the abstract claim of the first section — that a longer π system is a lower-energy, smaller-gap system — into something you can read off an instrument. When you reach the Spectroscopy chapter, the habit to carry in is that each technique answers a different question: IR names functional groups, mass spectrometry gives mass and fragments, NMR builds the carbon skeleton, and UV-Vis reports conjugation.
