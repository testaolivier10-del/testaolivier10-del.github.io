Carbonyl Chemistry · Section 67 of 121

Imines and enamines

Practice this — interactive lesson

Acetone and methylamine, CH3NH2, give (CH3)2C=N–CH3; acetone and dimethylamine, (CH3)2NH, give CH2=C(CH3)–N(CH3)2. Both release a molecule of water. Nucleophilic addition showed these two products side by side. This section runs the mechanism that makes them.

The first product is an imine: a compound with a carbon–nitrogen double bond, C=N. The second is an enamine: an amine nitrogen attached to one carbon of a C=C (“ene” for the alkene, “amine” for the nitrogen).

Both come from the same three steps. Only the last one differs, and one count decides it: how many hydrogens the nitrogen started with.

Step 1: the amine adds and a carbinolamine forms

An amine is a nucleophile, and the carbonyl carbon is an electrophile. So the nitrogen lone pair attacks the carbonyl carbon. As the new C–N bond forms, the C=O pi electrons move onto oxygen.

The result is a tetrahedral intermediate with a negative oxygen and a positive nitrogen on the same carbon. A molecule that carries both charges at once like this is called a zwitterion.

A proton then moves from the N+ to the O−, usually with a solvent molecule passing it along. That cancels both charges. The neutral product has an OH and an amine nitrogen on the same carbon, and it is called a carbinolamine.

Step 2: acid turns the OH into water, and water leaves

The carbinolamine now has to lose its OH. Hydroxide, HO−, is a poor leaving group. So an acid first puts a proton on the oxygen. The OH becomes OH2+, and water is a good leaving group.

Next the nitrogen lone pair pushes in toward carbon, forming a C=N double bond, while the C–O bond breaks and water leaves. The product is an iminium ion: a C=N double bond with a positive charge on nitrogen.

These are the same two moves as the loss of water in Acetals & hemiacetals: protonate the OH, then push a lone pair in as water leaves. There an oxygen lone pair pushes in. Here a nitrogen lone pair does, and nitrogen gives up its lone pair more readily than oxygen does. That is why imines form under much milder acid than acetals need.

Step 3: the last proton decides the product

The iminium ion carries a positive charge. To become a neutral product it has to lose a proton, and where that proton can come from decides the product.

Methylamine is a primary amine: its nitrogen carries one carbon and two hydrogens. It spent one hydrogen in Step 1, so its iminium ion still has an N–H. A water molecule takes that proton, the N–H electrons become a lone pair on nitrogen, and the neutral imine (CH3)2C=N–CH3 results.

Dimethylamine is a secondary amine: two carbons and one hydrogen on nitrogen. It spent that hydrogen in Step 1, so its iminium ion has no N–H at all. The nearest removable proton sits on the α carbon, the carbon right next to the C=N carbon (the same position that is α to a C=O in Aldehydes & ketones).

When water takes that α proton, three pairs of electrons shift at once. The C–H electrons form a new C=C bond. The C=N pi electrons move onto nitrogen as a lone pair. The double bond has moved from C=N to C=C, and the product is the enamine CH2=C(CH3)–N(CH3)2.

A tertiary amine, such as trimethylamine, (CH3)3N, has no N–H at all. It can still add to a carbonyl. But its nitrogen then has four bonds, a positive charge and no lone pair, so it has nothing to push in when water tries to leave. Forming C=N+ would need a nitrogen with five bonds. That is impossible rather than slow, so the addition simply reverses. This is why tertiary amines appear in these reactions as bases, not as reagents.
An enamine needs a hydrogen on the α carbon, and the nitrogen count says nothing about that. Benzophenone, Ph2C=O, has two benzene rings (Ph) on its carbonyl carbon and so no α hydrogen at all. With a secondary amine it can go as far as the iminium ion, but no further.

Why the pH has to be about 4.5

This reaction is the standard example of a rate that is fastest in the middle of the pH range. Acid is required, because the OH of the carbinolamine is a poor leaving group until it is protonated. But too much acid protonates the amine too. Methylamine becomes CH3NH3+, an ammonium ion, which has no lone pair and so cannot attack.

  • Too basic: the OH is rarely protonated, so water leaves only slowly and the carbinolamine builds up.
  • Too acidic: nearly all of the amine is ammonium, so the first step stalls.
  • About pH 4–5: enough acid to protonate the OH, not enough to switch off the amine.

It is worth being precise about which step the acid speeds up. Near the optimum the amine’s addition is fast, and the slow step is the loss of water from the carbinolamine. That is exactly the step a proton helps. Below about pH 3 the balance flips. Most of the amine is now ammonium, so the addition becomes the slow step instead, and that drags the rate back down.

Driving it forward, and back

Every step is reversible, and water is one of the products. So chemists remove water as it forms, which pulls the equilibrium toward the imine or enamine. A Dean–Stark trap does it by boiling the water off with the solvent and catching it in a side arm. Molecular sieves do it by soaking the water up into tiny pores.

Run the same steps backward and you get the reverse reaction. Add water and acid to an imine or an enamine, and it hydrolyzes: it splits back into the carbonyl compound and the amine.

The enamine is a nucleophile at carbon

This is what enamines are for. Push the nitrogen lone pair of CH2=C(CH3)–N(CH3)2 toward the neighboring carbon, and the C=C pi electrons have to move to the end CH2. That gives a second resonance structure with C=N+ and a negative end carbon.

That end carbon is the one that was α to the original C=O. The resonance structure says it carries a real share of the nitrogen’s electron pair, so it is nucleophilic. An enamine is therefore a nucleophilic carbon, reached with nothing stronger than an amine and mild acid.

The other way to make an α carbon nucleophilic is the enolate. A strong base pulls an α hydrogen off a ketone, and the anion left behind shares its negative charge between that carbon and the oxygen. Enolates are the subject of Enolate Chemistry, several chapters on. The table below is a preview.

The strong base usually named for this is LDA, lithium diisopropylamide, (i-Pr)2N− Li+. It is strong enough to turn nearly all of a ketone into its enolate, which is then called a lithium enolate.

The Stork enamine synthesis

Take cyclohexanone and the cyclic secondary amine pyrrolidine. With mild acid and water removal they give the pyrrolidine enamine of cyclohexanone. Add allyl bromide, CH2=CH–CH2Br. The enamine’s nucleophilic carbon, C2 of the ring, attacks the CH2 that carries the bromine, and bromide leaves in an SN2 step. Finally, aqueous acid hydrolyzes the product. What comes out is 2-allylcyclohexanone and pyrrolidine.

That three-stage sequence is the Stork enamine synthesis: form the enamine, alkylate or acylate the α carbon, then hydrolyze. Net result, an α-substituted ketone.

The sequence stops cleanly after one alkylation, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of reactivity. Alkylation turns the enamine into an iminium salt. That cation has no C=C, so it has no nucleophilic carbon. No strong base is present to pull it back to an enamine. A little proton exchange between the salt and enamine that has not yet reacted can give some dialkylated product, but it is a minor side reaction at most, not a competing pathway.

A lithium enolate has no such stop. The product of the first alkylation is a ketone that still has α hydrogens. Enolate that has not reacted yet is a strong enough base to take one of them. That makes a new enolate from the product, which alkylates a second time. This over-alkylation gives a mixture, and it is one of the problems Enolate Chemistry takes up.

The price of a milder nucleophile is a shorter list of electrophiles. Stork alkylation works well with reactive halides: methyl, allylic, benzylic, and α-halo esters and ketones. An ordinary primary halide such as 1-bromobutane reacts too slowly to be useful, where a lithium enolate would take it happily. The nitrogen lone pair can also attack the halide itself (N-alkylation), which competes with the carbon attack; with the reactive halides above, the carbon product wins. So the two routes are not interchangeable. The enolate alkylates with almost anything and over-reacts; the enamine is clean but fussy about the halide. Acylation with an acid chloride always works, because an acid chloride is far more electrophilic than any alkyl halide.

Enamines in conjugate addition

Take but-3-en-2-one, CH2=CH–CO–CH3, a ketone whose C=O is conjugated with a C=C. Number the four atoms of the conjugated system from the oxygen: O is 1, the carbonyl carbon is 2, and the far CH2 is 4. Aldehydes & ketones showed that a resonance structure puts positive charge on carbon 4 as well as on carbon 2.

So a nucleophile has two targets. Attack at carbon 2, the C=O carbon itself, is 1,2-addition. Attack at carbon 4, the far end of the C=C, is 1,4-addition, also called conjugate addition. When the nucleophile is an enolate or an enamine, 1,4-addition is called a Michael addition.

Enamines are reliable Michael donors: they add 1,4 cleanly. The pyrrolidine enamine of cyclohexanone and but-3-en-2-one give, after hydrolysis, a new C–C bond from C2 of the ring to carbon 4 of the enone. Enolates vary. Stabilized ones, such as the enolate of a 1,3-dicarbonyl compound, also add 1,4 routinely. A very reactive enolate is more prone to attack the C=O carbon directly. Enolate Chemistry sorts out which does which.

Imines elsewhere

Reductive amination. Reduce an imine and the C=N becomes a C–N single bond, giving an amine. The usual reagent is NaBH3CN, a milder cousin of the NaBH4 in Nucleophilic addition. It reduces the protonated imine (the iminium ion) faster than it reduces the starting C=O. This is the most reliable way to make a secondary or tertiary amine. It avoids the over-alkylation that SN2 on an amine suffers, where each new amine is itself a nucleophile and reacts again. Amines builds on it.

Oximes and hydrazones. Hydroxylamine, H2N–OH, and hydrazine, H2N–NH2, are primary amines of a sort, so they give imines too. The imine from hydroxylamine is an oxime, C=N–OH. The one from a hydrazine is a hydrazone, C=N–NH2. Many are crystalline, and their melting points were once how a carbonyl compound got identified.

Wolff–Kishner reduction. Heat a hydrazone with a strong base, such as KOH in a high-boiling solvent, and the two nitrogens leave as N2 gas. A CH2 is left where the C=O was. This is the standard way to delete a carbonyl group entirely.

Clemmensen reduction. Zinc amalgam, Zn(Hg), with HCl does the same C=O to CH2 change in acid. Choose Wolff–Kishner when the molecule cannot survive acid and Clemmensen when it cannot survive base. Both matter for the same reason: a Friedel–Crafts acylation, which puts a C=O group onto a benzene ring (Aromatic Chemistry, later), followed by one of them is how an unrearranged alkyl group gets onto a ring.

What carries forward

Count the N–H hydrogens: one left gives an imine, none left gives an enamine. Run it near pH 4.5 and remove the water. Then remember what the enamine is for: a neutral nucleophilic carbon that alkylates once and hydrolyzes back, the gentler of the two ways to put a new group on an α carbon.
