Polymers · Section 119 of 121

Condensation polymers

Practice this — interactive lesson

Start with one molecule of terephthalic acid and one of ethylene glycol. The acid has a COOH group at each end, and the glycol has an OH group at each end. An esterification joins one COOH to one OH and releases a molecule of water. The product still has a free COOH at one end and a free OH at the other, so it can react again at both ends. Repeat that thousands of times and you have PET, the plastic of drinks bottles.

PET is a condensation polymer: a chain built from monomers that each carry two reactive groups, with a small molecule released at every join. The word condensation names that release. Each join is an acyl substitution, the reaction that makes esters and amides (Nucleophilic acyl substitution). Monomers, repeat units and the step-growth way these chains get long are set out in What a polymer is.

Polyesters

A diacid, with two COOH groups, plus a diol, with two OH groups, gives a polyester: a chain whose units are joined by ester groups. The one to know is PET, short for polyethylene terephthalate. The figure builds it from its two monomers.

PET is the plastic of drinks bottles and the fiber of polyester fabric. It is also one of the most widely recycled plastics. Most of that recycling is mechanical: used bottles are collected, washed, shredded and melted into new products. The ester bonds add a second route that an all-carbon chain such as polyethylene lacks. Under forcing conditions, water or an alcohol can cut every ester bond and take the chain back to its monomers, which can then make new PET.

Polyamides

Swap the diol for a diamine, a molecule with two NH2 groups, and each join becomes an amide. The chain is then a polyamide. Nylon 6,6 is made this way from hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid, and each of those monomers has six carbons.

Mixed cold, the two monomers only form a salt, as any amine and carboxylic acid do. Heating that salt strongly drives off water and turns each ammonium carboxylate pair into an amide bond.

The two sixes give the name. In nylon X,Y the first number counts the carbons in the diamine and the second counts the carbons in the diacid, carboxyl carbons included. Nylon 6,6 hides the order, but nylon 6,10 shows it: hexamethylenediamine, with six carbons, joined to sebacic acid, the ten-carbon diacid.

Worked example — draw the repeat unit of nylon 6,10 and check it

Step 1 — read the name. The first number belongs to the diamine, so 6 means hexamethylenediamine, H2N(CH2)6NH2. The second belongs to the diacid, so 10 means sebacic acid, HOOC(CH2)8COOH. The ten carbons include the two carboxyl carbons, so the run of CH2 between them is only eight. Missing that is the usual slip.

Step 2 — make one amide. An amine nitrogen bonds to an acid carbonyl carbon, and the OH leaves with an H from the nitrogen as water. That gives –NH(CH2)6NH–CO(CH2)8CO–.

Step 3 — bracket it. –[NH(CH2)6NH–CO(CH2)8CO]–n. The repeat unit has sixteen carbons and two amide bonds.

Step 4 — check by mass. The diamine, C6H16N2, is 116, and the diacid, C10H18O4, is 202, which makes 318 together. The repeat unit, C16H30N2O2, is 282. The difference of 36 is two waters, one per amide, so it balances.

The trap. Writing nylon 6,10 from decanediamine and adipic acid puts the same two numbers in the wrong slots, and that is a different polymer. The diamine always comes first.

A polyamide chain is a protein backbone built from one repeating unit instead of twenty different amino acids. It has the same amide bond, kept flat by the same resonance. The N–H groups of one chain hydrogen-bond to the C=O groups of the next, just as the strands of a β-sheet do (Peptides and proteins). Nylon is strong for the same reason a β-sheet is.

Esters and amides under attack

Hydrolysis treats the two linkages differently. An amide is the least reactive acyl derivative, because the nitrogen lone pair donates into the C=O more strongly than an ester oxygen’s lone pair does (Esters and amides). So hot aqueous base cuts ester bonds much faster than amide bonds, and polyester fabric weakens in hot alkali sooner than nylon does.

That does not make nylon the tougher polymer overall. The two fail under different conditions. Strong acid, chlorine bleach and sunlight all damage nylon. PET stands up to acid and bleach in everyday use and gives way to hot base.

Nylon 6, and why one number is not two

Nylon 6 has a single number because it comes from a single monomer, 6-aminohexanoic acid, H2N(CH2)5COOH. That is a six-carbon chain with an amine at one end and a carboxylic acid at the other. One molecule carries both partners, so it can join to copies of itself, losing a water each time. Lactic acid does the same with its OH and COOH groups and gives the polyester PLA, poly(lactic acid).

Industrial nylon 6 starts from caprolactam instead. A lactam is a cyclic amide, and caprolactam is the seven-membered one: 6-aminohexanoic acid with its amine and acid ends joined to each other in a ring. The ring opens onto the amine at the end of a growing chain, which gets one unit longer, and no atoms leave.

That makes caprolactam the case where the two questions in What a polymer is come apart. By atom count nothing is lost, yet the product is a polyamide. How the chains grow depends on the conditions. With a strong base to start it, the anionic route, one active chain end adds ring after ring, which is chain-growth. The main industrial route starts with a little water, which opens some rings to 6-aminohexanoic acid. After that, rings add to chain ends and chain ends also join each other, so that route has step-growth character as well. Either way the finished chain is built from whole opened rings.

Nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 have the same empirical formula, the simplest whole-number ratio of their atoms. Nylon 6 repeats –[NH(CH2)5CO]–, which is C6H11NO. Nylon 6,6 repeats –[NH(CH2)6NH–CO(CH2)4CO]–, which is C12H22N2O2, exactly twice as much. The two polymers differ in the order of their atoms, not in the proportions.

That order gives a nylon 6 chain a direction. Every unit joined head to tail, so every amide in the chain reads the same way along it: N–H end first, then C=O. In nylon 6,6 the amides alternate, one reading NH–CO and the next CO–NH, so the chain reads the same from either end.

Direction decides how neighboring chains hydrogen-bond. Two nylon 6 chains can pair every facing N–H with a C=O only when they run in opposite directions. Two nylon 6,6 chains pair fully whichever way round they lie. The figure shows the best fit in each case.

So nylon 6,6 chains line up more easily, and that is one reason usually given for its higher melting point: about 265 °C, against about 220 °C for nylon 6.

Kevlar, and what aromatic rings buy

Kevlar is also a polyamide. Its diacid is terephthalic acid, the same one PET uses, and its diamine is para-phenylenediamine, a benzene ring with NH2 groups on opposite corners. Both monomers are aromatic and para-substituted.

The factory does not use the free acid. An aromatic amine is a weak nucleophile, because its lone pair is spread into the ring (Amine structure and basicity), and a carboxylic acid is a weak electrophile. Together they cannot reach the near-complete conversion a strong fiber needs. So the acid goes in as its acid chloride, terephthaloyl chloride, and each join releases HCl instead of water.

The shapes of the two monomers decide the rest, in three steps:

  • The rings are flat and rigid, so the chain cannot coil.
  • Para substitution makes the chain straight, so neighboring chains lie against each other along their full length.
  • Every amide makes a hydrogen bond to the next chain, and straight chains line those bonds up into sheets.

Compare nylon 6,6. The amide groups and the hydrogen bonds are the same, and only what sits between the amides differs. Nylon has a flexible run of CH2 groups that can twist out of line, so its chains lie straight only in patches. Kevlar has a para ring that cannot twist the chain out of line, so each chain stays straight along its whole length and its hydrogen bonds add up the whole way.

The result is a fiber stronger than steel for its weight. Nothing new is happening: it is the hydrogen bonding of a β-sheet, lined up perfectly.

Polycarbonates

Take bisphenol A, two phenol rings joined through one carbon that also carries two methyl groups. It is a diol, with both OH groups on rings. Its partner is phosgene, Cl2C=O, the acid chloride of carbonic acid on both sides. Each join is an acyl substitution you already know: a phenoxide oxygen attacks the carbonyl carbon and chloride leaves, so each join releases HCl.

The product is a polycarbonate. Its linkage, O–C(=O)–O, is a carbonate: one carbonyl carbon with an oxygen on each side, which makes it an ester of carbonic acid at both ends.

Newer plants avoid phosgene. They heat bisphenol A with diphenyl carbonate, and at each join a bisphenol oxygen replaces a phenol oxygen on the carbonate, releasing phenol. This ester exchange is a transesterification. The linkage is the same; only the leaving group differs.

The carbonate is also the weak point. Hydroxide or an amine attacks its carbonyl carbon, so polycarbonate slowly fails in hot alkali and can be broken down on purpose.

Polycarbonate is stiff because every repeat unit carries two rigid rings. It is also glass-clear. The central carbon is sp3, so the two rings leave it at an angle and its two methyl groups stick out. The chains therefore cannot pack into the ordered, crystal-like regions that make many plastics cloudy. A polymer whose chains stay disordered like this is called amorphous.

It stays rigid at room temperature because that is far below its glass transition, near 150 °C, the temperature at which its tangled chains start to slide. Structure and properties takes up packing and both transition temperatures. Stiff and clear together is exactly what safety glasses and bullet-resistant windows need.

Polyurethanes: nothing lost

Mix a diol with a diisocyanate, a molecule with two isocyanate groups. An isocyanate is R–N=C=O, one carbon double-bonded to both a nitrogen and an oxygen (Amine synthesis meets it in the Hofmann rearrangement). Each OH adds across an N=C=O group, and the chain grows. The link that forms, R–NH–C(=O)–O–R′, is a carbamate. Its older name, urethane, gives the polymer its name: polyurethane.

The isocyanate carbon sits between two electronegative atoms, so it is strongly electrophilic. The alcohol oxygen attacks it, and the electrons of the C=N π bond move onto nitrogen. The nitrogen, now negative, takes the proton from the positive oxygen. This is nucleophilic addition to a C=X double bond, just like addition to a ketone, and nothing has to leave.

So nothing is released, and the repeat unit weighs exactly what one diol and one diisocyanate weigh together. A polyurethane still grows the step-growth way, with any two pieces able to join, but it is not a condensation. That is why chemists call the whole class step-growth polymers rather than condensation polymers.

Read the last column before answering any question about this class. Two of the six lose nothing and two lose HCl rather than water, so “condensation loses water” describes only the first two rows. The small molecule depends on the monomers, not on the linkage. PET made from dimethyl terephthalate, the methyl diester of the acid, releases methanol instead of water. So a drawn chain cannot tell you what was lost; for that you have to go back to the monomers.

Why the kinetics feel different

Suppose 90% of the COOH groups in a polyester melt have reacted. That sounds nearly done, yet the average chain is only ten units long, and the product is an oil. The reason is the one from What a polymer is: any two pieces can join, so the mixture passes through a long stage of dimers and trimers before long chains exist.

Let p be the fraction of the functional groups that have reacted. The degree of polymerization, the average number of monomer units per chain, is then 1/(1−p). At 90% conversion that is 10. At 99% it is 100. At 99.9% it is 1,000.

Three requirements follow, and none of them is fussiness:

  • Exact stoichiometry. An excess of one monomer leaves every chain end carrying the same group, and a group cannot react with its own kind.
  • High purity. A monofunctional impurity, a molecule with only one reactive group, caps a chain end for good.
  • Continuous removal of the by-product. Every join is an equilibrium, so the water (or HCl, or methanol) has to be taken away to drive the reaction the whole way.
Worked example — what a 2% weighing error costs

The setup. The formula 1/(1−p) assumes the two kinds of group are present in exactly equal numbers. When they are not, let r be the ratio of the minority group to the majority one. The degree of polymerization becomes (1+r)/(1+r−2rp).

Step 1 — take the best case. Even at full conversion, p = 1, the expression simplifies to (1+r)/(1−r).

Step 2 — put in the error. A 2% excess of the diol makes r = 0.98. Then (1+r)/(1−r) = 1.98/0.02 = 99.

The point. A 2% weighing error caps the chains near a hundred units however long the reaction runs. That is the arithmetic behind the words “exact stoichiometry”.

What carries forward

A diacid with a diol gives a polyester, and a diacid with a diamine gives a polyamide; the small molecule lost depends on the monomers. Amides resist hydrolysis better than esters, so polyester gives way first in hot base, but acid, chlorine and sunlight attack nylon, so the difference lies in which conditions each survives. Rigid para-linked aromatic monomers give the straight, fully hydrogen-bonded chains of Kevlar. And 1/(1−p) explains why these reactions are pushed to a completeness that would be pointless for making a single ester.
