Polymers · Section 118 of 120

Condensation polymers

Practice this — interactive lesson

A condensation polymer is built by acyl substitution — esterification or amide formation — run repeatedly on monomers that each carry two reactive groups. A small molecule is expelled at every join, which is what the word condensation means and what distinguishes these from addition polymers — with one exception at the end of this section that is important enough to have changed what the class is called.

Polyesters

A diacid plus a diol gives a polyester, losing water at each ester bond. The one to know is PET — polyethylene terephthalate — from terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol. It is the plastic of drinks bottles and of polyester fabric, and it is the most recycled plastic there is, partly because the ester bonds can be hydrolyzed back to the monomers.

The ester group is what makes that possible and is also its weakness: a polyester is attacked by strong acid or base, which is why it can be depolymerized deliberately and why it degrades in the wrong conditions.

Polyamides

A diacid plus a diamine gives a polyamide, losing water at each amide bond. Nylon 6,6 comes from hexamethylenediamine (six carbons) and adipic acid (six carbons), which is where the name comes from. Read the numbers in that order: in nylon X,Y the first number counts the carbons in the diamine and the second counts the carbons in the diacid. Nylon 6,6 hides the distinction, but nylon 6,10 does not — that is hexamethylenediamine with the ten-carbon sebacic acid, not the other way around.

A polyamide is a protein backbone made of one repeating unit. The same amide bond, the same planarity from resonance, the same hydrogen bonding between N–H and C=O on neighboring chains. Nylon is strong for exactly the reason a beta sheet is.

And because amides are the least reactive acyl derivative, a polyamide is far more resistant to hydrolysis than a polyester — which is why polyester fabric weakens in hot alkali faster than nylon does. Be careful not to turn that into a blanket ranking: the two fail under different conditions. Nylon is the one attacked by acid, by chlorine and by sunlight, while PET shrugs off acid and bleach and gives way to base.

Nylon 6, and why one number is not two

A single number means a single monomer carrying both groups. Nylon 6 is the polyamide of 6-aminohexanoic acid, H2N(CH2)5CO2H: six carbons, one amine, one acid, so it condenses with itself and loses water, exactly as lactic acid does to give PLA. Industrially it is made a different way, from caprolactam, the seven-membered cyclic amide of that same amino acid. The ring opens onto the chain end and adds there, and because no atoms leave, that route is a ring-opening chain-growth polymerization even though what it builds is a polyamide. (The clean chain-growth case is the anionic ring-opening; the water-initiated industrial route opens the ring to 6-aminohexanoic acid first and then has step-growth character as well. Either way no atoms leave, which is the point here.)

So nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 are different polymers with the same empirical formula. Nylon 6 repeats –[NH(CH2)5CO]–, C6H11NO; nylon 6,6 repeats –[NH(CH2)6NH–CO(CH2)4CO]–, which is exactly twice that. What differs is the pattern, not the composition. In nylon 6 every amide points the same way along the chain, because every monomer was added head to tail. In nylon 6,6 the diamine and the diacid alternate, so the amides run in opposing pairs and two neighboring chains can register with each other whichever way round they lie — which is why it melts higher, near 265 °C against about 220 °C. One number: one monomer, both groups on it. Two numbers: two monomers, and the diamine is named first.
Worked example — draw the repeat unit of nylon 6,10 and check it

Step 1 — read the name. The first number is the diamine's carbons, so 6: hexamethylenediamine, H2N(CH2)6NH2. The second is the diacid's, so 10: sebacic acid, HO2C(CH2)8CO2H. Ten carbons including the two carboxyl carbons — that is the usual slip, because the run of CH2 between them is only eight.

Step 2 — make one amide. The amine nitrogen attacks the acid carbonyl and OH leaves as water: –NH(CH2)6NH–CO(CH2)8CO–.

Step 3 — bracket it. –[NH(CH2)6NH–CO(CH2)8CO]–n. Sixteen carbons in the repeat unit and two amide bonds.

Step 4 — check by atoms. C6H16N2 is 116 and C10H18O4 is 202, which is 318 together. The repeat unit C16H30N2O2 is 282. The difference, 36, is two waters — one per amide. It balances.

The trap. Writing nylon 6,10 from decanediamine and adipic acid. That is the same two numbers in the wrong slots, and it is a different polymer. The diamine is always first.

Kevlar, and what aromatic rings buy

Kevlar is a polyamide from terephthalic acid and para-phenylenediamine — both aromatic, both para-substituted. In practice the acid is activated before it is used: the industrial monomers are terephthaloyl chloride and the diamine, expelling HCl rather than water, because an aryl acid and an aryl amine are not nucleophilic and electrophilic enough between them to reach the conversion a fiber needs. Three things follow from the shapes, and they compound:

  • The rings are flat and rigid, so the chain cannot coil.
  • Para substitution makes the chain straight, so chains lie against each other along their full length.
  • Every amide contributes a hydrogen bond between neighboring chains, and a straight chain lines those up in sheets.

The result is a material stronger than steel by weight. Nothing exotic is happening — it is amide hydrogen bonding, the same interaction that holds a beta sheet together, multiplied by perfect alignment.

Polycarbonates and polyurethanes

A polycarbonate has a carbonate linkage, O–CO–O: one carbonyl carbon carrying two oxygens, so it is an ester of carbonic acid on both sides. The one to know is made from bisphenol A (two phenol rings joined through a C(CH3)2, so a diol) and phosgene, Cl2C=O, which is the diacid chloride of carbonic acid. Each join is acyl substitution of the kind you already know — a phenoxide attacks the carbonyl, chloride leaves — so the small molecule expelled here is HCl and not water. (The modern route replaces phosgene with diphenyl carbonate and transesterifies, expelling phenol instead: same linkage, different leaving group.)

Two rigid aromatic rings in every repeat unit are why polycarbonate is stiff, and the awkward bent geometry at the C(CH3)2 joint is why it cannot crystallize — so it is amorphous, and therefore glass-clear. Rigid but amorphous is exactly the combination safety glazing needs, which is why it makes safety glasses and bulletproof windows.

A polyurethane comes from a diol plus a diisocyanate, and the carbamate link it forms is the one genuine exception in this section: nothing is lost. The alcohol simply adds across the N=C=O group. That is ordinary nucleophilic addition to a carbonyl, with the twist that the leaving group is already attached: the isocyanate carbon sits between two electronegative atoms and is strongly electrophilic, the alcohol oxygen attacks it, and the negative charge lands on the nitrogen, which then takes the proton. The product R–O–CO–NH–R′ is a carbamate — the older name for it is a urethane, which is where the polymer's name comes from — and every atom of both monomers is still in it. So a polyurethane is a step-growth polymer that is technically not a condensation, which is why the broader name for this class is step-growth rather than condensation.

Read the last column before answering anything about condensation polymers. Two of these six lose nothing at all and two lose HCl rather than water, so "condensation loses water" is a description of the first two rows and not of the class. What all six share is the linkage in the backbone, which is why the honest name for the family is step-growth.

Why the kinetics feel different

In an addition polymerization, a growing chain is either active or dead, and active chains reach full length almost immediately. In a step-growth, any two pieces can join, so the mixture goes through a long stage of dimers and trimers before anything long exists.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Degree of polymerization is 1/(1−p), where p is the fraction of groups reacted. At 90% conversion that is 10 — an oil. At 99% it is 100. At 99.9% it is 1,000. So a step-growth polymerization needs exact stoichiometry, high purity, and the by-product removed continuously, because the last tenth of a percent is where the material appears. That formula assumes the two kinds of group are present in exactly equal numbers. When they are not, write r for the ratio of the minority group to the majority one, and the degree of polymerization becomes (1+r)/(1+r−2rp), which at full conversion simplifies to (1+r)/(1−r). A 2% excess of one monomer is r = 0.98, and 1.98/0.02 = 99 — so a 2% weighing error caps you near a hundred units however long you run the reaction. That is the arithmetic behind the words "exact stoichiometry".

What carries forward

Diacid plus diol gives a polyester; diacid plus diamine gives a polyamide; both lose water. Amides resist hydrolysis better than esters, so polyester gives way in hot base where nylon does not — but nylon is the one that acid, chlorine and sunlight attack, so it is a difference in which conditions, not a ranking. Aromatic para-substituted monomers give rigid, perfectly aligned chains and therefore Kevlar. And the 1/(1−p) relationship explains why these reactions are run to a completeness that would be pointless anywhere else.
