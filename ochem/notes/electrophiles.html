What it is

An electrophile ("electron-loving," but really electron-poor) carries a full or partial positive charge, or has an empty orbital, and seeks electrons from a nucleophile. It's the flip side of a nucleophile — one attacks, the other gets attacked.

Worked example: a carbonyl

In a ketone (C=O), oxygen's pull leaves the carbonyl carbon partially positive (δ+) — that's the electrophilic atom, not the oxygen. The polarization comes from the electronegativity difference in the C=O bond, not any formal charge (a neutral carbonyl carbon's formal charge is 0).

More examples

In CH₃–Br, the carbon is electrophilic (bromine pulls electron density away from it), not bromine itself, even though bromine is the one that eventually leaves. BF₃ has an empty p-orbital on boron, making it a strong electrophile (a place for electrons to go) — this is the same idea behind Lewis acids, which is simply the acid/base word for an electron-pair acceptor (Module 3).

Once you've found the nucleophile and electrophile in a reaction, you already know where the new bond forms — the nucleophile's electrons attack the electrophile's electron-poor atom.

Three more electrophile types

Carbocations (Module 6): a full positive charge and empty p orbital — among the strongest electrophiles. H⁺: the simplest electrophile, no electrons at all, grabs the nearest lone pair (acid-base chemistry, Module 3). Induced-dipole electrophiles: even nonpolar Br₂ becomes electrophilic as it approaches an alkene's π bond, which repels Br₂'s electrons and induces a temporary δ+ on the near bromine (Module 7, halogenation).
