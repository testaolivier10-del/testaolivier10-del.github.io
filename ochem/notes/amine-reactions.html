Amines · Section 95 of 121

Amine reactions

Practice this — interactive lesson

An amine's lone pair makes it a good nucleophile as well as a good base, and that combination is both its usefulness and its problem. The reactions in this section are largely about controlling a nucleophile that does not know when to stop.

SN2 alkylation: a messy way to build amines

A free amine attacks an alkyl halide directly in an SN2 reaction, forming a new C–N bond. That sounds like a clean route to a substituted amine, and it is not.

The problem is that the product is a more substituted amine, which is at least as nucleophilic as the starting material. It reacts with a second equivalent of alkyl halide, and a third, and ends as a quaternary ammonium salt. Direct alkylation of ammonia or a primary amine therefore gives a statistical mixture of mono-, di-, tri- and tetra-alkylated products that is difficult to separate and wasteful of material.

This is a genuine practical limitation, not a theoretical caveat. Two strategies work around it. Exhaustive alkylation — deliberately using excess alkyl halide to push everything to the quaternary salt — is fine when the salt is what you want. And the Gabriel synthesis uses phthalimide, whose single N–H is acidic enough to deprotonate and whose nitrogen after one alkylation is an amide and therefore unreactive; hydrolysis then releases a clean primary amine. The trick is to make the first product non-nucleophilic. The same runaway, used on purpose, is also how an amine is set up for elimination — see Hofmann elimination, later in this chapter.

Acylation: the clean, self-terminating alternative

React an amine with an acid chloride or anhydride and you get an amide, by the nucleophilic acyl substitution of Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives. The crucial point is what happens to the nitrogen: its lone pair is now delocalized into the new carbonyl, so it is no longer nucleophilic and no longer basic.

The reaction therefore self-terminates. One acylation, and the nitrogen is switched off. No mixture, no over-reaction. This is the same resonance-quenches-reactivity logic from the previous section, now put to practical use.

One practical detail: acylation releases HCl, which would protonate the remaining amine and stop the reaction at 50% conversion. So a hindered, poorly nucleophilic base — triethylamine, or Hünig's base, iPr₂NEt — is added to mop it up, or a second equivalent of the amine itself is sacrificed. Pyridine is also used, but for a second reason as well: it is nucleophilic enough to attack the acid chloride first, and the N-acylpyridinium ion it forms is more electrophilic than the acid chloride was, so pyridine speeds the acylation up as well as scavenging the HCl. That is the whole basis of DMAP catalysis, and it is why calling pyridine “non-nucleophilic” is a mistake.

Because acylation is clean and reversible by hydrolysis, it doubles as a protecting group strategy for amines — convert to an amide, do chemistry elsewhere, hydrolyze back. The Boc and Cbz groups used throughout peptide synthesis are carbamates working on exactly this principle, chosen because they come off under conditions mild enough to leave the peptide intact.

Reductive amination: the reliable route

Combining amine chemistry with carbonyl chemistry solves the over-alkylation problem completely. An amine condenses with an aldehyde or ketone to give an imine (Carbonyl Chemistry), and that imine is then reduced to the amine.

The condensation is worth spelling out, because three later reactions run through the same intermediates. The amine's lone pair attacks the carbonyl carbon and the pi electrons go up onto oxygen; a proton then shifts from nitrogen to that alkoxide oxygen, giving a hemiaminal (also called a carbinolamine) — one carbon carrying both an OH and an NHR′. The hemiaminal is almost never isolable. Protonating its OH turns it into water, which leaves with a push from the nitrogen lone pair to give an iminium ion, R₂C=N⁺HR′; losing the proton from nitrogen gives the neutral imine. The whole sequence needs mildly acidic conditions, around pH 4–5: too basic and there is no acid to protonate the OH, too acidic and the amine is protonated and cannot attack in the first place. A secondary amine reaches the iminium ion and then has no N–H to lose, so it loses an α C–H instead and gives an enamine.

The reason it does not over-react is that the new C–N bond is built through a controlled two-step sequence rather than a runaway chain: only one carbonyl can condense with a given nitrogen at a time, and the reduction step commits the product before it can do anything else.

The reducing agent matters. NaBH₃CN (sodium cyanoborohydride) is the standard choice because the electron-withdrawing cyano group makes it mild enough to reduce the protonated imine selectively while leaving the ketone starting material alone — which means the condensation and the reduction can be run in the same flask at the same time. Plain NaBH₄ would reduce the ketone before it had a chance to form the imine. Sodium triacetoxyborohydride, NaBH(OAc)₃, does the same job and is now the more common choice in practice. Both are attenuated hydride sources: the electron-withdrawing groups on boron make them weak enough to ignore a ketone and still strong enough to reduce the far more electrophilic protonated iminium.

Worked example — two routes to N-ethylcyclohexylamine

Route A, alkylation: cyclohexylamine + ethyl bromide. Gives a mixture of the secondary amine, the tertiary amine, the quaternary salt, and unreacted starting material. Poor yield, hard separation.

Route B, reductive amination: cyclohexylamine + acetaldehyde, with NaBH₃CN. The imine forms and is reduced — and here is the control: only one equivalent of acetaldehyde was added, so only one ethyl group can be delivered. The secondary amine product can condense again with a second equivalent of acetaldehyde to give the tertiary amine, and over-alkylation is a real side reaction here too. The difference from Route A is what governs it. There it was relative rates, which you cannot adjust; here it is how much carbonyl you weighed out. Use one equivalent of aldehyde and one equivalent is what gets installed.

Same bond formed, same fragments, and only one route is practical. This is why reductive amination is one of the most-used reactions in medicinal chemistry.

An amine's basicity will sabotage a reaction before its nucleophilicity helps. With any acidic substrate, the amine simply takes the proton — react an amine with a carboxylic acid and you get an ammonium carboxylate salt, not an amide. To make the amide you must first activate the acid, usually as the acid chloride. Whenever an amine is a reagent, check whether there is an acidic proton for it to grab first.
Worked example — one amine, four reagents

Give the product when benzylamine, PhCH₂NH₂, is treated with each of: (a) acetyl chloride and Et₃N; (b) excess CH₃I; (c) acetone and NaBH₃CN; (d) acetic acid.

(a) Acetyl chloride + Et₃N. Nucleophilic acyl substitution. The nitrogen attacks the carbonyl carbon, chloride leaves, the nitrogen loses its proton to Et₃N. Product: PhCH₂NH–C(=O)CH₃, N-benzylacetamide. It stops there, because the amide nitrogen has no nucleophilic lone pair left.

(b) Excess CH₃I. Nothing stops it. Three successive SN2 alkylations give the quaternary salt PhCH₂N(CH₃)₃⁺ I⁻. With limited CH₃I you would get a mixture, which is the whole point of this section.

(c) Acetone + NaBH₃CN. Condensation to the imine PhCH₂N=C(CH₃)₂, then reduction of that C=N. Product: PhCH₂NH–CH(CH₃)₂, a secondary amine. Note where the new bond is — on the old carbonyl carbon.

(d) Acetic acid. A trap, and no amide. The amine is a base and acetic acid is an acid, so all that happens is a proton transfer: PhCH₂NH₃⁺ ⁻O₂CCH₃. To reach the amide you would have to heat that salt above about 150 °C, or, in practice, activate the acid first.

Diazonium salts and the aromatic connection

Primary aromatic amines react with nitrous acid, generated in situ from NaNO₂ and HCl at 0 °C, to give diazonium salts, ArN₂⁺. These are remarkable because N₂ is the best leaving group in organic chemistry — nitrogen gas is so stable and so entropically favored on leaving that its departure is essentially irreversible.

The sequence is worth running once, because it is ordinary amine nucleophilicity doing the work. HCl protonates and dehydrates the nitrous acid to the nitrosonium ion, N≡O⁺ — a small, linear electrophile. The amine’s lone pair attacks it and the nitrogen loses a proton, giving an N-nitrosoamine; two tautomerizations and the loss of water then leave Ar–N≡N⁺. That is why the reaction needs acid (to make NO⁺) and why it needs a primary amine: a secondary amine has no second N–H to tautomerize through and stops at the nitrosoamine. It is also why the flask is kept at 0–5 °C — above about 5 °C the diazonium salt decomposes, which loses the reaction and releases nitrogen gas into a warm flask.

What nitrous acid does depends on the class of amine, and all three outcomes get asked about. A primary alkyl amine gives an alkyl diazonium ion that loses N₂ almost the instant it forms, leaving a carbocation and whatever mixture of substitution, elimination and rearrangement products follows — useless for synthesis, the one exception being the analytical trick of measuring the nitrogen gas evolved. A secondary amine, alkyl or aromatic alike, stops at the stable yellow N-nitrosamine, R₂N–N=O, because after nitrosation there is no second N–H to lose; these are potent carcinogens, which is why nitrite levels in cured meat are regulated. The primary aromatic amine is the useful case, and it is the one above.

That is what makes the salt worth making: the nitrogen can be swapped for almost anything, including groups — OH, CN, F, I — that electrophilic aromatic substitution cannot install at all. The copper-mediated Sandmeyer reactions, the rest of that menu, and the azo coupling that built the dye industry are the business of Aromatic Follow-Through; what matters here is that a primary aromatic amine is the gateway to all of it.

The synthetic significance, developed in full in Aromatic Follow-Through, is that an amino group becomes a placeholder. You install a nitro group on a ring where you want it, reduce it to an amine, use the amine's powerful directing effect to place the next substituent, then convert the amine to a diazonium salt and swap it for whatever the target actually needs. Aliphatic diazonium salts, by contrast, decompose immediately to carbocations and are not synthetically useful.

What carries forward

Reductive amination and acylation are standard moves in any synthesis involving nitrogen. Diazonium chemistry is the bridge back to Aromatic Chemistry: it is what lifts the limits the directing rules put on which substituent patterns a ring can be given, and the next chapter spends a whole section on it. And the recurring theme — control a reaction by making its product unreactive — is one of the more transferable ideas in the course.
