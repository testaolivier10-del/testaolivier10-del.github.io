Polymers · Section 120 of 121

Structure and properties

Practice this — interactive lesson

Two samples of polyethylene can be a shopping bag and a drainpipe. Same monomer, same repeat unit, same formula — and the difference is entirely in how the chains are arranged relative to each other. This section is about reading structure and predicting the material.

Crystalline and amorphous regions

A polymer is not a crystal and not a liquid. Stretches of chain that lie neatly against their neighbors form crystalline regions; stretches that are tangled form amorphous ones. Almost every real polymer has both, and the fraction that is crystalline is what most of its properties track.

  • More crystalline → denser, stronger, stiffer, more opaque, higher melting.
  • More amorphous → softer, more flexible, more transparent, no sharp melting point.

The opacity is a useful tell. Crystalline regions scatter light because their refractive index differs from the amorphous regions around them, so a highly crystalline polymer is cloudy and a fully amorphous one can be glass-clear. That is why PMMA and polycarbonate are used for windows and high-density polyethylene never is.

Be careful with the last row. Ordinary polystyrene is amorphous because radical polymerization makes it atactic, not because the phenyl group is bulky; syndiotactic polystyrene carries the same bulky group and is crystalline, melting near 270 °C. What bulk does is raise Tg and slow crystallization down, which is enough to keep a randomly placed chain from ever finding order.

This is the same argument as the fatty acids in the biomolecules chapter. Straight chains stack and melt high; kinked or branched chains do not. One idea, applied to a triglyceride in one chapter and to polyethylene in another.
Three material classes fall out of just two variables, crystallinity and cross-linking. A fiber is highly crystalline, has strong forces between its chains, and is drawn during manufacture to align them: nylon, PET, Kevlar. A plastic is partly crystalline or else glassy, stiff but still formable: polyethylene, polystyrene, PVC. An elastomer is amorphous, sits above its Tg, and is lightly cross-linked so that it springs back: vulcanized rubber. Naming the class first predicts most of the properties before you have looked at a single structure.

Two transition temperatures, not one

A small molecule has a melting point. A polymer has up to two transitions, and they describe different regions:

  • Tg, the glass transition temperature. Below it the amorphous regions are frozen and the material is hard and brittle; above it they can move. Whether the sample then feels rubbery depends on what else is holding it: with little crystallinity and no cross-links it is rubbery, but HDPE, PET and nylon are all far above their Tg at room temperature and stay rigid, because their crystallites act as physical cross-links up to Tm. Essentially every polymer has a Tg, because essentially every sample has some amorphous material in it.
  • Tm, the melting temperature. Where the crystalline regions melt. Only polymers with crystalline regions have one.

Read that table with two questions in hand. Is room temperature above or below Tg, and is there any crystallinity? Below Tg you have a glass: rigid, and brittle if nothing else is holding it. Above Tg with neither crystals nor cross-links you have a melt or a gum; above Tg with either of them you have a rubber or a tough plastic. As a rule of thumb Tg sits between a half and two-thirds of Tm on the absolute scale, so the two numbers are not independent of each other.

Tg is why the same material behaves differently with temperature in ordinary life. Natural rubber has a Tg well below room temperature, so it is elastic; cool it in liquid nitrogen and it shatters like glass, because you have taken it below Tg.

The difference is covalent, and it comes back to counting reactive sites. Two sites per monomer gives chains, which can slide past each other when heated. Three or more gives a network, and a network cannot melt because melting would mean breaking covalent bonds.

Cross-linking as a dial

Vulcanization is the clearest case. Natural rubber is a polymer of isoprene with a double bond in every repeat unit; heat it with sulfur and short S–S bridges form between chains. The bridges do not attach at random. S8 opens at the temperature of the process, and the sulfur attacks the allylic positions next to those retained double bonds — the same allylic C–H weakness that made allylic and benzylic radicals easy to form in earlier chapters. So the double bond left over from 1,4-polymerization is not a leftover at all: it is the handle vulcanization needs, and it is why a saturated chain like polyethylene cannot be vulcanized. The number of bridges is a dial:

  • None — chains slide past each other permanently. Sticky, and it flows.
  • A few percent — chains can stretch apart and are pulled back. This is elastic, and it is what a tire is.
  • Heavily cross-linked — nothing moves. Hard and brittle, like ebonite.

Everything a rubber band does is set by where on that dial it sits, and the dial is turned by one number: how much sulfur went in.

Plasticizers: lowering Tg without changing the polymer

There is a way to make a polymer softer that touches neither its monomer nor its architecture: mix in a small, non-volatile molecule that gets in between the chains. A plasticizer — typically a phthalate diester — holds the chains further apart and weakens the forces between them, so they can start moving at a lower temperature. The whole effect is to drop Tg, often by a hundred degrees, and it is entirely physical: no bond in the polymer is made or broken.

PVC is the case to remember, because it is the same polymer on both sides of the comparison. Unplasticized, its Tg is about 80 °C, well above room temperature, so it is glassy and rigid: drainpipe and window frame. Add 30–40% of a phthalate and Tg drops below room temperature, so that same polymer is now above its glass transition in ordinary use: flexible flooring, cable insulation, shower curtains. And because the plasticizer is only mixed in, it leaches out slowly over years — which is why old PVC goes brittle and cracks. No bond has changed; the Tg has simply climbed back up through room temperature.
Elasticity is an entropy effect, which is worth stating plainly because it is not obvious. Stretching a rubber pulls its coiled chains straight, and a straight chain has far fewer available conformations than a coiled one — so it has lower entropy. Releasing it lets entropy pull the chains back into a coil. The cross-links are what stop them simply sliding apart instead.

What carries forward

Read the structure and predict the material: linear and regular means crystalline, strong and opaque; branched or atactic means amorphous and flexible; hydrogen bonds between chains mean strong; cross-links mean it cannot be melted. And two numbers rather than one — Tg for the amorphous regions, Tm for the crystalline ones.
