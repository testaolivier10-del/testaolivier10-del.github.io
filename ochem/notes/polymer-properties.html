Polymers · Section 120 of 121

Structure and properties

Practice this — interactive lesson

A thin shopping bag and a stiff drainpipe can both be made of polyethylene. They share the same monomer, the same repeat unit and the same formula. What differs is how the chains are arranged relative to each other. This section shows how to read that arrangement from a structure and predict the material it gives.

Crystalline and amorphous regions

Follow one chain through a piece of polyethylene. In some stretches it lies straight, side by side with its neighbors, packed as neatly as the particles in a crystal. These ordered stretches make up the crystalline regions. In other stretches the same chain loops and tangles with the chains around it, with no order at all. These make up the amorphous regions; amorphous means “without a regular shape.”

Almost every real polymer has both kinds of region. The share of the sample that is crystalline is its crystallinity, and most of its properties follow that number:

  • More crystalline → denser, stronger, stiffer, more opaque, higher melting.
  • More amorphous → softer, more flexible, more transparent, no sharp melting point.

Opacity is a useful tell. A material’s refractive index is how strongly it bends light, and a crystalline region bends light by a slightly different amount than the amorphous material around it. Light therefore scatters at every boundary between the two, so a highly crystalline polymer looks cloudy. A fully amorphous polymer has no such boundaries and can be glass-clear. That is why PMMA and polycarbonate are used for windows and high-density polyethylene never is, and why a clear plastic is usually an amorphous one.

What lets chains pack

Start with two fatty acids from Lipids. Stearic acid has a straight chain and melts at 69 °C. Oleic acid has the same eighteen carbons with one cis kink, and it melts at 13 °C. Straight chains lie flat against each other, so the London dispersion forces between them add up along their whole length; a kink holds them apart.

Polymer chains follow the same rule. A chain that is straight and regular can lie against its neighbors and crystallize, while a branch or an irregular pattern of side groups keeps the chains apart. The two structural variables from Addition polymers work exactly this way: branching, and tacticity, the pattern of side groups along the chain (all on one side for isotactic, alternating for syndiotactic, random for atactic). Hydrogen bonds between chains, the subject of Condensation polymers, go further and tie neighboring chains together.

An atactic polymer is amorphous, but that does not settle whether it is soft. Atactic polypropylene is a sticky goo, while ordinary polystyrene, also atactic, is a rigid glass. The difference is the subject of the next section.

Two transition temperatures, not one

Natural rubber is soft and stretchy at room temperature. Cool a rubber ball in liquid nitrogen, at −196 °C, and drop it: it shatters like glass. No bond in it has changed. Its chains have simply lost the ability to move.

A small molecule has one melting point. A polymer has up to two transitions, and each belongs to a different kind of region:

  • Tg, the glass transition temperature, belongs to the amorphous regions. Below it, their tangled chains are frozen in place and the material is hard and brittle, like glass. Above it, the chains can wriggle and slide. Essentially every polymer has a Tg, because essentially every sample contains some amorphous material.
  • Tm, the melting temperature, belongs to the crystalline regions: it is where they melt. Only a polymer with crystalline regions has one.

Above Tg, whether a sample feels rubbery depends on what else holds it together. At room temperature HDPE is about 145 °C above its Tg, yet it stays rigid. Its crystalline regions hold the mobile chains in place until they melt at Tm. They act as physical cross-links. A cross-link is anything that ties one chain to another; a physical one does it by packing rather than by a bond.

Read the table with two questions in hand. Is room temperature above or below Tg? And is there any crystallinity? Below Tg the polymer is a glass: rigid, and brittle unless something else toughens it. Above Tg with neither crystalline regions nor cross-links, it is a gum or a thick liquid that flows. Above Tg with either of them, it is a rubber or a tough plastic.

The two temperatures are not independent of each other. As a rough rule, Tg is between a half and two-thirds of Tm when both are measured in kelvins. PET (343 K against 533 K) and nylon 6,6 (323 K against 538 K) fit the rule; polyethylene falls below it.

Worked example — why ordinary polystyrene is a clear, brittle glass

The case. Polystyrene carries a bulky phenyl group, a benzene ring, on every other carbon of its chain. Ordinary polystyrene is glass-clear, rigid and brittle, and it has no melting point. Is the bulk of the phenyl group the cause?

Step 1 — check the pattern. Ordinary polystyrene is made by radical polymerization, which gives an atactic chain: each phenyl group points one way or the other at random. Random chains cannot pack, so the polymer is amorphous, and that is why it is clear.

Step 2 — compare a regular chain. Syndiotactic polystyrene, made with a metal catalyst related to the Ziegler–Natta catalysts, carries the same phenyl groups, alternating from side to side. Its chains pack, and it is crystalline, melting near 270 °C. So the bulk alone does not prevent packing; the random pattern does.

Step 3 — find what the bulk does do. A large side group makes it harder for the backbone to twist, so the chains need more heat before they can move. That raises Tg to about 100 °C. (In isotactic polystyrene the bulky rings also make crystallization slow.) At room temperature, then, ordinary polystyrene is an amorphous polymer below its Tg, with no crystalline regions to toughen it: a rigid, brittle glass.

Cross-linking as a dial

Raw natural rubber is sticky, and on a warm day it slowly flows. Heat it with sulfur and it becomes the springy material of a tire. That change is vulcanization. It works by adding cross-links made of covalent bonds: short bridges of sulfur atoms, –S–S–, each tying one chain to a neighbor.

The bridges do not attach at random. Natural rubber is cis-polyisoprene, made by 1,4-addition, so every repeat unit keeps one C=C (see Addition polymers). During the process the S8 ring, the eight-atom ring that ordinary sulfur is made of, opens up. The sulfur then bonds at allylic carbons, the carbons right next to a C=C. Allylic hydrogens are the easiest in the chain to remove, for the reason given in Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions: the radical left behind is spread over the neighboring double bond.

So the double bond left over from 1,4-addition is not a leftover at all. It is the handle vulcanization needs, and it is why a saturated chain such as polyethylene cannot be vulcanized with sulfur.

The number of bridges works like a dial, and the amount of sulfur sets it:

  • None — the chains slide past each other for good. The rubber is sticky, and it flows.
  • A few percent sulfur — the chains can be stretched apart and are pulled back. The rubber is elastic; this is a tire or a rubber band.
  • Heavily cross-linked — nothing can move. The result is hard and brittle, like ebonite (hard rubber).

Why a stretched rubber springs back

Stretch a rubber band and it pulls back. A metal spring pulls back too, but for a different reason: stretching a spring strains its bonds, and the strained bonds pull back. In rubber the pull comes from entropy, a measure of how many ways the molecules can arrange themselves.

A coiled chain can take an enormous number of shapes. Pull it nearly straight and very few shapes are left, so stretching lowers the entropy. Let go, and the chains return to the far more probable coiled state. The cross-links keep them from simply sliding past one another instead, which is why an elastic rubber needs them.

Worked example — two odd things a rubber band does

The observations. Stretch a rubber band quickly and touch it to your lip: it feels warm. Hang a weight from a rubber band and warm the band with a hair dryer: it shortens and lifts the weight, where almost any other material would expand.

The warmth. Stretching a rubber barely changes the energy of its chains, because no bond is strained. The work you do has to go somewhere, so it comes out as heat.

The shortening. The pull toward the coil is an entropy effect, and entropy enters the free energy as TΔS. The higher the temperature, the larger that term, so the pull grows as the band warms, and a stretched band contracts.

Thermoplastic against thermoset

Heat a polyethylene milk bottle and it softens; it can be melted and molded again. Heat a cured epoxy glue or a Bakelite handle and it never softens; it chars. The first is a thermoplastic, the second a thermoset.

ExamplesPE, PET, nylonEpoxy, Bakelite, vulcanized rubber

The difference is covalent, and it comes back to counting reactive sites, as in What a polymer is. Two reactive sites per monomer give separate chains. Only intermolecular forces hold those chains to each other, and heat can overcome them, so the chains slide apart and the plastic softens. Three or more sites, or cross-linking, give a network, in which covalent bonds join the whole sample into one molecule. A network cannot melt, because melting would mean breaking those bonds; heated far enough, it decomposes instead. Vulcanized rubber is a network too, which is why a worn tire cannot be melted down and remolded.

Plasticizers: lowering Tg without changing the polymer

Rigid PVC makes drainpipes and window frames. The same polymer, made soft, becomes cable insulation, flooring and shower curtains. The chains are identical in both. The soft kind also contains a plasticizer: a small molecule that does not evaporate easily, typically a phthalate diester, mixed in among the chains.

The plasticizer molecules sit between the chains, hold them further apart and weaken the forces between them. The chains can then start moving at a lower temperature. So a plasticizer lowers Tg, often by a hundred degrees, and the effect is entirely physical: no bond in the polymer is made or broken.

Unplasticized PVC has a Tg of about 80 °C, well above room temperature, so it is glassy and rigid. Adding 30–40% of a phthalate drops Tg below room temperature, so the same polymer is above its glass transition in ordinary use and flexible. Because the plasticizer is only mixed in, it slowly leaches out over the years. Tg then climbs back up through room temperature, which is why old flexible PVC goes brittle and cracks.

Fibers, plastics and elastomers

A nylon thread, a polystyrene cup and a rubber band belong to three different classes of material. Crystallinity, Tg and cross-linking together decide the class, and naming the class predicts most of a material’s properties before you look at a single structure.

  • A fiber is highly crystalline and has strong forces between its chains. It is drawn during manufacture, meaning stretched, so its chains line up along the fiber: nylon, PET, Kevlar.
  • A plastic is partly crystalline, or else amorphous and below its Tg. It is stiff, but it can still be shaped when hot: polyethylene, polystyrene, PVC.
  • An elastomer is amorphous, sits above its Tg, and is lightly cross-linked so that it springs back: vulcanized rubber.

What carries forward

Read the structure and predict the material. Linear, regular chains crystallize, so the material is strong and opaque; branched or atactic chains stay amorphous. Hydrogen bonds between chains make a polymer strong, and cross-links mean it cannot be melted. Keep two temperatures in mind rather than one: Tg for the amorphous regions and Tm for the crystalline ones.
