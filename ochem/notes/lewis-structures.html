The recipe

The octet rule (from Atomic Structure) says most atoms are most stable with 8 electrons in their outer shell. A Lewis structure maps how a molecule's valence electrons are arranged to get every atom as close to that as possible — some as shared bonding pairs, some as lone pairs on one atom alone.

  1. Count total valence electrons.
  2. Connect atoms with single bonds.
  3. Give outer atoms full octets with lone pairs.
  4. Put any leftover electrons on the central atom.
  5. If the central atom is still short an octet, turn a lone pair on a neighbor into a double bond — that lone pair becomes a second shared bonding pair, giving the central atom extra electrons without changing the total count.

Worked example: CO₂

Valence electrons: C (4) + O (6) + O (6) = 16. Two single bonds leave carbon with only 4 electrons around it — 4 short of an octet. Converting both C–O single bonds to double bonds gives carbon a full octet (4 bonds = 8 electrons) and leaves every atom's formal charge at 0.

When more than one structure seems valid, formal charge is the tiebreaker: the realistic structure keeps formal charges as close to zero as possible. For CO₂, two double bonds (all charges 0) beats one single + one triple bond (charges of −1 and +1).

Three real exceptions to the octet rule

Incomplete octets (B, Be): BF₃ stops at 6 electrons on boron — correct as drawn, and exactly why BF₃ is such a strong electrophile (equivalently a Lewis acid — the acid/base term for an electron-pair acceptor, Module 3). Expanded octets (S, P, and other period 3+ elements): these really can hold more than 8 electrons (SF₆, H₂SO₄) — because they are big enough to fit extra neighbors and the extra bonds carry real ionic character, not because empty d orbitals mix in, as older textbooks claimed (see the step below). C/N/O/F can never do this; period 2 atoms are simply too small. Odd-electron species (free radicals): an odd total electron count makes a full octet on every atom impossible — covered later when radical mechanisms come up.
