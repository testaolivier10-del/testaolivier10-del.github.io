Lewis structures

Practice this — interactive lesson

A Lewis structure is the working drawing of organic chemistry. It shows which atoms are joined to which, where every valence electron sits, and which atoms carry charge — and from those three things you can predict geometry, polarity, acidity and reactivity. Nearly every mistake in a mechanism can be traced to a Lewis structure drawn carelessly at the start.

The method you will actually use: the valence rule

General chemistry teaches one algorithm for Lewis structures — count the electrons, pick a central atom, decorate outward. It works, and the next section runs it twice. But it assumes the molecule has a central atom, and organic molecules do not. Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, is a chain of three heavy atoms; nothing is central. So organic chemists use a different method, faster and better suited to molecules built from chains.

It rests on one table, which is the Atomic Structure section's octet counting written out. Every neutral atom in this course has a preferred number of bonds and a matching number of lone pairs, and together they always come to a full outer shell:

AtomValence e⁻BondsLone pairsWhy
H1101 shared pair fills its K shell (2)
C4404 shared pairs = 8 electrons
N5313 shared + 1 kept = 8
O6222 shared + 2 kept = 8
F, Cl, Br, I7131 shared + 3 kept = 8
bonds + lone pairs = 4 for C, N, O and the halogens  ·  and bonds go up as lone pairs go down

The procedure is then: (1) write the skeleton the formula gives you, joining the heavy atoms in the order they are written and hanging the hydrogens off them; (2) give every atom its quota of bonds from the table, adding double or triple bonds where a single bond leaves an atom short; (3) fill in lone pairs to bring every atom to its quota; (4) compute formal charges, and if an atom departs from its quota, write the charge the previous section's arithmetic gives.

Step 4 is the one that turns a rule into a method. An atom is neutral when it hits its quota. Every departure is a charge, and the direction is fixed: an extra bond in place of a lone pair is a positive charge (oxygen with 3 bonds, nitrogen with 4), a lone pair in place of a bond is a negative charge (oxygen with 1 bond, carbon with 3 bonds and a pair).

Reading a condensed formula

Organic molecules are usually written in condensed form, and until skeletal drawings arrive in Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons it is the only compact notation you have. Three conventions cover nearly all of it.

Hydrogens follow the atom they are on. In CH₃CH₂OH, the first carbon carries three hydrogens, the second carries two, and the O carries one. Read left to right and each heavy atom is bonded to the heavy atom before it.

Parentheses mean branches. (CH₃)₂CHOH is a central CH with two methyl groups hanging off it and an OH; (CH₃)₃COH has three methyls on a carbon that has no hydrogen left. The subscript outside the bracket counts copies of the whole group, not atoms.

Bonds are shown only when they are not single. CH₃C≡N and CH₂=CH₂ spell out the multiple bond; everything else joins with single bonds. A written-out O straight after a carbon, as in CH₃COOH or CH₃COOCH₃, is the awkward case: the first O is the doubly bonded one and the second is the one that continues the chain. That convention is worked through in the Functional Groups section, where it matters most.

Worked example — ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH

Skeleton. C–C–O, with three H on the first carbon, two on the second, one on the oxygen.

Quotas. Carbon 1: three bonds to H plus one to carbon 2 = 4. ✓ Carbon 2: two H, one C, one O = 4. ✓ Oxygen: one C, one H = 2. ✓ Every hydrogen has 1. ✓ No multiple bonds are needed — every atom hit its quota with single bonds.

Lone pairs. Only the oxygen is short: 2 bonds means 2 lone pairs. Carbons get none. Total drawn: 8 bonds and 2 lone pairs, which is 20 electrons — and counting valence electrons the other way, 2(4) + 6(1) + 6 = 20. ✓

Charges. Every atom sits at its quota, so every formal charge is zero, and the molecule is neutral. ✓

Worked example — methylamine, CH₃NH₂

Skeleton. C–N, three H on carbon, two on nitrogen.

Quotas. Carbon: 3 H + 1 N = 4 ✓. Nitrogen: 1 C + 2 H = 3 ✓.

Lone pairs. Nitrogen's quota is 3 bonds and 1 lone pair, so it gets one lone pair — and that lone pair is the whole chemistry of amines. It is what makes methylamine basic, what makes it a nucleophile, and what makes the molecule pyramidal rather than flat.

Charges. All zero. Now protonate it: add an H⁺ to that lone pair and nitrogen has 4 bonds and 0 lone pairs, so FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. The ion CH₃NH₃⁺ is drawn with the charge on nitrogen, and nothing else about the drawing changes.

Worked example — acetic acid, CH₃COOH

Skeleton. CH₃–C–O–H with a second oxygen on the middle carbon: the condensed formula's two O's are not a chain of two oxygens, they are one doubly bonded oxygen and one that carries the H.

Quotas. The methyl carbon: 3 H + 1 C = 4 ✓. The middle carbon: 1 C + 1 O(H) = 2, two short — so the other oxygen takes a double bond, bringing it to 4 ✓, and that oxygen to 2 ✓.

Lone pairs. The OH oxygen has 2 bonds → 2 lone pairs. The C=O oxygen has 2 bonds (the double bond counts twice) → 2 lone pairs.

Charges. Every atom at quota, all zero. Now remove the O–H hydrogen as H⁺, leaving acetate: that oxygen now has 1 bond and 3 lone pairs, FC = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1, and the ion carries a single negative charge. ✓

Worked example — an organic ion, methoxide CH₃O⁻

Skeleton. C–O, three H on the carbon, none on the oxygen.

Quotas. Carbon: 3 H + 1 O = 4 ✓. Oxygen: only 1 bond — one short of its quota of 2.

Lone pairs and charge. Below quota by one bond means one extra lone pair and one negative charge: oxygen gets 3 lone pairs and a −1. Check: 6 − 6 − 1 = −1 ✓, and the sum over the molecule is −1, matching the ion. The charge goes on the oxygen in the drawing, not floating beside the formula.

The general-chemistry recipe, for molecules with a real center

The octet rule says most atoms are most stable with 8 electrons in their outer shell (2 for hydrogen). A Lewis structure arranges a molecule's valence electrons — some as shared bonding pairs, some as lone pairs on a single atom — to get every atom as close to that as possible.

  1. Count total valence electrons. Add up each atom's group-number valence count. Then adjust for charge: add one electron per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge.
  2. Connect the atoms with single bonds. This recipe is for small molecules that genuinely have one central atom surrounded by others — CO₂, NO₃⁻, BF₃ — and there the least electronegative atom (never hydrogen) takes the middle. It is not a rule about organic molecules: in CH₃OH nothing is "central", and you use the valence rule above instead. Each bond uses 2 of your electrons.
  3. Give the outer atoms full octets with lone pairs, working from the most electronegative outward.
  4. Put any electrons still left over on the central atom.
  5. If the central atom is still short of an octet, convert a lone pair on a neighbor into a second shared pair — a double bond. This gives the central atom more electrons without changing the total count, which is why step 1 is the one you must not get wrong.

Then check formal charges, and check that they sum to the overall charge. That check is the whole reason the previous section exists.

Worked example — CO₂

Count: C (4) + O (6) + O (6) = 16 valence electrons.

Connect: O–C–O with two single bonds uses 4, leaving 12.

Fill the outer atoms: three lone pairs on each oxygen uses all 12, leaving 0.

Check the center: carbon has only 2 bonds — 4 electrons, four short of an octet.

Fix it: move one lone pair from each oxygen into a second bond, giving O=C=O. Carbon now has 4 bonds (8 electrons) and each oxygen has 2 bonds plus 2 lone pairs (8 electrons). Formal charges: carbon 4 − 0 − 4 = 0; each oxygen 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. All zero, summing to 0 for a neutral molecule. ✓

Worked example — a charged species, NO₃⁻

Count: N (5) + 3 × O (6) = 23, plus 1 for the negative charge = 24.

Connect: three N–O single bonds uses 6, leaving 18 — exactly three lone pairs for each oxygen. All 24 placed.

Check the center: nitrogen has 3 bonds, 6 electrons, two short.

Fix it: move one lone pair from one oxygen into a double bond. Now nitrogen has 4 bonds: FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. The doubly-bonded oxygen is 6 − 4 − 2 = 0; the two singly-bonded oxygens are 6 − 6 − 1 = −1 each. Sum: +1 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −1. ✓

Note that the choice of which oxygen gets the double bond was arbitrary — three equally good structures exist. That is not a defect in the method; it is the molecule telling you it has resonance, which is the subject of Drawing Molecules & Moving Electrons.

Using formal charge as the tiebreaker

When more than one arrangement satisfies the octet rule, the realistic structure is the one that keeps formal charges closest to zero, and puts any negative charge on the most electronegative atom. For CO₂, the symmetric O=C=O with every formal charge at zero beats the alternative with one single and one triple bond, which forces a −1 on one oxygen and a +1 on the other. Same atom count, same octets, worse charge distribution.

Three real exceptions to the octet rule

Incomplete octets. Boron and beryllium routinely stop short. BF₃ has only 6 electrons on boron, and that is the correct structure as drawn, not an error — which is exactly why BF₃ is such a powerful electrophile, or in the vocabulary of Acids & Bases, a strong Lewis acid: an electron-pair acceptor with a genuinely empty orbital waiting. Carbocations belong in this family too.

Expanded octets. This is the one exception the rest of the chapter points back to, so it is worth stating properly once. Start with why period 2 cannot: the n = 2 shell contains exactly four orbitals, one 2s and three 2p, and four orbitals hold eight electrons. A ninth electron on carbon, nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine would have nowhere to sit. That is the reason — not size, not a rule someone made up — and it is why "I gave carbon five bonds" is the single most common structural error in a first organic course, with no exception anywhere in chemistry.

Period 3 and beyond really do go past eight: SF₆, H₂SO₄, PCl₅ are all real. Older textbooks explained this by saying the empty 3d orbitals mix into the bonding, which is where the labels "sp³d" and "sp³d²" came from. Modern computation has retired that explanation — genuine d-orbital participation turns out to be minimal. What does the explaining is that a period-3 atom is physically larger, so six neighbors fit around it without crowding, and that the extra bonds carry substantial ionic character, so the central atom is not really sharing a full covalent pair with each one. The shapes and the practical rule are unaffected: sulfur and phosphorus can exceed an octet; carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine never can.

Odd-electron species. When the total valence electron count is odd, a full octet on every atom is arithmetically impossible. Nitric oxide (NO) has 11 valence electrons and one unpaired electron; so does every free radical. These are real, they are reactive, and they get their own treatment when radical mechanisms come up.

Four structures that are wrong, and what is wrong with them. Each of these is drawn constantly and each is caught by the valence rule in two seconds.
  • A hydrogen with two bonds. Hydrogen's only shell holds 2 electrons, which is one shared pair. There is no such thing as a bridging H in an ordinary organic structure, and an H drawn between two atoms is a slip of the pen.
  • An oxygen with three bonds and no charge. Three bonds puts oxygen above its quota, so FC = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1. Either draw the charge or redraw the bond. This is the commonest arrow-pushing casualty: a nucleophile attacks with an oxygen lone pair and the charge is forgotten.
  • A nitrogen with four bonds and no charge. Same arithmetic: 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. A four-bonded nitrogen always carries a plus, from ammonium to a protonated amine to the nitrogen of a nitro group.
  • A carbon with four bonds and a lone pair. That is ten electrons on a period-2 atom — the same impossibility as five bonds, wearing a different hat. Carbon gets four bonds and nothing else, or three bonds and one pair (carbanion, −1), or three bonds and nothing (carbocation, +1).
Carbon never gets five bonds. If a structure you have drawn has a carbon with five bonds, it is wrong, full stop — there is no exception, no resonance form, and no clever argument that rescues it. The same applies to nitrogen: four bonds maximum, and a nitrogen with four bonds always carries a +1 charge. When arrow-pushing goes wrong later in the course, this is usually the symptom, and the cause is almost always forgetting to break a bond while forming a new one.
Practice the count-and-check loop until it is automatic: total electrons, place them, verify octets, verify formal charges, verify the sum. It takes under a minute for any molecule in this course, and it catches errors that would otherwise propagate through an entire mechanism. Most structural mistakes in organic exams are not conceptual — they are a miscount in step 1.

What carries forward

Everything. Molecular geometry is read off a completed Lewis structure; resonance is the observation that some molecules need more than one; arrow pushing is the practice of converting one Lewis structure into another legally; and every mechanism in the course is a sequence of Lewis structures. If one section of Foundations is worth over-practising, it is this one.
