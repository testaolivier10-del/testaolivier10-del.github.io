Sigma bonds

A sigma (σ) bond forms from head-on orbital overlap, directly on the axis between the two nuclei. Every single bond is a sigma bond. Because the overlap is symmetric all the way around that axis, the atoms can freely rotate around it without disturbing anything.

Pi bonds

A pi (π) bond comes from the unhybridized p orbitals left over after hybridization (sp² carbon has 1 leftover p; sp has 2). These stick out above/below or side-to-side rather than along the bond axis, and two neighboring ones overlap sideways. That sideways overlap only works while the two p orbitals stay parallel — twisting one atom rotates its p orbital out of alignment and breaks the overlap. That's why a pi bond blocks rotation while a sigma bond doesn't.

single bond = 1σ  ·  double bond = 1σ + 1π  ·  triple bond = 1σ + 2π
Restricted rotation around a double bond's pi bond is the entire reason cis/trans (E/Z) isomers exist — groups on either side of the double bond stay fixed relative to each other. More on this in the Stereochemistry module.

Bond length and strength

More bonds between the same two atoms means shorter and stronger. C–C: ~154 pm, ~347 kJ/mol (~83 kcal/mol). C=C: ~134 pm, ~614 kJ/mol (~147 kcal/mol). C≡C: ~120 pm, ~839 kJ/mol (~200 kcal/mol). Both units are in common use; 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ. The rest of this course quotes energies in kcal/mol. The jump from 1→2 bonds roughly doubles the energy; the second pi bond (2→3) adds less, since sideways p-orbital overlap is inherently weaker than the sigma bond's head-on overlap.
