Acids & Bases · Section 18 of 121

Brønsted acids/bases

Practice this — interactive lesson

Acid–base chemistry is the most common reaction in this course, and not by a small margin. Protons move on and off oxygen and nitrogen in nearly every mechanism you will draw — activating an electrophile, generating a nucleophile, cleaning up a charge at the end of a sequence. It is also the cleanest place to learn to reason quantitatively about stability, because one number, pKa, captures the whole thing.

The Brønsted–Lowry definition

A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor. A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. Every acid–base reaction is a proton transfer from the acid to the base, and nothing more mysterious than that.

This definition replaces the older Arrhenius one — acid produces H⁺ in water, base produces OH⁻ in water — which is too narrow to be useful here, because it only works in aqueous solution. Brønsted–Lowry works in any solvent or none, which matters a great deal given that most organic reactions are run in THF, ether, DMSO or dichloromethane rather than water.

Note what a proton actually is: a bare hydrogen nucleus with no electrons. It cannot exist free in solution; it is always attached to something. So "donating a proton" always means handing it to a base's lone pair, and the two halves of an acid–base reaction are inseparable. There is no acid without a base to take the proton.

Both products get a name, and they are used from here on, so fix them now. What is left of the acid once the proton has gone is its conjugate base; what the base turns into once it has taken the proton on is its conjugate acid. Acetate is the conjugate base of acetic acid; ammonium is the conjugate acid of ammonia. That is the whole definition for the moment — the next section works out what the relationship is good for.

Worked example — HCl + NH₃

Ammonia's nitrogen has a lone pair; HCl has a polarized H–Cl bond with the hydrogen δ+.

Two arrows, simultaneously: tail on nitrogen's lone pair, head on the hydrogen, forming a new N–H bond; and tail on the H–Cl bond, head onto chlorine, which leaves as chloride.

Products: NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻. HCl is the acid, NH₃ the base. Check the charges: 0 + 0 on the left, +1 − 1 on the right. ✓

The second arrow is not optional. Without it, the hydrogen would end up with two bonds.

Worked example — sodium methoxide + acetic acid

The same two arrows, on a reaction you will actually run. Sodium methoxide (NaOMe) is dissolved in methanol; acetic acid is added.

Step 1 — find the acid. Acetic acid has four hydrogens. Three sit on the methyl carbon; one sits on oxygen. The O–H is the acid, because the anion it leaves behind is a carboxylate with its charge shared over two oxygens, while the methyl anion would be a carbanion.

Step 2 — find the base. Methoxide, CH₃O⁻, carries a negative charge and three lone pairs on oxygen. Any one of them can take the proton.

Step 3 — draw both arrows. Arrow one starts on a methoxide lone pair and ends on the acidic hydrogen. Arrow two starts on the O–H bond and ends on the acetic acid oxygen. Both arrows are required.

Step 4 — check the charges. In: −1 on methoxide, 0 on acetic acid. Out: 0 on methanol, −1 on acetate. Balanced.

Step 5 — which side wins? Acid on the left, acetic acid, pKa 4.76. Acid on the right, methanol, pKa 15.5. The right-hand side holds the weaker acid by nearly eleven units, so the equilibrium sits there essentially completely. Methoxide deprotonates a carboxylic acid quantitatively — which is why carboxylate salts are trivially easy to make and why a carboxylic acid cannot survive in a flask containing an alkoxide.

Worth knowing before you meet the vocabulary: every proton transfer has three roles in it, and How Reactions Happen will give all three names. The electron-rich partner that attacks — here the base's lone pair — is called the nucleophile; the electron-poor site it attacks — here the acidic proton — is the electrophile; and the piece that departs with the bonding pair — here the conjugate base — is the leaving group. You do not need those words yet. You need the arrows, which are the same arrows in every mechanism in the book: the later ones are longer, but they are not made of anything else.

Which side does the equilibrium favor?

Every proton transfer is an equilibrium, drawn with ⇌ even when it appears to go to completion. The reaction favors the side with the weaker acid and weaker base — which is to say, the more stable, lower-energy side.

A useful way to hold this: the proton ends up where it is held most tightly. If the base on the left holds protons more strongly than the base on the right, the proton moves left to right and stays there. The next section quantifies this exactly, and the rule becomes a one-line pKa comparison.

A rough guide worth internalizing now: a difference of about 4 pKa units means the equilibrium lies roughly 10⁴ to one — effectively complete for practical purposes. Chemists say a reaction "goes" when the pKa gap is comfortably positive in the right direction, and organic synthesis is full of steps designed to make that gap large.

Proton transfers are fast

One practical fact deserves stating early. Proton transfers between electronegative atoms — oxygen to oxygen, oxygen to nitrogen — are among the fastest reactions in chemistry, often diffusion-limited. They are effectively never the slow step in a mechanism.

This has a consequence you will lean on constantly: in a multi-step mechanism you can move protons around freely between heteroatoms without worrying about the cost, and a mechanism is not wrong for including a proton-shuffling step. What you cannot do for free is remove a proton from carbon, which is slow and which is why Enolate Chemistry needs its own treatment.

Not every hydrogen is acidic. Hydrogens on carbon are, with rare exceptions, not removable by anything you will meet in the first half of the course — an alkane C–H has a pKa around 50, which is beyond any practical base. When asked to find the acidic proton in a molecule, look on oxygen and nitrogen first, then at carbons adjacent to a carbonyl or in a terminal alkyne, and only then consider anything else.

Recognizing the acid and the base

In an organic mechanism, the acid is often not a bottle of HCl. It might be the solvent, a protonated intermediate generated two steps earlier, or the substrate itself. Likewise the base might be a carboxylate, an amine, a halide, or just water.

"Find the acidic proton" and "find the basic site" are the two most-asked questions on this topic, and both are answered by a scan rather than by recall. Run it in this order.

  • O–H and N–H first. A carboxylic acid O–H is around pKa 5; a phenol around 10; an alcohol or water around 16; an amine N–H around 38. If a molecule has an O–H anywhere, that is almost certainly the answer.
  • Then C–H, but only two kinds. A hydrogen on the carbon next to a C=O sits near pKa 20 for a ketone, higher for an ester or acid; a terminal alkyne C–H sits at 25. Everything else on carbon is pKa 44 to 50 and is not an acid in any useful sense.
  • For the basic site, look for the most available lone pair. A negative charge beats a neutral atom. Among neutral atoms, nitrogen beats oxygen — nitrogen is less electronegative, so it holds its pair more loosely and hands it over more willingly.

Run that on 4-aminobutan-1-ol, H₂N–CH₂CH₂CH₂–CH₂OH, and the two answers land on opposite ends of the molecule. Most acidic proton: the O–H, pKa about 16 — it beats the N–H at 38 by twenty-two orders of magnitude, and the C–H bonds at roughly 50 are not in the running. Most basic site: the nitrogen lone pair, not the oxygen. Both atoms carry lone pairs, but nitrogen's is held less tightly; protonate this molecule with one equivalent of HCl and the proton goes to nitrogen every time, giving the ammonium salt and leaving the alcohol untouched.

Notice that the answers are on different atoms, and that the molecule is neither "an acid" nor "a base" in the abstract. It is both, and which role it plays depends entirely on what else is in the flask. Species that can go either way are amphoteric, and the Conjugate acids/bases section takes that idea up properly.

The acid is often a cation

Students learn to look for a bottled acid and then cannot find one in a mechanism that plainly has an acidic step. The reason is usually that the acid is a cation made two steps earlier. Protonating a neutral oxygen or nitrogen produces a species with a full positive charge, and a positive charge is enormously acid-strengthening: losing the proton neutralizes it, so the proton leaves readily.

Three you will meet constantly: hydronium, H₃O⁺ (pKa −1.7), which is what "aqueous acid" actually means; a protonated alcohol, R–OH₂⁺ (pKa about −2), which is the species that lets water leave as a neutral molecule in substitution and elimination; and a protonated carbonyl, C=OH⁺ (pKa about −7), which is how acid catalysis makes a carbonyl carbon more electrophilic. Compare the first two with their neutral parents — water at 15.7, an alcohol at 16 — and the cost of the charge is about seventeen or eighteen pKa units in each case. The ketone has no neutral O–H to compare with at all, which is the point: protonation invents an acidic hydrogen where there was none.

Three traps worth naming before you meet them. Concentrated is not strong. Strength is a pKa — a property of the molecule; concentration is how much of it is in the bottle. Glacial acetic acid is concentrated and weak; dilute HCl is dilute and strong. Draw the arrow from the base, not from the hydrogen. The arrow tail always sits on the electrons that move, and the electrons that form the new bond belong to the base's lone pair — an arrow starting at the H says the hydrogen supplied the electrons, which is exactly backwards. The most electronegative atom does not always carry the most acidic hydrogen. It holds across a row, so O–H beats N–H beats C–H; it fails down a column, where HI beats HF by thirteen orders of magnitude. The Factors affecting acidity section takes that apart.

What carries forward

The Brønsted framework is the basis of the pKa scale, which is the most quantitative tool in the course and which sets leaving-group ability, nucleophile strength, and whether a given base can deprotonate a given substrate. The next two sections build it: conjugate pairs first, then the number itself. The Lewis definition at the end of this chapter generalizes proton transfer to reactions with no proton at all. And in Carbonyl Chemistry through Enolate Chemistry, acid and base catalysis is what makes half the reactions in the module run at a useful rate.
