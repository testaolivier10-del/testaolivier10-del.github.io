The Brønsted-Lowry definition

A Brønsted-Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor. A Brønsted-Lowry base is a proton acceptor. Every acid-base reaction is fundamentally a proton transfer from the acid to the base — nothing more mysterious than that. This definition is broader than the older Arrhenius definition (acid = produces H⁺ in water, base = produces OH⁻ in water), which only works in aqueous solution; Brønsted-Lowry works in any solvent, or no solvent at all, which is essential since most organic reactions don't happen in water.

Worked example: HCl + NH₃

HCl donates its proton to the lone pair on nitrogen in NH₃. HCl is the acid (proton donor); NH₃ is the base (proton acceptor, using its lone pair). The products are NH₄⁺ (ammonium) and Cl⁻ (chloride).

Curved arrows describe this exactly like Module 2 taught: one arrow from nitrogen's lone pair to the H, and one arrow from the H–Cl bond to Cl. The base's lone pair is always the nucleophile here, and the acidic proton is always the electrophile — Brønsted acid-base chemistry is just a specific, extremely common case of the nucleophile/electrophile framework from Module 2.

Which side does the equilibrium favor?

Every proton transfer is technically an equilibrium (shown with two arrows, ⇌), even when it looks like it goes to completion. The reaction favors the side with the weaker acid and weaker base — because that's the lower-energy, more stable side. You'll quantify "weaker acid" precisely with pKa in the next lesson.
