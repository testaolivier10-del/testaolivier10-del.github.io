What it is

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom pulls on the electrons in a bond. It increases up and to the right on the periodic table (excluding noble gases) — fluorine is the most electronegative element there is. In any bond between two different atoms, the shared electrons sit closer to whichever atom is more electronegative.

Worked comparisons

O vs. H: oxygen is more electronegative. C vs. H: carbon is more electronegative (though only slightly — this is why C–H bonds are treated as close to nonpolar). C vs. Cl: chlorine is more electronegative. Fluorine is the single most electronegative element on the table, full stop.

This is where "electron-poor" and "electron-rich" come from. An electronegativity difference makes a bond polar — and it's what makes the carbon in C–Br electron-poor enough for a nucleophile to attack, the exact idea behind SN1/SN2. C–F is more polar than C–Br because fluorine's electronegativity gap with carbon is bigger.

The Pauling scale and polarity thresholds

Key values: F 3.98, O 3.44, N 3.04, Cl 3.16, Br 2.96, C 2.55, I 2.66, H 2.20 (look up any element in the periodic table tool). Rule of thumb for the difference (ΔEN): <0.5 nonpolar covalent (C–H = 0.35); 0.5–1.7 polar covalent (C–O = 0.89, C–Cl = 0.61); >1.7 ionic (Na–Cl = 2.23). Nearly every organic bond falls in the covalent range.
