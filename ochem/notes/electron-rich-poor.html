How Reactions Happen · Section 39 of 121

Electron-rich vs. electron-poor atoms

Practice this — interactive lesson

Put ammonia and chloromethane in the same flask. You can predict what happens without knowing the name of the reaction. Ammonia’s nitrogen has a lone pair that it is not using. Chloromethane’s carbon is δ+, because chlorine pulls the C–Cl bonding pair toward itself. So the lone pair goes to the carbon and forms a new N–C bond, and chloride leaves with the old C–Cl pair.

That prediction came from a single question: where is the electron density, and where is it missing? Nucleophiles and Electrophiles looked at each half of a reaction on its own. This page puts the two halves together, so that you can scan any molecule and find both kinds of site.

One lens for everything

An electron-rich atom has electron density it can share, and it shows one of three signs: a lone pair, a negative charge or a π bond. An electron-rich atom tends to act as a nucleophile, a species that donates an electron pair to form a new bond.

An electron-poor atom is short of electron density, and it also shows one of three signs: a δ+ caused by a more electronegative neighbor, a positive charge, or an empty orbital. An electron-poor atom tends to act as an electrophile, a species that accepts an electron pair to form a new bond.

In every polar reaction step, the kind in which electrons move in pairs, a rich site meets a poor one. The curved arrow starts at the rich site and points at the poor one.

None of this is a new principle. It is the electronegativity and bond polarity of Foundations, used to predict what a molecule will do instead of only describing how it is built.

Do not confuse “electron-rich” with “negatively charged.” Many nucleophiles in this course are neutral: water, alcohols, amines, alkenes and aromatic rings (benzene and rings like it). A negative charge makes a nucleophile stronger, but a nucleophile does not need one. In the same way, most electrophilic carbons carry no formal charge. They are neutral atoms that a neighbor has made δ+.

Electron-poor is not enough

Compare chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl, with ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH. In both, the CH₂ carbon is bonded to a more electronegative atom, so in both it is δ+. Yet nucleophiles replace the chlorine of chloroethane, while the OH of ethanol stays put.

The difference lies in the old bond. A carbon that already has four bonds can take a new pair only if it gives up one of its bonds at the same time. In chloroethane, the C–Cl bond breaks, and chlorine leaves as chloride ion, Cl⁻, taking the bonding pair with it. A group that departs this way is a leaving group, as Electrophiles described. In ethanol, the group would have to leave as hydroxide ion, HO⁻, and hydroxide does not leave.

For now, a short list is enough. Chlorine, bromine and iodine on carbon leave as halide ions. Hydroxide (HO⁻), alkoxides (RO⁻, the group an ether or an alcohol would have to lose) and the amide ion (H₂N⁻) do not. Leaving groups, the next page, explains why.

A carbon with a π bond has a second way to take a pair. In acetaldehyde, CH₃CHO, the C=O carbon is δ+. When a nucleophile bonds to it, the C=O π bond breaks and its pair moves onto oxygen. The carbon still ends up with four bonds, and nothing has to leave.

So an electron-poor carbon is a reaction site only if it passes a second test. It must hold a leaving group, or a π bond whose pair can move onto a more electronegative atom.

Scanning a whole molecule

Make this the first thing you do with any unfamiliar molecule. Find the lone pairs, negative charges and π bonds: these are the rich sites. Find the δ+ atoms, positive charges and empty orbitals: these are the poor sites. Then check which poor sites also have a way to take a pair, through a leaving group or a π bond. The scan takes under a minute, and it predicts the first step of most reactions in the course, including ones you have not met yet.
Worked example — which carbon does a nucleophile attack?

Take 1-chloro-2-ethoxyethane, CH₃–CH₂–O–CH₂–CH₂–Cl. Walk along the chain and ask the two questions at every atom. Is it electron-poor? And does it have a way to take a new pair?

The CH₃ on the left. It is bonded only to carbon and hydrogen, so nothing makes it δ+. It is not a site.

The oxygen. It has two lone pairs and is δ−. It is electron-rich, so it could act as a nucleophile itself, but it is not a target.

The two CH₂ groups bonded to oxygen. Oxygen pulls on both, so both are δ+. But the group either one would have to lose is an alkoxide, and alkoxides do not leave. Neither is a site.

The CH₂ bonded to chlorine. Chlorine makes it δ+, and chloride can leave. This carbon passes both tests, so this is where a nucleophile attacks.

Notice what the answer does not rest on. You never had to decide whether the carbon next to chlorine is more δ+ than the carbons next to oxygen. Oxygen is the more electronegative atom (3.44 against 3.16 for chlorine), but electronegativity alone cannot settle that comparison, because bond length matters too, as Bond polarity showed. The second test decides the site.

Exams like to blur two different questions: “which atom is attacked?” and “which atom is most electron-poor?” The reaction answers the first one.

Worked example — a nucleophile and an electrophile in one molecule

Now scan 5-aminopentan-2-one, H₂N–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CO–CH₃. This molecule has a rich site and a poor site of its own. Its carbons are numbered from the CH₃ next to the C=O, so that CH₃ is C1, the C=O carbon is C2, and the nitrogen sits on C5.

The nitrogen. It has a lone pair, and no π bond sits next to it to spread that pair out. It is electron-rich, and it is the molecule’s nucleophile.

C5, the carbon bonded to nitrogen. Nitrogen makes it slightly δ+ by induction. But it could react only by losing H₂N⁻, one of the worst leaving groups there is. A δ+ carbon with no way to take a pair is not a reaction site.

C4, C3 and C1. Each of these carbons is at least two bonds from the nitrogen and from the oxygen, so the pull on them has almost faded, and none of them carries anything that can leave. None is a site. (The hydrogens on C3 and C1, the carbons next to the C=O, are slightly acidic, but that story belongs to Enolate Chemistry.)

C2, the carbonyl carbon. The C=O makes it strongly δ+, and it needs no leaving group, because its π pair can move onto oxygen. This is the molecule’s electrophile.

The carbonyl oxygen. It has two lone pairs and is δ−, so it is electron-rich too. But a small, very electronegative atom holds its pairs tightly, so it is a much weaker nucleophile than the nitrogen. What it does readily is pick up a proton, which is why acids speed up so many carbonyl reactions. Rich and poor are the first cut, then: among the rich atoms, the ones that hold their pairs loosely react first.

The verdict. The nitrogen is the nucleophile, C2 is the electrophile, and nothing in the molecule can leave. Because both sites are in the same molecule, the chain can curl round until the nitrogen reaches C2. The new N–C2 bond then closes a ring of five atoms: N, C5, C4, C3 and C2. Five-membered rings close easily. With one CH₂ fewer, the chain would have to close a four-membered ring, which is strained and forms far less easily.

You are not expected to know the final product. You are expected to say which two atoms bond.

Induction: a pull through σ bonds

Look at 1-chloropropane, Cl–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃. Chlorine pulls the C–Cl bonding pair toward itself, so C1 is δ+. C1 in turn pulls a little on its bond to C2, so C2 is slightly δ+ as well. By C3 almost nothing is left.

This pull, passed along the σ bonds, is induction, the same effect that Factors affecting acidity used to rank acids. Electronegativity drives it. It needs no curved arrows and no new structure, because it only tilts the pairs in the bonds that are already there. And it weakens quickly with distance, fading out within two or three bonds.

The acids show the fade in numbers. Chloroacetic acid, ClCH₂COOH, is about 80 times more acidic than acetic acid (pKa 2.86 against 4.76). Move the chlorine three carbons away from the acid group, as in 4-chlorobutanoic acid, and the pKa is 4.52, almost back to acetic acid’s.

Resonance can reach further than you would expect

Now take propenal, H₂C=CH–CHO, an aldehyde whose C=O is joined directly to a C=C. The carbon bonded to the C=O carbon is the alpha (α) carbon. The next one along, at the far end of the C=C, is the beta (β) carbon. A compound built this way is an α,β-unsaturated aldehyde.

Push the arrows. The C=O π pair moves onto oxygen, and the C=C π pair moves over to form a new π bond between the α carbon and the C=O carbon. The result is a resonance structure with a − on oxygen and a + on the β carbon.

The β carbon is three bonds from the oxygen and has no electronegative neighbor of its own, so induction cannot reach it. Resonance can. That resonance structure is a real part of the hybrid, and it makes a real prediction: nucleophiles attack the β carbon. This is conjugate addition, also called 1,4-addition. When the nucleophile is an enolate, the reaction is the Michael reaction of Enolate Chemistry, one of the most important ways to make carbon–carbon bonds.

In general, resonance moves electron pairs through a conjugated system, an unbroken chain of overlapping p orbitals, as Resonance showed. It is drawn with curved arrows, and it gives new resonance structures. It barely weakens along the conjugated path. It stops abruptly where the conjugation ends, at the first carbon with four single bonds (an sp³ carbon), because that carbon has no p orbital to pass the pair along.

When induction and resonance disagree

Aniline is a benzene ring carrying an NH₂ group, where benzene is the six-carbon ring drawn with three alternating double bonds. Nitrogen is more electronegative than carbon, so by induction it pulls a little density out of the ring through the C–N σ bond.

But nitrogen’s lone pair sits right next to the ring’s π bonds, so it can also push density into the ring by resonance. Push the arrows and you get a resonance structure with a + on nitrogen and a − on a ring carbon beside the one that carries the nitrogen (the ortho carbon).

The resonance push is much larger than the inductive pull. The ring ends up richer in electrons than benzene, and aniline reacts with electrophiles far faster than benzene does. This one comparison drives the directing effects in Aromatic Chemistry.

Resonance usually wins because it moves a whole electron pair onto another atom, at least in one resonance structure, and the real molecule is partly that structure. Induction only tilts a bonding pair a little toward one end and never moves it anywhere. A whole pair shifted beats a fraction of one pair tilted. The table sums up the two effects.

What carries forward

This lens is the working method for the rest of the course. It tells you which carbon is attacked in Substitution & Elimination, why the carbonyl carbon is the target in Carbonyl Chemistry, which position on a ring reacts in Aromatic Chemistry, and which proton is removed in Enolate Chemistry. When a later page says a site is “activated” or “deactivated,” it is talking about electron density, and the explanation will always turn out to be induction, resonance, or both.
