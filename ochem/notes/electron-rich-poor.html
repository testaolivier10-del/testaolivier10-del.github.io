One lens for everything

An electron-rich atom has extra electron density (a lone pair, negative charge, or π bond) and acts as a nucleophile. An electron-poor atom has a deficit (a positive charge, a partial positive from a nearby electronegative atom, or an empty orbital) and acts as an electrophile. This is the same idea as electronegativity and bond polarity, applied to predict reactivity.

Worked examples

CH₃–Cl: chlorine's electronegativity pulls density away from carbon, leaving carbon electron-poor. :NH₃: nitrogen's lone pair makes it electron-rich, exactly why ammonia is a nucleophile.

Resonance can reach further than you'd expect

In H₂C=CH–CHO (an α,β-unsaturated aldehyde), the carbonyl pulls electron density through the whole conjugated π system, making even the far (β) carbon electron-poor and electrophilic — nucleophiles can attack there too (conjugate addition, more in Module 11).

Whenever you meet a new molecule, ask "where is the electron density, and where isn't it?" first — it tells you where a reaction will start. In CH₃–CH₂–O–CH₂–CH₂–Cl, the carbon attached to Cl is the most electron-poor atom (and the one a nucleophile would attack) because chlorine pulls density directly off it.

Induction vs. resonance

Induction: through sigma bonds, driven by electronegativity, no curved arrows, weakens quickly with distance. Resonance: through overlapping p orbitals in a conjugated system, drawn with curved arrows, barely weakens across the conjugated path — this is how a carbonyl reaches all the way to a β-carbon.
