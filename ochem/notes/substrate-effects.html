Step 1: substrate class rules mechanisms in or out

Methyl and primary substrates: SN2 only (too little steric bulk to matter, and can't form a stable primary carbocation for SN1/E1). Tertiary substrates: SN2 is blocked entirely (too crowded for backside attack) — only SN1/E1 are possible, since a tertiary carbocation is easily accessible. Secondary substrates are the genuinely ambiguous case: sterically possible for SN2, but also capable of forming a (moderately stable) secondary carbocation for SN1/E1 — the decision comes down to the nucleophile/base and solvent.

Step 2: nucleophile/base strength decides bimolecular vs. unimolecular

A strong nucleophile/base (like OH⁻, OR⁻, CN⁻) forces a bimolecular pathway (SN2 or E2) whenever sterically possible — it doesn't wait around for a slow ionization. A weak nucleophile/base (like H₂O, ROH) can't force a bimolecular reaction efficiently, so the reaction proceeds unimolecularly (SN1/E1) whenever the substrate can ionize at all.

Steric bulk in the base (like tert-butoxide) pushes secondary and tertiary substrates toward elimination over substitution, even when the base is technically strong — a bulky base has trouble threading into a crowded carbon for SN2, but can still reach an exposed, peripheral beta-hydrogen for E2.

Step 3: solvent tips substitution vs. elimination, and reinforces the mechanism

The split that matters is whether the solvent has an O–H or N–H bond to hydrogen-bond with (protic) or not (aprotic) — see the Nucleophiles lesson, Module 2. Polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, acetone, DMF) have no such bond, so they leave a strong nucleophile/base unsolvated and highly reactive, reinforcing SN2/E2. Polar protic solvents (water, alcohols) solvate and stabilize cations and anions through hydrogen bonding, reinforcing SN1/E1, and also stabilizing a strong nucleophile enough to weaken it into behaving more like a base (favoring E2 over SN2 in borderline cases). Heat favors elimination over substitution across the board (E1 over SN1, E2 over SN2) because elimination has a more favorable entropy change, forming two smaller molecules from one.

Quick reference table

Methyl/primary + strong Nu → SN2. Methyl/primary + strong bulky base → E2 (rare but possible with very bulky bases on primary substrates with beta-branching). Secondary + strong small Nu/base, aprotic → SN2/E2 mix (favors substitution if unhindered, elimination if branched or heated). Secondary or tertiary + weak Nu/base, protic solvent → SN1/E1 mixture. Tertiary + strong base → E2 exclusively (SN2 impossible).
