Substitution & Elimination · Section 48 of 121

Substrate & solvent effects

Practice this — interactive lesson

You now have four mechanisms that compete for the same substrates. This section is the decision procedure: given a substrate, a reagent and a solvent, work out which of SN1, SN2, E1 and E2 you actually get. It is the most practically tested skill in the course, and it is reliable if you take the checks in order.

Step 1: substrate class rules mechanisms in or out

This first check eliminates more possibilities than any other, so do it first.

Methyl and primary. SN2 only. There is too little steric bulk to block backside attack, and a primary carbocation is too unstable to form, which rules out both SN1 and E1. The one exception is a strong bulky base, which can force E2 even on a primary substrate.

Tertiary. SN2 is impossible — the backside is completely blocked. What remains is SN1 and E1 with weak reagents, or E2 with a strong base.

Secondary. The genuinely ambiguous case, where all four are possible in principle and the reagent and solvent decide. Most exam questions live here.

Two special classes override the general rule. Allylic and benzylic substrates are fast by every pathway — their cations are resonance-stabilized, and their SN2 transition states are also stabilized by the adjacent pi system. Vinyl and aryl halides do none of these reactions at all: SN2 is blocked by the sp² geometry, and vinyl and aryl cations are prohibitively unstable.

Step 2: the reagent decides bimolecular versus unimolecular

A strong nucleophile or base — hydroxide, alkoxide, cyanide, azide, thiolate, acetylide, amide — forces a bimolecular pathway, SN2 or E2, whenever the substrate permits it. It does not wait around for a slow ionization.

A weak nucleophile or base — water, alcohols, carboxylic acids — cannot force a bimolecular reaction at a useful rate, so the reaction proceeds unimolecularly through whatever cation the substrate can form. Weak reagent plus ionizable substrate means SN1 and E1 together.

Having settled bimolecular versus unimolecular, decide substitution versus elimination by asking whether the reagent is better as a nucleophile or as a base.

Two names in that table are worth a clause each. DBU is an amidine — a nitrogen base whose cation is resonance-stabilized, which makes it strong, while the two fused rings around the reacting nitrogen make it almost useless as a nucleophile. It is the reagent of choice when you want elimination and nothing else. And acetylide, ⁻C≡CR, is the anion left when a terminal alkyne is deprotonated; you met it as a conjugate base in Acids & Bases, and it is used as a reagent — a strong carbon nucleophile that builds carbon chains — in Alkenes & Alkynes. For now, just place it with the strong nucleophiles.

A bulky base such as tert-butoxide pushes secondary and tertiary substrates toward elimination even though it is technically a strong reagent: it cannot thread into a crowded carbon for SN2, but it can still reach an exposed peripheral beta hydrogen for E2. This is the clearest practical demonstration that nucleophilicity and basicity are different properties — the same molecule is excellent at one and poor at the other.

Step 3: solvent and temperature reinforce the call

The split that matters is protic versus aprotic — whether the solvent has an O–H or N–H available for hydrogen bonding.

Polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, acetone, acetonitrile) leave a strong nucleophile unsolvated and highly reactive, reinforcing SN2 and E2. Polar protic solvents (water, alcohols, acetic acid) stabilize both the developing cation and the departing anion, reinforcing SN1 and E1; they also cage a strong nucleophile enough to blunt it, which in borderline cases tips SN2 toward E2.

Heat favors elimination across the board — E1 over SN1, E2 over SN2 — because elimination forms two molecules from one and therefore gains entropy, and the TΔS contribution to ΔG grows with temperature. "Heat" or "reflux" in a set of conditions is a deliberate signal that elimination is intended.

Worked example 1 — 1-bromobutane + NaCN in DMSO

Substrate. Primary. That deletes SN1 and E1 outright — a primary cation does not form.

Reagent. Cyanide is a strong nucleophile and a weak base (HCN's pKa is about 9, so ⁻CN is a feeble base). Strong reagent means bimolecular; weak base means substitution rather than elimination.

Solvent. DMSO is polar aprotic: it leaves cyanide unsolvated and at its most reactive. Everything points the same way.

Products. Pentanenitrile, essentially exclusively. The competing product would be but-1-ene from E2, and it barely appears, because cyanide has no appetite for a proton.

Worked example 2 — 2-bromobutane + NaOEt in ethanol, heated

Substrate. Secondary — the ambiguous class. Nothing is deleted.

Reagent. Ethoxide is strong on both counts: a good nucleophile and a strong base (ethanol's pKa is 16). Bimolecular for certain, but SN2 and E2 both remain live.

Solvent and temperature. Ethanol is protic, which blunts the nucleophile a little; heat favors elimination. Both tip the same way.

Products, major and minor. Elimination wins: but-2-ene, the Zaitsev alkene, and mostly its E isomer, since the trans double bond is the less crowded one. The minor elimination product is but-1-ene, from the other beta carbon. And there is a third product in the flask that questions often forget: sec-butyl ethyl ether, from the SN2 that did not quite lose. A secondary substrate with a strong base gives a mixture; naming only the major product is a partial answer.

Worked example 3 — 2-bromo-2-methylpropane in aqueous ethanol, warm

Substrate. Tertiary. SN2 is impossible.

Reagent. Water and ethanol are weak as nucleophiles and weak as bases, so nothing forces a bimolecular step: this is a solvolysis, SN1 and E1 from the same cation.

Solvent and temperature. Protic, which is exactly what ionization wants; warmth adds elimination.

Products. tert-Butanol and tert-butyl ethyl ether from SN1 (two nucleophiles are present, so there are two substitution products), plus 2-methylpropene from E1. All three, always — that is the trouble with unimolecular conditions, and the reason nobody runs them for synthesis.

Worked example 4 — the borderline case: 2-bromobutane + NaN₃ in acetone versus in water

Same substrate, same class of reagent, different answer — and the only thing that moved was the solvent.

In acetone (polar aprotic). Azide is a strong nucleophile and a very weak base (HN₃'s pKa is about 4.6). Unsolvated, it attacks fast: clean SN2 to 2-azidobutane, with inversion at C2. Almost no alkene.

In water. Azide is now hydrogen-bonded and slowed, and water is itself an ionizing solvent, so the substrate's own slow ionization starts to compete. The product becomes a mixture: some inverted 2-azidobutane from SN2, some racemic 2-azidobutane and butan-2-ol from SN1, and a little but-2-ene. The honest answer to a borderline secondary question is "a mixture, and here is why each piece is there" — not one product.

What to say in an exam. Name the major pathway, name the competitor, and give the variable that decides between them. A question that gives you a secondary substrate is usually testing exactly that.

Worked example 5 — when the leaving group is the deciding variable

Leaving-group quality is one of the four variables and almost never the one an exam changes, which is why it is worth seeing once on its own. Take butan-2-yl chloride and butan-2-yl tosylate, and warm each one in ethanol with no added reagent.

The substrate class is the same, the reagent is the same, the solvent is the same. The chloride barely moves: chloride is a mediocre leaving group, and with nothing to push it out the ionization is too slow to see. The tosylate solvolyzes smoothly, because ⁻OTs spreads its charge over three oxygens and is a far more stable anion. Product: sec-butyl ethyl ether, largely racemic, plus some butene — SN1 and E1.

The lesson is the one from the SN1 section: a leaving group that would be adequate when a strong nucleophile is shoving it out can be hopeless when it has to leave unassisted.

Worked example 6 — the substrate with no beta hydrogen

Treat neopentyl bromide, (CH₃)₃C–CH₂Br, with sodium ethoxide, and run the framework honestly.

Substrate. Primary, so SN1 and E1 are out. SN2 is allowed on paper, but the neighboring quaternary carbon blocks the backside approach and the rate is about 10⁵ times slower than ethyl bromide's.

What about E2? Find the beta carbon: there is exactly one, and it is the quaternary carbon, which carries three methyls and no hydrogen at all. E2 requires a beta hydrogen. There is none. E2 is not slow here; it is impossible.

So what happens? Very slow SN2, and not much else. Neopentyl is famous for being unreactive — not because anything unusual happens, but because its one available pathway is throttled and its obvious alternative does not exist. Count the beta hydrogens before you propose an elimination; a substrate with none cannot eliminate however strong the base is.

Do not skip to the reagent. The single most common error is reading "strong base" and answering E2 without first checking whether the substrate can do it, or reading "tertiary" and answering SN1 without noticing that the reagent is sodium ethoxide. Substrate first, reagent second, solvent third — every time, in that order.

Reading the evidence backwards

Exam questions often run the other way: here is what happened, which mechanism was it? The diagnostics are clean. A rate that depends on the nucleophile means bimolecular; a rate that does not means unimolecular. Clean inversion at a stereocenter means SN2; racemization means SN1. A rearranged carbon skeleton means a carbocation, so SN1 or E1. A product whose alkene geometry traces to the starting diastereomer means E2. These are the observations the mechanisms were deduced from in the first place.

Worked diagnosis. An optically active secondary bromide is warmed in methanol. Three observations come back: adding sodium azide, a far better nucleophile, does not speed the disappearance of the bromide up at all; the product is optically inactive; and a second, isomeric product has a carbon skeleton with a branch the starting material did not have. Take them in turn. A rate independent of the nucleophile means the nucleophile is not in the rate-determining step, so the mechanism is unimolecular. Loss of optical activity means the stereocenter passed through something flat. A rearranged skeleton means there was a real intermediate with time to shift a group. All three name the same thing: a carbocation, so SN1 — with the rearranged product coming from the same cation after a hydride or methyl shift. Any one observation alone would be suggestive; together they are conclusive, and that is how these questions are built.

What carries forward

This four-way framework is the model for every later competition in the course: 1,2- versus 1,4-addition in Conjugation & Pericyclic Reactions, substitution versus addition on aromatic rings in Aromatic Chemistry, and which carbonyl derivative reacts fastest in Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives. The habit of asking "what does the substrate allow, what does the reagent want, what does the solvent reward" transfers directly.
