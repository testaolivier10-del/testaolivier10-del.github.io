The Diels–Alder reaction

Practice this — interactive lesson

The Diels–Alder reaction builds a six-membered ring in one step, from two pieces, with no intermediate, no catalyst and complete control of the stereochemistry. It is the most useful single reaction in this course and one of the most useful in organic chemistry generally, and almost everything about it follows from one picture: six electrons moving round a circle.

What it is

A conjugated diene (four π electrons) reacts with a two-π-electron partner called the dienophile — literally "diene-loving". With an alkene dienophile the product is a cyclohexene; with an alkyne it is a cyclohexa-1,4-diene, because the alkyne's second π bond survives untouched into the ring. Because it joins a four-π-electron component to a two-π-electron one, it is classified as a [4+2] cycloaddition.

That word cycloaddition puts it in a family. A pericyclic reaction is one whose bonds break and form together in a single step, with the electrons travelling round a closed loop and no intermediate anywhere — which is what the chapter title is naming. Cycloadditions are one class; electrocyclic ring closures and sigmatropic shifts such as the Cope and Claisen rearrangements are the other two classes, and a dedicated pericyclic course is where you would meet them; this course teaches only the cycloaddition. The Diels–Alder is by some distance the most useful of them, and everything below is a consequence of that single-step requirement.

The arithmetic of the bonds explains why it is so favorable. Three π bonds go in; out come two new σ bonds and one π bond. σ bonds are stronger than π bonds, so trading two of the latter for two of the former releases energy, and the reaction is downhill without anything having to drive it.

It is concerted: every bond breaks and forms in the same step, through a single cyclic transition state, with no intermediate at all. There is no carbocation, so there are no rearrangements, and there is nothing whose stability you need to compare. Almost every special property below is a consequence of that one fact.

The diene must be able to reach s-cis

For the two ends of the diene to reach the two ends of the dienophile at the same time, they have to be on the same side — the diene must react from its s-cis conformation. This is the commonest reason a Diels–Alder simply does not happen, and it is a geometric requirement rather than an electronic one.

  • Cyclopentadiene is locked s-cis inside its ring and is so reactive that it dimerizes on standing at room temperature, one molecule acting as the diene and another as the dienophile. It is stored cold as the dimer and cracked by distillation when needed.
  • A diene locked s-trans in a ring cannot react at all, however electron-rich it is.
  • An open-chain diene can rotate into s-cis, but bulky groups at the inner positions that clash in that conformation slow it down badly.

Electronics: electron-rich diene, electron-poor dienophile

The reaction is fastest when the two partners are electronically mismatched in the right direction. The argument runs through the frontier orbitals introduced in Conjugated systems: the diene brings the electrons, so what matters is its HOMO, the highest orbital that has any; the dienophile receives them, so what matters is its LUMO, the lowest orbital that has room. Electron-donating groups on the diene push its HOMO up; electron-withdrawing groups on the dienophile pull its LUMO down. The closer those two orbitals come in energy, the more strongly they interact and the faster the reaction goes.

In practice this means a good dienophile carries a carbonyl, a nitrile, a nitro group or an ester next to its double bond. Maleic anhydride, with two carbonyls flanking the alkene, is the classic fast dienophile; ethene itself reacts only under forcing conditions because it has nothing pulling its LUMO down. A note on the names, in advance. Aldehydes, esters and anhydrides — and the …-carbaldehyde and …-dicarboxylate endings their products carry — belong to Carbonyl Chemistry and Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives. Every structure below is drawn beside its name, and the only property this section asks of any of them is that the C=O withdraws electrons.

This is also where a Lewis acid catalyst gets its effect. AlCl₃, ZnCl₂ or a similar acid binds the lone pair on the dienophile's carbonyl oxygen, which drains electron density out of the C=C and drops the LUMO further still. Rates commonly rise by orders of magnitude, the reaction can be run cold instead of hot, and the endo preference usually sharpens with it. No Diels–Alder needs a catalyst — the bond arithmetic already pays for the reaction — but a sluggish one is often rescued this way.

Regiochemistry: which way round the two partners join

As soon as both partners carry a substituent there is a second question the bond arithmetic cannot answer. The diene can approach the dienophile either way round, and the two approaches put the substituents in different places on the ring. One of them wins, heavily, and the reason is the same electronics as above seen one atom at a time.

You can settle it with resonance alone. Draw the structure that shows where each partner is most polarized, and then join the negative end of one to the positive end of the other — the two atoms with the biggest appetite for each other make one of the new bonds, and the second bond has nowhere else to go.

  • Donor on the diene's C1. Push the donor's electrons in and the negative charge comes out at C4, the far terminus.
  • Donor on the diene's C2. Do the same and the negative charge comes out at C1.
  • Withdrawing group on the dienophile. Push toward the withdrawing group and the positive charge appears on the far carbon of the double bond, the one the group is not attached to.

Joining those two atoms gives, every time, one of two outcomes, and organic chemists borrow benzene's vocabulary to name them:

  • A 1-substituted diene plus a monosubstituted dienophile gives the "ortho" product — the two substituents on adjacent ring carbons.
  • A 2-substituted diene plus a monosubstituted dienophile gives the "para" product — the two substituents 1,4 across the ring.
Three things to keep straight about these names. The product is a cyclohexene, not a benzene ring, so ortho and para here are only borrowed words for the 1,2 and 1,4 relationships — nothing is aromatic. There is no "meta" outcome: the rule produces one or the other and never the 1,3 arrangement, which is the fastest way to eliminate a wrong answer. And the preference is a preference, not a law: it is strong when the diene is well donor-substituted and the dienophile well acceptor-substituted, and it weakens as either partner becomes less polarized.
Worked example — placing the substituents

Take (E)-penta-1,3-diene (a 1-substituted diene, methyl on the terminus) with propenal, CH₂=CH–CHO.

Polarize the diene. The methyl is the donor end, so treat the far terminus — the CH₂ — as the nucleophilic carbon.

Polarize the dienophile. Push the alkene toward the aldehyde and the positive charge lands on the terminal CH₂, the carbon not carrying the CHO.

Join those two. The diene's CH₂ terminus bonds to the dienophile's CH₂, which forces the diene's methyl-bearing terminus to bond to the carbon carrying the CHO. Those two substituents are now on neighboring ring atoms: 2-methylcyclohex-3-ene-1-carbaldehyde, the "ortho" product.

Regiochemistry is only half of that answer. Ring carbons 1 and 2 of that product are adjacent stereocenters, and their relative configuration is fixed as well — by the endo preference in the next section. An (E)-1-substituted diene carries its methyl in the outward position of the s-cis conformation, and an outward substituent finishes up cis to whatever the dienophile tucks underneath, so the methyl and the aldehyde come out cis to each other. Read the full answer as cis-2-methylcyclohex-3-ene-1-carbaldehyde; a name with no stereochemistry in it has answered the easier question.

Now move the methyl to C2 of the diene — that is isoprene. Polarizing it makes C1 the nucleophilic terminus instead, so C1 takes the dienophile's CH₂ and C4 takes the CHO-bearing carbon. Count round the ring and the methyl and the aldehyde are now 1,4: 4-methylcyclohex-3-ene-1-carbaldehyde, the "para" product.

Stereochemistry, and why it is completely predictable

Because the reaction is concerted, the geometry of the starting materials is carried straight into the product. Three separate statements follow, and they are the reason the reaction is so heavily used in synthesis.

It is stereospecific with respect to the dienophile. Both new bonds form on the same face of the dienophile — the word for that is suprafacial, and it simply means "both bonds to one face" — so substituents that were cis across its double bond come out cis on the ring, and trans comes out trans. There is no step at which anything can rotate.

The diene's geometry is carried through too, and the addition is suprafacial on the diene as well. Draw the diene s-cis and look at its two terminal carbons: each carries one substituent pointing outward, away from the other end of the diene, and one pointing inward. Two substituents that both point outward end up cis to each other on the ring; one outward and one inward end up trans.

In practice that reduces to a rule you can apply from the name. An (E,E)-diene — (2E,4E)-hexa-2,4-diene, say — has both of its methyls outward in the s-cis conformation, so they come out cis on the ring. An (E,Z)-diene has one of each, so they come out trans. And because the dienophile's geometry is carried through at the same time, a single diene and a single dienophile give a single diastereomer of the product.

The endo product is favored. When the dienophile carries a substituent, the two partners can stack in two orientations: endo, with the dienophile's substituent tucked under the diene, or exo, with it pointing away. The endo arrangement is more crowded and is nevertheless the faster one, because the substituent's π system interacts favorably with the developing π system of the diene in the transition state — a secondary orbital interaction. Endo is therefore the kinetic product, which is worth noticing: it is a case where the kinetic product is the more hindered one, and the general rule from the last section still holds because nothing here is reversing at ordinary temperatures.

Worked example — predicting a product completely

React buta-1,3-diene with dimethyl maleate, in which the two ester groups are cis across the double bond.

Skeleton. [4+2] gives a cyclohexene. The new double bond sits between what were C2 and C3 of the diene, and the two new σ bonds join the diene's C1 and C4 to the two dienophile carbons.

Stereochemistry. The two esters were cis on the dienophile, and the addition is suprafacial on it, so they are cis on the ring — on adjacent carbons, on the same face.

Check by switching. Run the same reaction with dimethyl fumarate, the trans isomer, and the two esters come out trans on the ring. One change in the starting material, one predictable change in the product, and no mixture in either case.

Worked example — the canonical one: cyclopentadiene + maleic anhydride

This is the reaction endo/exo was invented to describe, because it is the one where the two orientations give genuinely different compounds.

Skeleton first. Cyclopentadiene is a diene locked s-cis, with a CH₂ bridging the two termini. Its C1 and C4 reach the two carbons of the anhydride's C=C, which closes a new six-membered ring — and because those two carbons were already tied together by the five-membered ring, the product is bicyclic: a norbornene, with the old CH₂ now a one-carbon bridge and the new C=C in a two-carbon bridge.

Dienophile stereochemistry. The anhydride holds its two carbonyls cis across the double bond and the addition is suprafacial, so they stay cis: both on the same face of the new ring, on adjacent carbons.

Now the endo/exo question. The two partners can stack with the anhydride ring pointing under the diene or pointing away from it. Endo — pointing under, so that in the product the anhydride ends up on the same side as the two-carbon C=C bridge and away from the CH₂ bridge — is the more crowded arrangement and the faster one.

Answer. The endo adduct, cis-bicyclo[2.2.1]hept-5-ene-2,3-dicarboxylic anhydride, formed essentially exclusively at room temperature.

The trap: reading "endo is favored" as "endo is more stable". It is not — exo is usually the more stable adduct. Endo wins on rate, and it keeps winning only because nothing here reverses at ordinary temperatures. Heat the same reaction hard enough and long enough and the exo adduct can take over.

Running it backwards

The reaction is reversible, and heating a cyclohexene of the right kind drives the retro-Diels–Alder, splitting it back into a diene and a dienophile. Cracking cyclopentadiene dimer before use is exactly this. It also matters in synthesis planning: seeing a cyclohexene ring and mentally cutting it apart at the retro-Diels–Alder disconnection is one of the most productive moves available when you are working a synthesis backwards.

Making that cut is mechanical once you know where to put the scissors. Find the ring's double bond. The two carbons next to it — one on each side — were the diene's C1 and C4, so the bonds you break are the two that run from those carbons to the next atoms round the ring. What falls out is a diene on one side and a two-carbon dienophile on the other.

Worked example — planning a ring backwards

Your target is 4-methylcyclohex-3-ene-1-carbaldehyde. What two pieces make it?

Find the alkene. It sits between C3 and C4, and C4 carries the methyl.

Place the cut. Step one carbon out from each end of the double bond — to C2 and to C5 — and break the two bonds that lead away from them round the ring, C2–C1 and C5–C6. The four atoms C2–C3=C4–C5 become the diene, and C6–C1 becomes the dienophile.

Restore the bonds. The diene fragment regains a double bond where each σ bond was broken, giving CH₂=C(CH₃)–CH=CH₂ — isoprene. The dienophile fragment regains its π bond, giving CH₂=CH–CHO — propenal.

Check the regiochemistry forwards. A 2-substituted diene plus a monosubstituted dienophile gives the "para" product, and the methyl and the aldehyde in the target are indeed 1,4. The disconnection is sound, and it builds the ring and places both substituents in one step.

What carries forward

Three things. The Diels–Alder is the first concerted reaction in this course that forms two bonds at once, and the reasoning — frontier orbitals, suprafacial addition, stereospecificity — is the template for pericyclic chemistry generally. It is the standard way to make a six-membered ring, which makes it a fixture of retrosynthesis. And the endo rule is a clean reminder that "more stable" and "forms faster" are separate questions, which is the lesson of the section before this one.
