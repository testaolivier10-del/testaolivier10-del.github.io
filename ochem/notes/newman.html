How to draw one

A Newman projection is a view straight down a specific C–C bond. The front carbon is drawn as a dot with three lines radiating out at 120° (its three other substituents). The back carbon is drawn as a circle, with three lines starting at the circle's edge (its three other substituents) — the bond connecting front and back carbons is the dot/circle itself, pointing straight at you, so it isn't drawn as a line.

Staggered vs. eclipsed

The angle between a front substituent and the nearest back substituent is the dihedral (torsion) angle. In the staggered conformation, front and back substituents are offset by 60° — as far apart as possible. In the eclipsed conformation, they line up directly (0° apart), overlapping in the drawing.

Eclipsed conformations are higher in energy than staggered ones — this is torsional strain, caused by repulsion between the electron clouds of eclipsing bonds. For ethane, rotating from staggered to eclipsed costs about 2.9 kcal/mol (roughly 1 kcal/mol per eclipsing H–H interaction, and there are three). This small barrier is why ethane's C–C bond rotates extremely fast at room temperature — staggered and eclipsed aren't different, isolable molecules, just different snapshots along one continuous rotation.

Butane: four named conformations

For butane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃), look down the central C2–C3 bond with a methyl group front and back. As you rotate through 360°, you pass through: anti (methyls 180° apart, staggered, lowest energy, defined as 0 kcal/mol), gauche (methyls 60° apart, staggered, but the two methyls are close enough to clash sterically, costing about +0.9 kcal/mol), eclipsed (methyl eclipsing a hydrogen, about +3.6 kcal/mol), and totally eclipsed / syn (the two methyls directly eclipsing each other, the highest point, about +4.5 to +6 kcal/mol). There are two energetically-equal gauche conformations and two equal (methyl-H) eclipsed conformations by symmetry.
