Alkanes & Conformations · Section 27 of 117

Newman projections

Practice this — interactive lesson

Single bonds rotate freely — that was established in Foundations, when sigma bonds turned out to be cylindrically symmetric. But "freely" does not mean "without preference." As a molecule rotates about a C–C bond it passes through arrangements of noticeably different energy, and it spends most of its time in the low ones. This chapter is about which arrangements those are, and it starts with the drawing that makes them visible.

What a conformation is

A conformation is a spatial arrangement of a molecule reachable from another by rotation about single bonds alone, with no bonds broken. Different conformations of one molecule are not different compounds; they interconvert tens of billions of times per second at room temperature and cannot be separated in a bottle.

This distinguishes conformations from the isomers of Stereochemistry, which require bond breaking to interconvert and genuinely are different substances. Keeping the distinction sharp saves a lot of confusion later.

How to draw a Newman projection

A Newman projection is a view straight down one specific C–C bond, chosen because it is the rotation you care about. The front carbon is a dot with three lines radiating outward at 120°, showing its other three substituents. The back carbon is a circle, with three lines starting at the circle's edge. The bond joining front and back points directly at your eye, so it is not drawn at all — the dot and the circle are its two ends.

Practical advice: identify the bond you are looking down before you draw anything, and label the front and back atoms on the original structure. Most Newman errors are not drawing errors but bookkeeping errors — a substituent placed on the wrong carbon.

Staggered versus eclipsed

The angle between a front substituent and the nearest back substituent is the dihedral or torsion angle. In a staggered conformation, front and back substituents are offset by 60°, as far apart as they can get. In an eclipsed conformation they line up at 0°, overlapping in the drawing (conventionally drawn slightly offset so both are visible). That convention catches people out: a drawing whose front and back bonds are a few degrees apart is showing you a 0° eclipse, not a near-staggered conformer. If the back bonds peek out from behind the front ones by a hair, it is eclipsed; a genuine stagger puts them squarely in the middle of the gaps.

Eclipsed conformations are higher in energy than staggered ones. This is torsional strain, and while it is often described as electron-cloud repulsion between eclipsing bonds, the better modern explanation is hyperconjugation. Every sigma bond comes with a partner orbital it does not use: filling the bonding combination of two atomic orbitals leaves the out-of-phase combination empty, and that empty one is the antibonding orbital, written sigma* and always higher in energy than the bond itself. In the staggered arrangement each filled C–H sigma orbital lines up with an empty C–H sigma* orbital on the neighboring carbon, so a little electron density leaks into it and the molecule is stabilized. Eclipsing throws that alignment away. For ethane the barrier is about 2.9 kcal/mol — roughly 1 kcal per eclipsing H–H pair, and there are three.

Put 2.9 kcal/mol in perspective: it is small enough that ethane rotates some tens of billions of times a second at room temperature, so staggered and eclipsed ethane are not isolable substances but snapshots along one continuous motion. It is nonetheless large enough to be measured, and large enough that at any instant the great majority of molecules are near a staggered arrangement.

Butane: four named conformations

Ethane is symmetric enough that all three staggered forms are identical. Butane is where conformational analysis gets interesting. Look down the central C2–C3 bond, with a methyl group on the front carbon and another on the back. Rotating through a full 360° passes through four distinguishable arrangements.

ConformationDihedralTypeEnergy
anti180°staggered0 (reference)
gauche60°staggered+0.9
eclipsed (Me/H)120°eclipsed+3.6
syn (Me/Me)0°eclipsed+4.5 to 6

By symmetry there are two equivalent gauche conformations and two equivalent methyl/hydrogen eclipsed ones. The anti conformation is the global minimum, and butane spends roughly 70% of its time there at room temperature, with most of the rest in the two gauche forms.

The gauche penalty of 0.9 kcal/mol is worth remembering by name. It is steric strain, not torsional strain: the two methyl groups are staggered, so there is no eclipsing problem, but at 60° they are close enough to bump into each other. Steric strain and torsional strain are separate effects with separate causes, and the gauche conformation is the cleanest place to see one without the other.

Anti and gauche are not words about methyl groups specifically. They describe the dihedral angle between whichever two groups you have nominated as the reference pair — conventionally the two largest — so 1,2-dibromoethane has an anti conformer (Br 180° from Br) and two gauche ones exactly as butane does, and a molecule carrying four substituents can still be described this way once you say which pair you are tracking.

Estimating any conformer: the per-interaction values

The four butane numbers are not four separate facts to memorize. They are sums of a small set of interaction costs, and knowing the costs lets you estimate the energy of a conformer you have never seen — which is what an exam question asking "how much strain is in this drawing" is really testing.

InteractionKindCost (kcal/mol)
H/H eclipsedtorsional1.0
H/CH₃ eclipsedtorsional + steric1.4
CH₃/CH₃ eclipsedtorsional + steric2.5–3
CH₃/CH₃ gauchesteric only0.9

Check the arithmetic against the table above. Butane’s methyl/hydrogen eclipsed form has one CH₃/H eclipse and two H/H eclipses: 1.4 + 1.0 + 1.0 = 3.4, against the measured 3.6. The syn form has one CH₃/CH₃ eclipse and two H/H: about 2.5–3 + 1.0 + 1.0, which is the 4.5–6 range quoted. Ethane is three H/H eclipses, 3 × 1.0 ≈ 2.9. The scheme is approximate and it is meant to be — it gets you to within a few tenths on anything you can draw.

One thing the table makes explicit that the butane totals hide: an eclipsed CH₃/CH₃ pair costs far more than three times an H/H pair, and the excess is steric strain. Steric strain does not switch off when the bonds eclipse — eclipsed conformers carry it on top of the torsional cost, which is exactly why the syn conformer is the highest point on the curve and not merely another eclipse.

Worked example — turning a structure into a Newman projection

Draw 2-methylbutane as a Newman projection down the C2–C3 bond, then find its best conformer.

Step 1 — name the two carbons. The skeleton is CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃. C2 is the branch carbon; C3 is the CH₂ next to it. Mark them on the structure before you draw anything, because a substituent put on the wrong carbon is the commonest error in the whole topic.

Step 2 — list what each carbon carries besides the bond you are sighting down. C2 (front) carries two CH₃ groups and one H. C3 (back) carries one CH₃ and two H.

Step 3 — draw. Front carbon: a dot with three spokes at 120°, labeled CH₃, CH₃, H. Back carbon: a circle with three spokes from the rim, labeled CH₃, H, H, offset 60° from the front set so the drawing starts staggered.

Step 4 — rotate the back carbon and name what you get. Turn it 60° and every back bond lands on a front bond: an eclipsed conformer, with one CH₃/CH₃ eclipse among the three. Turn it another 60° and it is staggered again, with the back methyl now next to the front carbon’s hydrogen instead.

Step 5 — pick the winner by counting. Ignore the eclipsed forms; the answer is always one of the staggered ones. Count gauche CH₃/CH₃ pairs in each. The staggered conformer that puts the back methyl between the two front methyls has two of them (1.8 kcal/mol); the other two staggered conformers each have one (0.9). So 2-methylbutane has two equally-favored best conformers, each carrying a single gauche interaction, and — unlike butane — no conformer that escapes gauche strain entirely.

The reverse direction is the same bookkeeping run backwards: read the front carbon’s three groups and the back carbon’s three, write them as two connected carbons, and the Newman has told you the whole structure.

Worked example — reading an energy diagram

Plot butane's energy against dihedral angle and you get a curve with three minima and three maxima over 360°.

The minima are at 60° (gauche), 180° (anti) and 300° (the other gauche). The maxima are at 0° (syn, the highest), 120° and 240° (methyl/hydrogen eclipsed).

The difference between the highest maximum and the lowest minimum — about 5 kcal/mol — is the rotational barrier. Small enough to be crossed constantly at room temperature; large enough that the anti form is genuinely preferred.

Gauche is staggered. The gauche conformation is a local energy minimum, not a maximum, and it is a perfectly normal shape for a molecule to adopt — about a quarter of butane molecules are gauche at any moment. People often assume "gauche is bad" and confuse it with an eclipsed arrangement. It costs 0.9 kcal/mol relative to anti, which is real but modest.

What carries forward

Conformational preferences look like a detail and are not: they decide reaction outcomes. Enzymes bind one particular conformation of their substrate. Torsional strain and steric strain, introduced here in their simplest setting, are two of the three contributions to ring strain in the next section, and the gauche interaction reappears on a cyclohexane ring as the 1,3-diaxial interaction — the same clash in a different geometry.

The one that pays off furthest away is anti-periplanar, the name for butane's most comfortable arrangement. E2 elimination demands that the leaving group and the departing hydrogen sit at exactly that 180° angle, so a molecule can only eliminate through conformations that make the geometry available — which is how a drawing exercise from this section turns into a prediction about a reaction in Substitution & Elimination.
