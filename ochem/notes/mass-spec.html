Ionization and the molecular ion

A mass spectrometer bombards a vaporized sample with high-energy electrons, knocking out one electron from a molecule to form a radical cation, M⁺• (called the molecular ion). This ion's mass-to-charge ratio (m/z, effectively its molecular weight, since it carries a single positive charge) appears as the highest-mass peak in the spectrum (barring rare, low-intensity isotope peaks) — giving you the molecule's exact molecular weight directly, something neither IR nor routine NMR provides on its own.

Fragmentation: the molecular ion breaks apart in predictable ways

The molecular ion carries substantial excess energy from ionization and often fragments into smaller pieces before ever reaching the detector — a neutral fragment is lost (undetected) and a smaller cation is detected at a correspondingly lower m/z. Fragmentation isn't random: bonds break preferentially at points that produce a more stable resulting cation, exactly the same stability logic from every carbocation-forming reaction across the course (SN1, E1, Friedel-Crafts alkylation).

A mass spectrum is really a map of a molecule's weak points — the bonds most prone to fragmenting are the ones that produce the most stable cation or that break next to a resonance- or hyperconjugation-stabilizing group, so reading a fragmentation pattern is fundamentally an exercise in the same stability reasoning used everywhere else in this course.

Alpha cleavage: fragmentation next to a heteroatom or carbonyl

A bond adjacent to (alpha to) an oxygen, nitrogen, or carbonyl breaks preferentially, because the resulting cation is resonance-stabilized by the adjacent lone pair — directly analogous to the oxocarbenium ion (Acetals lesson) or the acylium ion (EAS lesson). For a ketone like 2-butanone (CH₃COCH₂CH₃), alpha cleavage can lose either a methyl radical (giving a resonance-stabilized acylium-like fragment at m/z = M−15) or an ethyl radical (giving the other acylium-like fragment at m/z = M−29) — and the more substituted, more stable resulting cation dominates, exactly the same "which cation forms preferentially" logic from Markovnikov addition.

Isotope patterns: chlorine and bromine leave a fingerprint

Most elements in organic molecules (C, H, N, O) have one overwhelmingly dominant isotope, so their molecular ion peak has only a small, easily-overlooked M+1 peak (from natural ¹³C abundance, useful mainly for confirming carbon count). Chlorine and bromine are dramatically different: chlorine is roughly 3:1 (³⁵Cl:³⁷Cl) and bromine is roughly 1:1 (⁷⁹Br:⁸¹Br) between their two major isotopes, both of which differ by 2 mass units. This produces an unmistakable, easily recognized M and M+2 peak pair in a roughly 3:1 ratio (chlorine) or roughly 1:1 ratio (bromine) — a direct, unambiguous signature that lets you identify the presence (and count) of chlorine or bromine atoms from the spectrum alone, without needing any other spectroscopic technique.
