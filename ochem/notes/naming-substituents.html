IUPAC Nomenclature · Section 15 of 119

Substituents & locants

Practice this — interactive lesson

With the parent chain found and numbered, everything hanging off it has to be named and placed. That is the prefix, and it is where most of the fiddly rules live — not because the chemistry is hard, but because the conventions are arbitrary and have to be applied in a fixed order.

Alkyl groups: an alkane minus one hydrogen

A carbon branch is named by taking the root for its length and adding -yl. One carbon is methyl, two is ethyl, three is propyl, four is butyl, and so on up the same list of roots. The suffix -yl means "this is attached to something else rather than standing alone", so methane is a molecule and methyl is a piece of one.

From three carbons up, a branch can attach through different carbons, and those are genuinely different groups with different names:

propyl attaches through an end carbon · isopropyl (systematically propan-2-yl) attaches through the middle one · butyl through an end · sec-butyl (butan-2-yl) through the second carbon of a four-chain · isobutyl (2-methylpropyl) is a three-chain with a methyl, attached at the end · tert-butyl (tert-butyl, systematically 2-methylpropan-2-yl) attaches through a carbon bearing three methyls · neopentyl (2,2-dimethylpropyl) is a CH₂ carrying a carbon with three methyls — five carbons, attached at the end

The systematic names in italics are what current IUPAC prefers and what you should be able to decode; the common names are what almost every lecturer, paper and exam actually says. tert-Butyl in particular is universal — nobody says 2-methylpropan-2-yl out loud. Learn both directions and use whichever the question uses.

Halogens and other simple prefixes

Halogens are named as prefixes, always: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo. They never become a suffix and never outrank anything — but nothing outranks them either. For numbering, a halogen and an alkyl group have equal standing and compete on first-point-of-difference terms like any other pair of prefixes, and on an exact tie the alphabet decides between them. That makes them the easiest substituent in the language: a chlorine is a chloro wherever it sits, and its only job is to collect a locant.

Simple halides also carry common names built from the alkyl group, and the substitution and elimination chapters use them constantly: isopropyl bromide is 2-bromopropane, tert-butyl chloride is 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, ethyl iodide is iodoethane. Read them as "alkyl group, then halide" and you can convert in either direction.

Two others behave the same way and are worth meeting now, because they turn up constantly and are never suffixes: nitro (–NO₂) and alkoxy (–OR, so –OCH₃ is methoxy and –OCH₂CH₃ is ethoxy). An ether has no suffix of its own in substitutive nomenclature; it is always named as an alkoxy substituent on something else.

Multiplying prefixes, and the locant that must be repeated

Two identical substituents take di-, three take tri-, four take tetra-, five penta-. Each one still gets its own number, even when two of them sit on the same carbon: a pentane with two methyls both on C2 is 2,2-dimethylpentane, and writing 2-dimethylpentane is not a shorthand — it is missing a locant, and the reader cannot tell where the second methyl went.

Count the locants against the multiplying prefix every time you write a name. di needs two numbers, tri needs three. It is the fastest self-check in nomenclature and it catches most typos.

One variant to recognize rather than produce: when the repeated substituent is itself complex, the multiplier becomes bis-, tris-, tetrakis- and the group stays in parentheses — bis(2-methylpropyl) rather than di(2-methylpropyl) — because "di" in front of a name that already contains a number could be read as part of that name.

Alphabetical order, and what does not count

Substituents are cited in the name in alphabetical order, regardless of their locants. So 4-ethyl-2-methylhexane, with ethyl first, even though its number is higher.

The question is what you alphabetize by, and the rule has a sharp edge:

  • Ignore the multiplying prefixes di-, tri-, tetra-. "Dimethyl" files under m, not d. So in 4-ethyl-2,2-dimethylhexane, ethyl still comes before dimethyl because e precedes m.
  • Ignore the italicized structural prefixes sec- and tert-. tert-Butyl files under b.
  • Do not ignore iso, neo or cyclo. These are treated as part of the name: isopropyl files under i, neopentyl under n, and cyclohexyl (a six-membered ring used as a branch; rings are named in the last section of this chapter) under c. This is the inconsistency people trip on — iso is joined to the word, tert- is hyphenated and italic, and that typographical difference is exactly what marks which ones count.

The order in which the rules apply

These rules interact, and applying them in the wrong order produces a wrong name that looks reasonable. The order is:

  • First, number for lowest locants. The numbering is decided by the structure, not by the alphabet.
  • Then, if and only if both directions give the identical locant set, break the tie by giving the lower number to the substituent that comes first alphabetically.
  • Last, write the substituents in alphabetical order in the name, whatever their numbers turned out to be.

The middle rule is narrow and is often misremembered as "the alphabetically first substituent always gets the lowest number", which is false: it only applies to a genuine tie.

Worked example — the one time the alphabet numbers the chain

A heptane carries an ethyl on C3 and a methyl on C5, counting from the left. Counting from the right, the methyl is on C3 and the ethyl on C5.

Number. Left gives {3, 5}; right gives {3, 5}. Identical, so the structure has nothing to say about the direction.

Break the tie. Ethyl comes before methyl alphabetically, so ethyl takes the lower number: count from the left.

Assemble. 3-ethyl-5-methylheptane, and not 5-ethyl-3-methylheptane. Both look finished; one follows the rule. Compare it with the example below, where the two locant sets differ at their first term and the alphabet is never consulted about the numbers at all.

Worked example — a name with three substituents

A seven-carbon chain carries a chlorine, an ethyl group and a methyl group. Numbered from the left they sit at C2 (methyl), C4 (ethyl) and C5 (chloro); numbered from the right, at C3, C4 and C6.

Number. Left gives {2, 4, 5}, right gives {3, 4, 6}. First point of difference: 2 beats 3, so number from the left. The alphabet plays no part in this step.

Name the pieces. chloro at 5, ethyl at 4, methyl at 2.

Order them alphabetically. c, then e, then m.

Assemble. 5-chloro-4-ethyl-2-methylheptane. Note the numbers are not in order in the finished name, and that is correct — the alphabet sets the order of the words, the structure set the numbers.

Complex substituents

A branch can itself be branched. When it is, the branch gets a name in parentheses, numbered from its own point of attachment: for a complex (branched) substituent the attaching carbon is C1. A simple systematic -yl name works differently — it gives the free valence the lowest locant available, which is why the isopropyl group is propan-2-yl and not propan-1-yl. A three-carbon branch bearing a methyl on its second carbon is a (2-methylpropyl) group, and the parentheses matter: they tell the reader that the 2 counts along the branch and not along the parent chain.

For alphabetizing, a complex substituent files under the first letter of its complete name, so (2-methylpropyl) files under m.

Worked example — a branch with its own numbering

A nine-carbon row carries, on its fifth carbon, a three-carbon branch whose middle carbon bears a methyl.

Find the parent. The row is nine. The longest route through the branch is three branch carbons, then C5, then four more — eight. Nonane, and the branch is a substituent.

Name the branch from its attachment. The carbon bonded to C5 is the branch's C1, the middle carbon is its C2, the far carbon its C3, and the methyl sits on C2: 2-methylpropyl, in parentheses because that 2 belongs to the branch.

Number the parent. The branch is on C5 from either end — nine is odd and the branch is in the middle — so there is no direction to choose.

Assemble. 5-(2-methylpropyl)nonane. Using the common name of the group instead, 5-isobutylnonane; both are correct, the second is what most people say, and the figure above shows why the parentheses are not optional in the first.

What carries forward

Every substituent in this section is a prefix and none of them can ever be anything else — that is what makes them easy. The next section introduces groups that compete to be the suffix, and the numbering rule changes to serve whichever one wins. The mechanics you have just learned do not change: you still find the longest chain, still number for lowest locants, still alphabetize the prefixes. One more question simply gets asked first.
