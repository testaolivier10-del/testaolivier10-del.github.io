Carbohydrates

Practice this — interactive lesson

Carbohydrates are where Carbonyl Chemistry stops being an exercise. A sugar is a polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone, and almost everything distinctive about one — the ring, the two forms it exists in, whether it reduces Tollens' reagent — follows from a carbonyl reacting with an alcohol that happens to be attached to the same molecule.

The vocabulary, quickly

  • An aldose has an aldehyde; a ketose has a ketone. Combined with the carbon count: glucose is an aldohexose, fructose a ketohexose.
  • A monosaccharide is one sugar unit, a disaccharide two joined together, a polysaccharide many.
  • D and L describe the configuration at the stereocenter furthest from the carbonyl. Drawn as a Fischer projection with the carbonyl at the top, OH on the right at that carbon means D. Essentially every naturally occurring sugar is D.
D and L are not the same as (+)/(−) or R/S. D says only where one particular OH sits in a Fischer projection; it does not tell you which way the compound rotates plane-polarized light, and you cannot infer it. D-glucose and D-fructose are both D and rotate light in opposite directions.

The ring is a hemiacetal, and that explains nearly everything

A sugar in solution is overwhelmingly cyclic, and the ring is not a new kind of bond. It is the intramolecular hemiacetal from Carbonyl Chemistry: one of the molecule's own OH groups attacks its own carbonyl carbon. Five- and six-membered rings form because those are the ring sizes that close without strain.

  • A six-membered ring is a pyranose; a five-membered ring is a furanose.
  • The former carbonyl carbon becomes the anomeric carbon — the one bearing both an OR and an OH.

How a ring sugar is drawn: the Haworth projection

A Haworth projection is the standard way to draw a sugar ring. The ring is drawn flat and seen edge-on, as though it were a tabletop tipped toward you: the bonds along the bottom of the drawing are the ones nearest you and are drawn heavier, and the ring oxygen is placed at the back right. The anomeric carbon sits at the right-hand corner and the numbering runs clockwise from it. Every substituent is then a vertical stick, straight up or straight down from its ring carbon — a Haworth drawing never uses wedges or dashes, because the ring itself is already doing the three-dimensional work.

Converting a Fischer projection into a Haworth ring is mechanical, and there are only two rules to carry. An OH drawn on the right in Fischer points down in Haworth; one on the left points up. And for a D-sugar the terminal CH2OH is drawn up, above the ring. The second rule is not an exception to the first: closing a pyranose ring means rotating C5 about the C4–C5 bond so that its OH can reach C1, and that rotation is what swings the CH2OH to the top. Nothing about the molecule changes in the conversion — only the drawing convention does, exactly as it did when Fischer projections replaced wedges and dashes back in Stereochemistry.

Closing the ring creates a new stereocenter at the anomeric carbon, which did not exist in the open-chain form. The two resulting diastereomers are called anomers and are labeled α and β — a special case of the epimers defined in Stereochemistry, differing at one center only. Drawn as above for a D-sugar, α has the anomeric OH pointing down, on the side of the ring opposite the CH2OH, and β has it up, on the same side as the CH2OH. Keep the "same side as the CH2OH" version: it survives into the L-sugars and into the chair drawing, where up and down on the page stop being reliable.

Worked example — drawing β-D-galactopyranose from the Fischer projection

D-galactose is D-glucose with C4 inverted. In Fischer that reads OH right at C2, left at C3, left at C4, right at C5.

Draw the empty ring first. Six-membered, ring oxygen at the back right, C1 at the right, numbering clockwise. Do not think about substituents yet.

Place the CH2OH. It is a D-sugar, so C5 carries its CH2OH up.

Convert C2, C3 and C4 one at a time. C2 right becomes down. C3 left becomes up. C4 left becomes up — and in glucose C4 was down, so that single stick is the whole of galactose.

Set the anomeric carbon last. β means the C1 OH is on the same side as the CH2OH, so it points up. Had the question said α, it would point down.

The order matters more than it looks. Placing the CH2OH before the anomeric OH means you never have to recall which side α is on in the abstract; you only have to recall that β matches the CH2OH.

Mutarotation is the observation that a freshly dissolved pure anomer slowly changes its optical rotation until it reaches a fixed value. The explanation is that the ring is opening and reclosing through the open-chain aldehyde, so the two anomers interconvert until they reach equilibrium. It is direct evidence that the open form is present, even though it is a tiny fraction of the mixture. For D-glucose the mixture settles at about 36% α and 64% β, with well under 1% open chain at any instant: pure α starts at +112°, pure β at +18.7°, and the equilibrium reads +52.7°. β wins because it is the anomer that puts the anomeric OH equatorial in the chair, which makes β-D-glucopyranose the one common hexose able to place every substituent equatorial at once — a conformational argument from Alkanes & Conformations rather than a new one.
Two traps worth naming now. Mutarotation changes one stereocenter, not five — the ring opens only at the anomeric carbon, so α-D-glucose can never mutarotate into anything but β-D-glucose, and the other four centers are untouched because no C–C bond is broken. And an anomer is not an enantiomer: α- and β-D-glucose differ at one of five stereocenters, so they are diastereomers, with different melting points, different rotations and different solubilities.

Hemiacetal or acetal: the distinction that decides reducing sugars

Carbonyl Chemistry made the point that a hemiacetal can revert to the carbonyl and an acetal cannot without acid. In sugar chemistry that is the whole difference between a reducing and a non-reducing sugar.

  • A free anomeric OH means the ring is a hemiacetal, so it can open to the aldehyde. That aldehyde is oxidizable, so the sugar is a reducing sugar — it gives a positive Tollens' or Benedict's test.
  • If the anomeric position is tied up as an acetal — a glycosidic bond to another alcohol — the ring cannot open, no aldehyde appears, and the sugar is non-reducing.
Worked example — why sucrose does not reduce and maltose does

Maltose joins two glucose units through the anomeric carbon of one and an ordinary OH of the other. That leaves the second unit's anomeric carbon free as a hemiacetal, so it can still open. Maltose is a reducing sugar.

Sucrose joins glucose to fructose through both of their anomeric carbons. Neither ring can open, so no aldehyde is ever available. Sucrose is non-reducing, which is unusual among common disaccharides and is the standard exam example.

Lactose (galactose β-1,4 glucose) and cellobiose (glucose β-1,4 glucose) are the same story as maltose with a β link instead of an α one: one anomeric carbon committed, one left free, so both are reducing. Maltose, lactose and cellobiose all reduce; sucrose is the common disaccharide that does not, which is why it is the one worth remembering by name.

The reasoning never mentions sweetness, size or source. It is only ever: is there a free anomeric OH.

Three reactions, all of them ones you already have

A sugar in solution is overwhelmingly the ring, but each reagent below reacts with the open-chain form and the ring equilibrium simply resupplies it. That is the whole trick, and it is why a reducing sugar behaves like the aldehyde it can become even though almost none of it is an aldehyde at any instant.

  • Reduction. NaBH4 reduces the open-chain aldehyde to a primary alcohol, giving an alditol — a sugar with no carbonyl left anywhere and an OH on every carbon. D-glucose gives D-glucitol, sold as sorbitol. This is the hydride addition from Oxidation & Reduction, run on the trace of open chain.
  • Oxidation. Br2 in water is a mild oxidant that leaves alcohols alone, so it oxidizes the aldehyde and stops: the product is an aldonic acid, and D-glucose gives D-gluconic acid. A stronger oxidant such as warm nitric acid takes the terminal CH2OH as well and gives the diacid, an aldaric acid. Tollens' and Benedict's reagents do the same job as bromine water but are used as tests rather than preparations.
  • Glycoside formation. An alcohol plus acid converts the hemiacetal into the full acetal — a glycoside. Methanol and HCl give methyl α- and β-D-glucopyranoside, and the product is non-reducing because the ring can no longer open. Being an acetal, it is stable to base and hydrolyzed straight back by warm aqueous acid.
Tollens' and Benedict's are run in base, and base is what lets a ketose cheat. Removing a proton α to the C2 carbonyl and reprotonating on oxygen gives an enediol — a C=C carrying an OH on both carbons, the intermediate from Enolate Chemistry — and collapsing it the other way puts the carbonyl at C1, which is an aldose. Fructose therefore gives a positive test with no aldehyde anywhere in it. The honest rule is that a sugar with a free anomeric center reduces Tollens', ketoses included; a capped anomeric carbon is what makes a sugar safe.

The polysaccharides worth knowing

  • Cellulose — glucose joined β-1,4. The β linkage makes a straight chain that stacks into fibers, and human enzymes cannot hydrolyze it.
  • Starch (amylose) — glucose joined α-1,4. The α linkage makes a helical chain, and we digest it easily.

The two polymers are made of the same monomer and differ only in the configuration at one carbon. That single stereochemical difference is why one is food and the other is wood, which is as sharp a demonstration of why stereochemistry matters as this course contains.

What carries forward

Nothing in this section is new chemistry. It is hemiacetal and acetal formation, Fischer projections and diastereomers, applied to molecules that happen to be biological. If a carbohydrate question looks unfamiliar, the useful move is to find the carbonyl — or the carbon that used to be one — and ask what has added to it.
