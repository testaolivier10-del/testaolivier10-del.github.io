No intervention matters if air cannot reach the lungs, which is why the airway comes first in every assessment. This chapter traces the path a breath follows from the nose and mouth to the lungs, explains why one small structure, the epiglottis, accounts for so much airway trouble, and then works through what you actually do about a threatened airway: the two manual maneuvers and when each one applies, sizing and inserting the oral and nasal airways, suctioning within the limits that keep it from doing harm, reading the four sounds an obstructed airway makes, and the technique that makes a bag-valve mask work rather than merely look like it is working.

Quick answer

When do you use a jaw-thrust instead of a head-tilt/chin-lift?

Use the head-tilt/chin-lift by default, when no spinal injury is suspected, and the jaw-thrust when you suspect a spinal injury, because it opens the airway without moving the neck. If the jaw-thrust does not open the airway, use the maneuver that does, because an open airway outranks spinal precautions.

9.1

Airway Anatomy & the Manual Maneuvers

Airway, Respiration & Ventilation domain

Every breath follows the same physical path, and airway management is entirely about keeping that path open

Air enters through the nose or mouth, travels through the throat and down the windpipe, and reaches the lungs, where the actual exchange of gases takes place. The nose does something the mouth does not: as air passes through its curved internal passages, the nose warms and humidifies it. The whole path is divided into two regions with a specific dividing line. The upper airway runs from the mouth and nose down to the vocal cords, and the lower airway is everything below that.

The epiglottis is a single small structure doing a genuinely critical job

Right where the throat meets the windpipe sits the epiglottis, a flap that flips downward every time you swallow, sealing off the airway so that food and liquid go toward the esophagus instead of the lungs. Just below it is the glottic opening, at the level of the vocal cords, which marks the actual boundary between the upper and lower airway. This one structure explains a great deal of airway pathology. Swelling here, injury here, or failure of the mechanism itself, as in a patient with a depressed level of consciousness who can no longer protect their own airway, is why airway problems are addressed before almost anything else in patient care.

In an unresponsive patient the tongue is the most common obstruction, and it is an obstruction you can fix with your hands

The tongue is anchored to the lower jaw. When a patient loses consciousness, the muscles that hold the jaw forward relax, the jaw falls back toward the spine, and the tongue falls back with it against the posterior wall of the throat. Nothing is stuck in the airway and nothing is swollen; the airway has simply folded shut. This is the single most common cause of airway obstruction in an unresponsive patient, and the reason the first thing you do for such a patient costs nothing and takes two seconds.

Both manual maneuvers work by the same mechanism: they move the lower jaw forward, and the tongue, being attached to it, comes forward off the back of the throat. Neither one opens anything. They un-fold something.

Head-tilt/chin-lift is the default; jaw-thrust is the one you use when the spine is in question

The head-tilt/chin-lift is the standard maneuver for a patient with no suspected spinal injury. One hand on the forehead tilts the head back; the fingertips of the other hand under the bony part of the chin lift it upward. Keep your fingers on bone. Pressing into the soft tissue under the chin pushes the base of the tongue up into the airway, which is the opposite of what you are trying to do.

The jaw-thrust is the maneuver for a patient in whom you suspect a spinal injury, because it opens the airway without moving the neck. You position yourself at the patient's head, place your fingers behind the angles of the jaw on both sides, and displace the jaw forward while keeping the head in a neutral, in-line position. It is harder to hold and it ties up both of your hands, which is why it is not the default.

There is an order of priority worth being explicit about, because scenarios are written to test it. A patient with a suspected spinal injury who has no airway gets the jaw-thrust first. If the jaw-thrust does not open the airway, you use the maneuver that does. An open airway is the higher priority, because a protected spine in a patient who is not breathing is not a good outcome.

A child's airway is not a small adult airway, and the differences change what you do

Several differences matter at the bedside. A child's tongue is proportionally larger relative to the mouth, so tongue obstruction is more likely and more complete. The airway structures themselves are smaller and softer, so a given amount of swelling narrows the airway far more, and so the trachea can be collapsed by pressure from outside. The back of an infant's head is proportionally large, so when an infant is laid flat on a hard surface the head is pushed forward into flexion and the airway closes.

The practical consequence is that the neutral position for an infant or small child is not the same as for an adult. You may need a folded towel under the shoulders to bring the airway back into line, and over-extending the neck, tilting the head back as far as it will go, will kink a soft trachea shut rather than open it.

Key points · 9.1
  • Airway path: nose/mouth → throat → windpipe → lungs (gas exchange).
  • The nose warms and humidifies incoming air; the mouth does not.
  • Upper airway: mouth/nose down to the vocal cords. Lower airway: everything below the vocal cords.
  • Epiglottis: flap that seals the airway during swallowing, directing food/liquid to the esophagus instead of the lungs.
  • Glottic opening (at the vocal cords) marks the boundary between upper and lower airway.
  • A patient with depressed LOC may lose the ability to protect their own airway via this mechanism: why airway comes first in patient care.
  • The tongue is the most common airway obstruction in an unresponsive patient; it is attached to the jaw, so moving the jaw forward moves the tongue forward.
  • Head-tilt/chin-lift: the default maneuver, no suspected spinal injury. Lift the bony chin, not the soft tissue under it.
  • Jaw-thrust: the maneuver for suspected spinal injury: opens the airway without moving the neck.
  • If a jaw-thrust does not open the airway, use the maneuver that does. An open airway outranks spinal precautions.
  • Pediatric airway: larger tongue, smaller and softer structures, large occiput. Padding under the shoulders keeps an infant's airway in line; over-extension kinks it shut.

9.4

Choking: Foreign Body Airway Obstruction

Airway, Respiration & Ventilation domain

Mild or severe is the only question, and the answer is how much air is moving

A foreign body in the airway produces one of two problems, and telling them apart decides everything that follows. The deciding evidence is air movement, not whether the patient looks distressed.

A mild obstruction leaves enough of the airway open for effective air exchange. The patient can cough forcefully, can speak, and can move air you can hear. Do not intervene. A forceful cough generates more airflow, and more sudden pressure, than any thrust a rescuer can deliver by hand, and the patient is already doing the most effective thing available. Stay with them, encourage the coughing, and watch. Reaching in with thrusts or a finger on a patient who is still clearing the airway themselves can drive the object further in and convert a mild obstruction into a severe one.

A severe obstruction means air exchange has become inadequate. The cough is weak or absent, the patient cannot speak, there is high-pitched stridor or no sound at all, and cyanosis follows. Many patients clutch the throat: the universal choking sign. Note the trap here: a patient with a weak, ineffective cough is still technically coughing, and still counts as severe. The question is never whether something is happening but whether enough air is moving to sustain the patient.

Silence with visible effort is the worst finding in the whole chapter: the patient is trying to breathe and nothing is moving.

The 2025 sequence: five back blows first, at every age

The 2025 AHA guidelines settled on one shape for the maneuver across every age group: five back blows, then five thrusts, repeated. Only the second half changes with age.

Table 9.1  The 2025 sequence: five back blows first, at every age
AdultChild (1 year to puberty)Infant (under 1 year)
First5 back blows between the shoulder blades, with the heel of the hand
Then5 abdominal thrusts5 abdominal thrusts5 chest thrusts, heel of one hand on the lower sternum

If you cannot reach around the abdomen5 chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts: late pregnancy, or significant obesityAbdominal thrusts are not used at this age at all

For a responsive adult or child, position yourself behind the patient, place a fist thumb-side in just above the navel and below the xiphoid, grasp it with the other hand, and deliver each thrust inward and upward as a distinct movement rather than a squeeze. For an infant, support the head and jaw, lay the infant face down along your forearm with the head lower than the chest for the back blows, then turn the infant as a unit and deliver chest thrusts on the lower sternum.

Abdominal thrusts are never used on an infant. An infant's liver is proportionally large and sits low, largely below the protection of the rib cage, and an abdominal thrust can tear it.

Key points · 9.4
  • Mild vs. severe turns on air exchange, not on distress. Forceful cough and speech = mild = encourage coughing and do nothing else.
  • A weak or ineffective cough, stridor, inability to speak, or cyanosis = severe, even though the patient is still 'doing something.'
  • 2025 AHA sequence, every age: 5 back blows, then 5 thrusts, repeated until the object clears or the patient goes unresponsive.
  • Adult and child: the thrusts are abdominal. Infant under 1 year: the thrusts are chest thrusts, with the heel of one hand.
  • Back blows are new for adults and children: it used to be abdominal thrusts alone. A cohort of 709 patients showed back blows relieved more obstructions with fewer injuries.
  • Infant chest thrusts use the heel of one hand, not two fingers. Two fingers did not reach adequate depth, and were dropped from infant compressions too.
  • Abdominal thrusts are not used under 1 year: the infant liver is large, low, and poorly protected.
  • Cannot get your arms around the abdomen (late pregnancy, significant obesity)? 5 chest thrusts replace the abdominal thrusts.
  • Patient goes unresponsive: lower them and start CPR with compressions. Look in the mouth before each set of breaths, remove only what you can see.
  • No blind finger sweeps at any age, because they push the object deeper.
  • Every patient whose obstruction is relieved is still transported: thrust injuries, retained fragments, and later aspiration pneumonia.
  • Thrusts relieve a foreign body. They do nothing for a swollen airway (anaphylaxis, burns, epiglottitis): that is oxygen, calm, and rapid transport.
