## 3. Essential knowledge per topic (our words)

Source per unit: Units 1 to 4.8 **[CED]** (WebFetch extract of the CED PDF); 4.9 to 7.8 **[CED-FC]** (Firecrawl
parse of the same PDF). Unit 1 EK was confirmed by both. Codes in brackets.

Unit 1 Thinking Geographically
- 1.1 [IMP-1.A]: reference vs thematic maps; maps show absolute and relative distance and direction, clustering,
  dispersal, elevation; every map selects what it shows; **every projection distorts shape, area, distance or direction**
  (no projection is named in EK).
- 1.2 [IMP-1.B]: field data from organizations or individuals; geospatial tools (GIS, satellite navigation, remote
  sensing, online mapping and visualization); written and qualitative sources (field notes, media, travel accounts,
  policy documents, interviews, landscape analysis, photo interpretation).
- 1.3 [IMP-1.C]: census data and satellite imagery inform personal, business and government decisions at every scale.
- 1.4 [PSO-1.A]: absolute and relative location, space, place, flows, distance decay, time-space compression, pattern.
- 1.5 [PSO-1.B]: sustainability, natural resources, land use; thinking moved from environmental determinism to
  possibilism.
- 1.6 [PSO-1.C, PSO-1.D]: scales of analysis are global, regional, national, local; a pattern can look different, and
  be read differently, at another scale.
- 1.7 [SPS-1.A]: regions rest on shared traits or activity patterns; formal, functional, perceptual/vernacular;
  boundaries are transitional, often contested and overlapping; regional analysis works at local, national, global
  scales.

Unit 2 Population and Migration
- 2.1 [PSO-2.A]: physical and human factors shape distribution, and which factors matter depends on scale.
- 2.2 [PSO-2.B-D]: arithmetic, physiological and agricultural density and what each says about pressure on land;
  distribution and density affect services, society and environment; carrying capacity.
- 2.3 [PSO-2.E-F]: age structure, sex ratio, population pyramids (reading growth, decline, future needs).
- 2.4 [IMP-2.A]: fertility, mortality, migration; rate of natural increase (RNI) and doubling time; social, cultural,
  political, economic influences on them.
- 2.5 [IMP-2.B]: demographic transition model; epidemiological transition (changing causes of death).
- 2.6 [IMP-2.B]: Malthusian theory and its critiques.
- 2.7 [SPS-2.A]: pronatalist, antinatalist and immigration policies, intent and effects.
- 2.8 [SPS-2.B]: more female education, jobs, health care and contraception lower fertility in most places; changing
  female roles also affect mortality and migration, illustrated by Ravenstein's laws of migration.
- 2.9 [SPS-2.C]: aging from birth rates, death rates, life expectancy; political, social, economic effects; dependency
  ratio.
- 2.10 [IMP-2.C]: push and pull factors; intervening opportunities and obstacles; cultural, demographic, economic,
  environmental, political causes.
- 2.11 [IMP-2.D]: forced (slavery, refugees, internally displaced persons, asylum seekers) vs voluntary
  (transnational, transhumance, internal, chain, step, guest worker, rural-to-urban).
- 2.12 [IMP-2.E]: political, economic and cultural effects of migration, past and present.

Unit 3 Culture
- 3.1 [PSO-3.A]: culture as shared practices, technologies, attitudes and behaviors passed on; culture traits (food,
  architecture, land use); cultural relativism vs ethnocentrism.
- 3.2 [PSO-3.B, PSO-3.C]: cultural landscape as physical features plus farming, industry, religious and linguistic
  marks, sequent occupance, traditional and postmodern architecture, land use; attitudes to ethnicity and gender
  (including women in the workforce), ethnic neighborhoods, Indigenous communities and lands shape space.
- 3.3 [PSO-3.D]: regional patterns of language, religion, ethnicity give sense of place and placemaking; they act as
  centripetal and centrifugal forces.
- 3.4 [IMP-3.A]: relocation vs expansion diffusion; expansion includes contagious, hierarchical, stimulus.
- 3.5 [SPS-3.A]: interaction of local traits with global forces creates new forms (creolization, lingua franca);
  colonialism, imperialism, trade shaped cultural patterns.
- 3.6 [SPS-3.A]: culture is socially constructed and changes through urbanization and globalization via media,
  technology, politics, economics, social ties; communication technology and time-space convergence spread English,
  threaten Indigenous languages, and drive both cultural convergence and divergence.
- 3.7 [IMP-3.B]: language families (Indo-European named), languages, dialects, religions, ethnic cultures and gender
  roles spread from hearths; shown by maps, charts, toponyms; universalizing religions (Christianity, Islam,
  Buddhism, Sikhism) spread by expansion and relocation; ethnic religions (Hinduism, Judaism) stay near the hearth or
  spread by relocation.
- 3.8 [SPS-3.B]: acculturation, assimilation, syncretism, multiculturalism as effects of diffusion.

Unit 4 Political
- 4.1 [PSO-4.A]: independent states as the basic unit; nations, nation-states, stateless nations, multinational
  states, multistate nations, autonomous and semiautonomous regions (e.g. American Indian reservations).
- 4.2 [PSO-4.B]: sovereignty, nation-states, self-determination; colonialism, imperialism, independence movements and
  devolution shaped today's boundaries.
- 4.3 [PSO-4.C]: power as control over people, land, resources (neocolonialism, shatterbelts, choke points);
  territoriality ties people, culture and economy to land.
- 4.4 [IMP-4.A]: boundary types: relict, superimposed, subsequent, antecedent, geometric, consequent.
- 4.5 [IMP-4.B]: boundaries are defined, delimited, demarcated, administered, and often disputed; they often follow
  cultural, national or economic lines, sometimes set by DMZs or policy (Berlin Conference); land and maritime
  boundaries shape identity and interaction; UNCLOS (territorial seas, EEZs, international waters).
- 4.6 [IMP-4.B]: voting districts, redistricting, gerrymandering affect elections at several scales.
- 4.7 [IMP-4.C, IMP-4.D]: unitary vs federal states and how each spreads or centralizes power in space.
- 4.8 [SPS-4.A]: devolution factors: physical division, ethnic separatism, ethnic cleansing, terrorism, economic and
  social problems, irredentism.
- 4.9 [SPS-4.B] [CED-FC]: devolution into autonomous regions or subnational units (Spain, Belgium, Canada, Nigeria
  named) or state breakup (Sudan, former Soviet Union named); communication technology aids devolution,
  supranationalism and democratization; global problems, economies of scale, trade deals and alliances drive
  supranationalism; UN, NATO, EU, ASEAN, Arctic Council, African Union can limit members' sovereignty.
- 4.10 [SPS-4.C] [CED-FC]: centrifugal forces can lead to failed states, uneven development, stateless nations, ethnic
  nationalist movements; centripetal forces can lead to ethnonationalism, fairer infrastructure, cultural cohesion.

Unit 5 Agriculture [CED-FC]
- 5.1 [PSO-5.A]: climate and environment shape farming (Mediterranean, tropical named); intensive (market gardening,
  plantation, mixed crop-livestock) vs extensive (shifting cultivation, nomadic herding, ranching).
- 5.2 [PSO-5.B]: farming shapes rural land use; settlement patterns clustered, dispersed, linear; survey methods metes
  and bounds, township and range, long lot.
- 5.3 [SPS-5.A, SPS-5.B]: hearths of domestication (Fertile Crescent, Indus valley, Southeast Asia, Central America);
  Columbian Exchange and agricultural revolutions spread plants and animals.
- 5.4 [SPS-5.C]: Second Agricultural Revolution: technology, more food, better diets, longer lives, labor freed for
  factories.
- 5.5 [SPS-5.D]: Green Revolution: high-yield seeds, more chemicals, mechanization; positive and negative human and
  environmental effects.
- 5.6 [PSO-5.C]: subsistence vs commercial (monocropping, monoculture); land cost and **bid-rent theory** help set
  intensive vs extensive use.
- 5.7 [PSO-5.C]: large commercial operations replacing family farms; commodity chains; technology raises economies of
  scale and carrying capacity.
- 5.8 [PSO-5.D]: **von Thünen** explains rural land use by transport cost from the market; specialty farming regions
  often break the rings.
- 5.9 [PSO-5.E]: global supply chains; dependence on one or a few export commodities; food distribution shaped by
  politics, infrastructure, trade.
- 5.10 [IMP-5.A]: environmental effects (pollution, land cover change, desertification, salinization, conservation);
  landscape-changing practices (slash and burn, terraces, irrigation, deforestation, wetland draining, shifting
  cultivation, pastoral nomadism); societal effects (diets, women's role, economic purpose).
- 5.11 [IMP-5.B]: debates over biotechnology, GMOs, aquaculture (sustainability, soil and water, biodiversity,
  fertilizer and pesticide use); food-choice movements (urban farming, CSA, organic, value-added specialty crops, fair
  trade, local food, dietary shifts); feeding the world (food insecurity, food deserts, distribution, weather,
  farmland lost to suburbs); economics of processing location, scale, distribution, policy.
- 5.12 [IMP-5.C]: women's role in food production, distribution and consumption varies by place and type of
  production.

Unit 6 Cities [CED-FC]
- 6.1 [PSO-6.A]: site and situation shape where cities start, what they do and how they grow; transport,
  communication, population growth, migration, development, policy drive urbanization.
- 6.2 [PSO-6.A]: megacities and metacities, increasingly in periphery and semiperiphery countries; suburbanization,
  sprawl, decentralization produce edge cities, exurbs, boomburbs.
- 6.3 [PSO-6.B]: world cities top the urban hierarchy and drive globalization; city networks mediate global processes.
- 6.4 [PSO-6.C]: **rank-size rule, primate city, gravity, Christaller's central place theory**.
- 6.5 [PSO-6.D]: **Burgess concentric zone, Hoyt sector, Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei, galactic city, bid-rent,
  and urban models drawn from Latin America, Southeast Asia and Africa** (the regional models are not named after
  their authors in EK).
- 6.6 [IMP-6.A]: housing and land use reflect and shape culture, technology, cycles of development, infilling.
- 6.7 [IMP-6.B]: location and quality of infrastructure shape economic and social development patterns.
- 6.8 [IMP-6.C, IMP-6.D]: mixed use, walkability, transit-oriented development, smart growth (New Urbanism,
  greenbelts, slow-growth cities); praise (less sprawl, walkability, housing choice, livability) and criticism (higher
  housing costs, possible de facto segregation, loss of historic character).
- 6.9 [IMP-6.E]: quantitative census and survey data on urban population; qualitative field studies and narratives on
  attitudes to change.
- 6.10 [SPS-6.A]: housing problems (redlining, blockbusting, affordability), access to services, crime, environmental
  injustice, disamenity zones or zones of abandonment; squatter settlements and land tenure conflicts; responses
  (inclusionary zoning, local food); urban renewal and gentrification both ways; fragmented local government.
- 6.11 [SPS-6.B]: sprawl, sanitation, climate change, air and water quality, ecological footprint, energy use;
  responses (regional planning, brownfield cleanup, urban growth boundaries, farmland protection).

Unit 7 Industry and Development [CED-FC]
- 7.1 [SPS-7.A]: industrialization from new technology plus resources; more food, population growth, urban factory
  jobs, new class structures; search for raw materials and markets fed colonialism and imperialism.
- 7.2 [SPS-7.B]: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary, quinary sectors; manufacturing location set by labor,
  transport (shipping containers), break-of-bulk points, **least cost theory** (Weber's, though EK does not name him),
  markets, resources; core, semiperiphery, periphery locations.
- 7.3 [SPS-7.C]: GDP, GNP, GNI per capita, sectoral structure (formal and informal), income distribution, fertility,
  infant mortality, health care access, fossil vs renewable energy use, literacy; Gender Inequality Index
  (reproductive health, empowerment, labor participation); HDI as a composite.
- 7.4 [SPS-7.D]: women's roles change with development; more women work but without wage or opportunity equity;
  microloans and small businesses.
- 7.5 [SPS-7.E]: **Rostow's stages of economic growth, Wallerstein's world system theory, dependency theory, commodity
  dependence**.
- 7.6 [PSO-7.A]: complementarity and comparative advantage; neoliberal policy and trade blocs (EU, WTO, Mercosur, OPEC
  named); tariffs and government initiatives at all scales; debt crises, IMF, microlending show interdependence.
- 7.7 [PSO-7.A]: outsourcing and restructuring move jobs from core to newly industrialized countries; special economic
  zones, free-trade zones, export-processing zones; international division of labor; post-Fordism, multiplier effects,
  economies of scale, agglomeration, just-in-time, service growth, high tech, growth poles.
- 7.8 [IMP-7.A]: sustainable development (resource depletion, mass consumption, pollution, climate change);
  ecotourism; UN Sustainable Development Goals.

### Task verbs (CED exam section, [CED-FC], paraphrased)
- Identify: give the information, no elaboration.
- Define: give a specific meaning of a term.
- Describe: give the relevant characteristics.
- Explain: say how or why a relationship, process, pattern, position or outcome happens, with evidence or reasoning.
- Compare: describe or explain similarities and/or differences.
- Also seen in scoring guidelines: "explain the degree to which" (state a degree, then justify) and "explain a
  limitation" of a source (2024 set 1 Q3, 2025 set 1 Q2, set 2 Q2). CR reports note "define" is rarely used (2025 set 1).

## 5. Exam format, May 2027

- **Date**: Monday 3 May 2027, **Session 1** (exam page, student page). Session 1 starts 8:00 a.m. local in most US
  schools, 7:00 a.m. in Alaska; exams may start up to 1 hour after the listed time (start-times page).
- **Mode**: fully digital in Bluebook, responses auto-submitted. **2 h 15 min** total (student exam page). The 2023
  paper instructions show a 10-minute break between sections; whether the digital exam keeps it was not stated on the
  pages read (https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-human-geography-exam-instructions.pdf, 2022-23 paper
  version). No calculator listed.
- **Section I MCQ**: 60 questions, 60 min, 50% of score; individual items and sets; **about 30-40% of questions use a
  stimulus** (maps, tables, charts, graphs, images, infographics, landscapes), split roughly evenly between
  quantitative and qualitative sources (exam page). Student page adds satellite images, photographs and cartoons.
  **Answer choices: five (A-E) per the CED sample questions [CED-FC]**; the 2027 exam pages do not state a count and no
  official source read confirms the digital exam still uses five (Kaplan's undated strategy page also says five,
  https://www.kaptest.com/study/ap-human-geography/ap-human-geography-multiple-choice-strategies/ , [3P]). Verify
  before writing MCQs; see section 9.
- **Section II FRQ**: 3 questions, 75 min, 50% of score, **7 points each, one point per part A-G (21 raw)** (CED-FC;
  every 2023-2026 SG read has seven 1-point parts). No suggested time per question was found; students move freely
  among the three.
  - **Q1: no stimulus.**
  - **Q2: one stimulus** (data, image or map).
  - **Q3: two stimuli** (data, images and/or maps).
  - Scale: the CED says **at least two** of the three FRQs assess analysis across scales; the AP Central exam page
    says "at least one". Build for two.
  Sources: https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-human-geography/exam ;
  https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-human-geography/assessment ; CED.
- **Every FRQ crosses units.** Each 2023-2026 question paired two units (e.g. 2 with 4, 3 with 5, 6 with 7). CR reports
  in 2023, 2024 and 2025 all tell teachers to practice cross-unit questions.

## 7. Where students lose points (CR reports 2023-2025, SG 2026)

No 2026 CR report or scoring statistics are posted yet (past exam questions page lists only FRQ and SG for 2026).

### Task verb errors (every report)
- **Describe or explain asked, identify given.** Naming a diffusion type without describing it (2024 set 2 Q1A);
  defining devolution instead of explaining why a group secedes (2023 set 2 Q1E); naming gerrymandering, cracking or
  packing without the effect on votes (2024 set 1 Q2F, 2023 set 2 Q1F).
- **Explain needs a full causal chain.** Readers want every link: sprawl, so more driving, so more air pollution
  (2024 set 2 Q2D); transit, so fewer cars, so less pollution (2024 set 2 Q2E); a farm practice, so the specific harm
  (2024 set 2 Q1D). Advice: linking words such as because, therefore, whereas (2023 set 2).
- **Compare needs both sides.** Defining both terms without comparing (2023 set 1 Q1C RNI vs TFR); discussing only
  urban or only rural (2023 set 1 Q1D); only unitary states (2023 set 2 Q1C); only one map (2025 set 1 Q3C); only one
  direction of trade (2025 set 2 Q2F).
- **"Explain the degree to which"**: students skip the degree. Reports in 2023, 2024 and 2025 all advise stating
  high, moderate or low first, then justifying (2023 set 1 Q1, 2023 set 2 Q3G, 2024 set 1 Q1G, 2024 set 2 Q1G, 2025 set
  1 Q2G, 2025 set 2 Q2F).
- **Restating the prompt** earns nothing (2023 set 2 Q2, 2024 set 2 Q3G).

### Scale of analysis errors
- Answering at the wrong scale: country instead of city (2025 set 2 Q1B); saying "national" when the map's data are
  subnational (2025 set 2 Q3A, only 38% correct). Rule from the report: the scale of analysis is the scale of the data
  shown, not the size of the area on the map.
- Linking global to local: global farm surplus to a local farm's response (2024 set 1 Q1F).
- Limits of a source across scales: a regional map cannot support a national conclusion (2024 set 1 Q3G, ecological
  fallacy idea); a national pyramid shows nothing about one city or about migration (2025 set 1 Q2F).

### Specific example missing or vague
- "Clustered" or "dispersed" alone earns nothing; students must say where (inland, northeast, which continent or
  region) (2024 set 1 Q2B and Q3B, 2025 set 1 Q3B). Absolute claims ("all world cities are in...") also fail.
- Reports advise pairing every concept with a concrete place or event (2025 set 1 and set 2 general remarks).
- Off-region answers: discussing countries outside the asked continent (2025 set 1 Q3C).

### Reading maps and data
- Misreading a legend (metacity vs world city, 2024 set 1 Q3A); confusing exports with imports or profits (2025 set 2
  Q2B); not citing table values when told to use the stimulus (2023 set 2 Q2F, 2024 set 1 Q3G); placing low-growth
  countries in DTM stage 1 or 2 instead of 4 or 5 (2024 set 2 Q3A). Advice: practice reading legends and choropleth
  maps, comparing two maps, and saying what a source cannot show (2025 set 1).

### Recurring content confusions (build distractors and checks on these)
- Carrying capacity vs population density (2024 set 1 Q1A); large area vs dense population (2025 set 2 Q3).
- GMOs counted as Green Revolution (the Green Revolution is roughly 1960s to 1980s; commercial biotech crops came in
  the late 1990s) (2024 set 1); Green Revolution confused with the Green New Deal (2025 set 2 Q2D).
- Comparative vs absolute advantage (2025 set 2 Q2C, 6% success).
- Redlining described as about race or income in general, not as refusing services or setting worse terms by
  neighborhood location; blockbusting described wrongly (2025 set 2 Q1C, 18%).
- Edge cities treated as residential suburbs instead of business and job centers (2024 set 2 Q2F).
- Site vs situation (2024 set 2 Q2B); boundary types vs region types (2024 set 2 Q2A).
- Pronatalist vs antinatalist (2024 set 2 Q3B and E); RNI formula reversed or including migration (2023 set 1 Q1A).
- Territoriality vs sovereignty vs territory (2023 set 2 Q1A); supranational sovereignty limits misread as unanimity
  rules (2025 set 1 Q1F); federal system described without the national level (2024 set 2 Q2C).
- Assimilation vs acculturation vs multiculturalism (2024 set 1 Q2C and D); cultural landscape read as the physical
  landscape (2023 set 2 Q3C, 2024 set 1 Q2E).
- Galactic city model unknown or mixed with multiple nuclei or sector (2023 set 1 Q3D); growth pole vs agglomeration
  (2023 set 1 Q3B); global supply chain reduced to transport (2023 set 1 Q2G).
- Columbian Exchange confused with Colombia (2023 set 1 Q2D); toponym not known (2024 set 2 Q1F); Rostow confused with
  DTM or Wallerstein (2023 set 2 Q2G); HDI components confused with other indicators (2023 set 2 Q2A-B).
- Economic vs environmental vs social outcome asked, wrong kind given (2024 set 1 Q1D, 2024 set 2 Q3D, 2023 set 2 Q2C).

### Reader advice to build into the course
Teach Units 6 and 7 fully (often rushed at year end, 2023 set 1); apply urban models to real maps; teach terms in
context, then apply to unfamiliar places; practice several questions on one dataset; teach comparative advantage with
a two-country, two-good example (2025 set 2); write legibly (2024 set 2).

## 8. Every model and theory students must know (with CED topic)

All are named in EK unless marked (topic column gives the EK source above). The "core idea" and "limits" columns are
our own summaries of standard textbook content **[ours]**, not CED text, written to serve skills 1.E and 5.D
(strengths, weaknesses, how well a model fits across contexts or scales); an accuracy reviewer should check them.

| Model or theory | Topic | Core idea (our words) | Common limits to teach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Environmental determinism vs possibilism | 1.5 | environment dictates culture vs environment offers choices people make | determinism used to justify colonial hierarchy; possibilism can understate real limits |
| Demographic transition model | 2.5 | birth and death rates fall in sequence as a society develops, moving through stages (teach 5) | built on European history; ignores migration; timing differs; stage 5 not universal |
| Epidemiological transition | 2.5 | main causes of death shift from infectious disease and famine to degenerative and human-made diseases | reemerging infections and pandemics break the sequence |
| Malthusian theory (and critiques) | 2.6 | population grows faster than food, so checks follow | technology raised output (Green Revolution); fertility fell with development; distribution, not supply, causes much hunger. 2024 set 1 Q1E asked for a weakness |
| Ravenstein's laws of migration | 2.8 | most moves are short, step-wise, toward cities, with counterflows; patterns differ by sex and age | 19th-century British data; long-distance and international flows now larger |
| Push-pull, intervening opportunities and obstacles | 2.10 | migration weighs origin and destination factors plus what lies between | not a named model in EK (Lee is not named) |
| Diffusion types | 3.4 | relocation vs expansion (contagious, hierarchical, stimulus) | real spread mixes types |
| Centripetal and centrifugal forces | 3.3, 4.10 | unifying vs dividing forces | same trait can do both at different scales |
| Boundary genetic types | 4.4 | antecedent, subsequent, consequent, superimposed, relict, geometric | a boundary can fit more than one type |
| Von Thünen | 5.8 | farm land use in rings set by transport cost to one market | isolated flat plain, one market; specialty and refrigerated transport break rings |
| Bid-rent theory | 5.6, 6.5 | land users bid more for access to the center, so intensity falls with distance | multiple centers, zoning and transport change the curve |
| Rank-size rule | 6.4 | the nth city has 1/n the population of the largest | fits some national urban systems, not those with a primate city |
| Primate city | 6.4 | the largest city is far bigger than the second and dominates the country | often a colonial or capital legacy |
| Gravity model | 6.4 | interaction rises with the size of two places and falls with distance between them | distance decay changes with technology; borders and culture matter |
| Christaller central place theory | 6.4 | settlements of different orders serve hexagonal market areas set by range and threshold | uniform plain and travel assumptions; online retail |
| Burgess concentric zone | 6.5 | rings around the CBD from industry out to commuter zone (built on 1920s Chicago) | one city, pre-car; ignores transport corridors |
| Hoyt sector | 6.5 | wedges along transport lines, high-rent sectors persist | still CBD-centred |
| Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei | 6.5 | several centres of activity, not one CBD | does not predict where nuclei form |
| Galactic city (peripheral) | 6.5 | CBD plus edge-city nodes along a ring road or highway junctions | describes US car-based metros; weak elsewhere. 2023 set 1 Q3D students did not know it |
| Latin American city model | 6.5 | spine of elite land use from the CBD, zones of maturity and in situ accretion, peripheral squatter settlements | EK does not name the authors (commonly Griffin and Ford) |
| Southeast Asian city model | 6.5 | port zone focus, mixed commercial zones, no single strong CBD | EK unnamed (commonly McGee) |
| African city model | 6.5 | several CBDs (colonial, traditional, market), ethnic neighborhoods, outer informal settlements | EK unnamed (commonly de Blij) |
| Least cost theory (Weber) | 7.2 | factories locate to minimize transport, labor and agglomeration costs; weight-gaining vs weight-losing | assumes cost minimizing; ignores policy and amenities |
| Rostow's stages of economic growth | 7.5 | five stages from traditional society to high mass consumption | assumes one Western path; ignores colonial history and global structure |
| Wallerstein world system theory | 7.5 | core, semiperiphery and periphery linked in one capitalist economy | hard to measure; countries can move between tiers |
| Dependency theory | 7.5 | peripheral poverty results from dependence on the core | some former peripheries industrialized |
| Commodity dependence | 7.5, 5.9 | reliance on one or few raw exports makes an economy vulnerable to price swings | |
| Complementarity and comparative advantage | 7.6 | trade happens where one place has what another needs, and each produces at lower opportunity cost | 2025 set 2 Q2C: 6% success |

## 9. Maps, projections and scales

- **Map types (EK 1.1)**: reference vs thematic. The thematic types students meet in stimuli (choropleth, dot density,
  graduated or proportional symbol, isoline, cartogram, flow lines) are not listed in EK; the 2025 set 1 CR names
  choropleth maps and reading legends as a skill gap, so teach them as reading skills [ours, plus CR].
- **Projections (EK 1.1)**: EK names none, only that every projection distorts shape, area, distance or direction.
  Teach examples as illustrations of the trade-off [ours]: Mercator (keeps angles and local shape, inflates area
  toward the poles), Gall-Peters (equal area, distorts shape), Robinson and Winkel tripel (compromise), azimuthal or
  polar (true direction and distance from the center), Goode homolosine (equal area, interrupted). Do not write items
  that require a projection's name.
- **Map scale vs scale of analysis**: EK 1.6 covers scale of analysis (global, regional, national, local). Cartographic
  scale (ratio, large vs small scale) is not in the EK read; teach it briefly because "large scale" means a small area
  in cartography but students use it the other way. The scored skill is scale of analysis (category 5): identify the
  scale of the data in a source, then explain patterns across scales (CR 2025 set 2 Q3A).
- **Regions (1.7)**: formal, functional, perceptual/vernacular; 2026 Q3B accepted formal, functional or
  transitional/overlapping (SG).
- **Data sources (1.2, 6.9)**: census, surveys, satellite imagery, GIS, field work, narratives; quantitative vs
  qualitative.
