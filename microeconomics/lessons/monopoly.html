Unit 4 · Topic 4.2

Monopoly

A monopolist produces where MR = MC and charges the price on the demand curve above that quantity.

Practice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A drug company holds the patent on a new allergy test, so for the next twelve years no one else may sell it. It could charge $500, but few people would buy; at $20 everyone would, but it would barely cover costs. Where does a monopolist set its price, and who loses when it does?

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. The monopolist faces the whole market demand curve→its MR curve lies below demand
  2. It produces where MR = MC→its quantity (QM) is smaller than the quantity where demand meets MC
  3. It reads its price from demand at QM→the price is above marginal cost
  4. Units buyers value above their cost are not produced→there is a deadweight loss, the triangle between D and MC from QM to the efficient quantity

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Find Q where MR = MC, then go up to demand for P. Profit (or loss) is (P−ATC)×Q(P - \text{ATC}) \times Q at that Q.
  • A monopolist produces on the elastic part of demand (MR > 0). Total revenue is largest where MR = 0, the unit-elastic point.
  • Monopoly deadweight loss: the triangle between D and MC from QM to where D meets MC. Consumer surplus is under D and above PM, out to QM.
  • Natural monopoly: ATC falls over the whole range of demand. Socially optimal price P = MC (efficient, but a loss); fair-return price P = ATC (zero economic profit). A lump-sum tax raises ATC only: P and Q stay put.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A lump-sum tax on a monopolist raises its price, because the firm passes the tax on to buyers.

What actually happens: A lump-sum tax is a fixed cost: it raises ATC but not MC. MR = MC at the same quantity, the price on demand is the same, and the whole tax comes out of profit. A per-unit tax, which raises MC, is the one that raises the price.

A profit-maximizing monopoly, areas lettered

1. The graph shows a monopolist that maximizes profit. Which area is consumer surplus?

  1. Area a
  2. Area b
  3. Area c
  4. Area d
Show the answer

Consumer surplus lies under the demand curve and above the price the monopolist charges (read up on demand at the quantity where MR = MC), from zero to that quantity: area a.

  • Correct: Area a: Right: under D, above the monopoly price, out to the monopoly quantity.
  • Area b: Area b lies between the price and ATC: that is the economic profit.
  • Area c: Area c lies under ATC out to the quantity sold: that is total cost.
  • Area d: Area d lies between D and MC beyond the monopoly quantity: that is the deadweight loss.

2. Which area is the monopolist's economic profit?

  1. Area a
  2. Area b
  3. Areas b and c
  4. Area d
Show the answer

Profit is (P minus ATC) times Q: the rectangle between the price and ATC at the profit-maximizing quantity, from zero to that quantity. That is area b.

  • Area a: Area a lies above the price, under demand: it is consumer surplus.
  • Correct: Area b: Right: the rectangle between the price and ATC out to the quantity sold.
  • Areas b and c: Together b and c are price times quantity: total revenue, not profit.
  • Area d: Area d is the deadweight loss, the surplus no one gets.

3. Which area is the deadweight loss caused by the monopoly?

  1. Area a
  2. Area b
  3. Area d
  4. Areas b and d
Show the answer

Deadweight loss lies between the demand curve and MC, from the monopoly quantity (where MR = MC) to the quantity where D meets MC: area d. It is the value of units buyers would pay more for than they cost to make, which the monopolist does not produce.

  • Area a: Area a is surplus buyers still get; it is not lost.
  • Area b: Area b goes to the monopolist as profit; it is not lost.
  • Correct: Area d: Right: between D and MC, from the monopoly quantity to the efficient quantity.
  • Areas b and d: Area b is profit the firm keeps; only area d is lost to everyone.

6. Use the table. At its profit-maximizing output, what price does Quillon charge?

  1. $9, the price buyers will pay for 4 kits
  2. $6, where marginal revenue equals marginal cost
  3. $8, where the price equals marginal cost at 5 kits
  4. $7, the average total cost of 4 kits
Show the answer

At 4 kits, MR = MC = $6, but the price is read from demand: buyers will pay $9 for 4 kits a day.

  • Correct: $9, the price buyers will pay for 4 kits: Right: go up to demand at the quantity where MR = MC.
  • $6, where marginal revenue equals marginal cost: $6 is the height of MR and MC at 4 kits; the price comes from the demand curve above it.
  • $8, where the price equals marginal cost at 5 kits: P = MC is the efficient point; the monopolist stops at 4 kits.
  • $7, the average total cost of 4 kits: $7 is ATC. Charging ATC would leave zero economic profit.

7. The city replaces the lump-sum license fee with a per-unit tax of $1 on every unit the monopolist sells. What happens?

  1. Quantity falls and the price rises
  2. Quantity and price stay the same and profit falls
  3. Quantity rises and the price falls
  4. Price rises by the full $1 and quantity stays the same
Show the answer

A per-unit tax raises marginal cost, so MC crosses MR at a smaller quantity, and the price read from demand rises. (A lump-sum tax would leave MC, price and quantity unchanged.)

  • Correct: Quantity falls and the price rises: Right: MC shifts up, so MR = MC at a smaller quantity, higher on demand.
  • Quantity and price stay the same and profit falls: That is the effect of a lump-sum tax, which does not change MC.
  • Quantity rises and the price falls: A tax on each unit makes each unit more costly; the firm makes fewer.
  • Price rises by the full $1 and quantity stays the same: Quantity changes because MC changes; the price rise depends on demand.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

A monopolist produces where MR = MC and charges the price on the demand curve above that quantity. Its profit is (P minus ATC) times Q; it can last because barriers block entry. It produces less and charges more than a competitive industry would, on the elastic part of demand, leaving a deadweight loss between D and MC from its quantity to the efficient one. A natural monopoly, whose ATC falls over the whole range of demand, can be regulated at P = MC (efficient but a loss) or at the fair-return price P = ATC (zero economic profit). A lump-sum tax changes ATC and profit but not price or quantity.
