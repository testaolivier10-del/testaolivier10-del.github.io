Unit 6 · Topic 6.4

The Effects of Government Intervention in Different Market Structures

The same policy can have different effects in different market structures.

Practice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A rule that sets a highest legal price causes shortages in a market with many sellers. Put the same kind of rule on a single power company and it can do the opposite: more electricity sold, at a lower price. The effect of a policy depends on the market it lands in.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A monopoly sells more only by cutting its price on every unit→MR is below price, so it restricts output and P > MC
  2. A price ceiling is set where demand meets MC→each unit up to demand earns the ceiling price, so MR equals the ceiling
  3. The firm produces where MC meets that flat MR→output rises toward the efficient quantity and price falls
  4. For a natural monopoly, ATC is above MC at that point→the firm makes a loss, so the regulator subsidizes it or uses the fair-return price P = ATC

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • A price ceiling lowers quantity in a competitive market but can raise it for a monopoly, set between the monopoly price and the price where demand meets MC.
  • Natural monopoly regulation: the socially optimal price (P = MC) is efficient but leaves a loss; the fair-return price (P = ATC) gives a normal profit and less deadweight loss than no regulation.
  • Lump-sum taxes and subsidies change profit only. Per-unit ones change MC: a tax cuts a monopoly's output and raises its price; a subsidy does the opposite.
  • A minimum wage lowers employment in a competitive labor market but can raise it in a monopsony.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A price ceiling always causes a shortage and lowers the quantity sold.

What actually happens: That is true in a competitive market. For a monopoly, a ceiling below the monopoly price but at or above the price where demand meets MC removes the reason to restrict output, so the firm sells more at the lower price.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. A binding price ceiling lowers the quantity traded in a perfectly competitive market. Why can a price ceiling raise the quantity a monopoly sells?

  1. The ceiling raises the demand for the monopoly's good
  2. The ceiling lowers the monopoly's marginal cost
  3. A ceiling makes MR flat, so cutting output no longer raises price
  4. Monopolies can ignore price ceilings set below their own price
Show the answer

A monopoly restricts output because selling more means cutting the price on every unit. A ceiling removes that reason up to the demand curve.

  • The ceiling raises the demand for the monopoly's good: A ceiling does not shift demand.
  • The ceiling lowers the monopoly's marginal cost: Costs do not change; the firm's marginal revenue does.
  • Correct: A ceiling makes MR flat, so cutting output no longer raises price: Right: up to demand, each unit sold earns the ceiling price, so MR = ceiling, and the firm produces where MC meets it.
  • Monopolies can ignore price ceilings set below their own price: The firm must obey the ceiling; it changes the firm's best output.

2. Which statement describes the fair-return price for a regulated natural monopoly?

  1. Price equals MC, so output is allocatively efficient
  2. Price is where MR equals MC
  3. Price equals the minimum of ATC
  4. P = ATC, so the firm earns zero economic profit
Show the answer

Fair return: P = ATC. Socially optimal: P = MC. Unregulated: Q at MR = MC, P from demand.

  • Price equals MC, so output is allocatively efficient: That is the socially optimal price, which leaves a loss.
  • Price is where MR equals MC: That is the unregulated monopoly's choice.
  • Price equals the minimum of ATC: A natural monopoly's ATC is still falling where it meets demand.
  • Correct: P = ATC, so the firm earns zero economic profit: Right: the firm covers all costs, including a normal profit.

Show the answer

MR = MC = $2 at 4 thousand units. The price from demand is $6 and ATC is $5, so profit = (P - ATC) × Q = ($6 - $5) × 4 = 4 thousand dollars.

6. Use the table. What is the fair-return price?

  1. $2, where price equals MC
  2. $6, where MR equals MC
  3. $4, where price equals ATC
  4. $5, the ATC of the monopoly output
Show the answer

The fair-return price is where the demand curve meets ATC, so the firm earns zero economic profit.

  • $2, where price equals MC: That is the socially optimal price.
  • $6, where MR equals MC: That is the unregulated monopoly price.
  • Correct: $4, where price equals ATC: Right: at 6 thousand units the demand price and ATC are both $4.
  • $5, the ATC of the monopoly output: At $5 buyers want less than 6 thousand units, and the price does not match ATC on demand.

Scenario

Taxing a monopoly ferry

The Corvel Ferry Company (invented) is the only ferry to an island. It maximizes profit by carrying 50 riders a day at $12 each, for total revenue of $600 and total cost of $400. The island council considers two taxes: a lump-sum tax of $150 a day, or a tax of $2 for every rider carried.

7. In the scenario, why does the lump-sum tax leave Corvel's price and number of riders unchanged?

  1. It is too small to matter to the firm
  2. Riders pay the tax, not the firm
  3. A monopoly sets any price it wants regardless of costs
  4. It leaves MC unchanged, so MR = MC holds at 50 riders
Show the answer

Lump-sum taxes change profit, not the profit-maximizing quantity or price.

  • It is too small to matter to the firm: Size is not the reason; even a large lump-sum tax leaves MC unchanged.
  • Riders pay the tax, not the firm: The tax is a fixed charge on the firm.
  • A monopoly sets any price it wants regardless of costs: A monopoly picks its quantity where MR = MC; a change in MC would change it.
  • Correct: It leaves MC unchanged, so MR = MC holds at 50 riders: Right: a fixed amount owed whatever the output raises ATC but not MC.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The same policy can have different effects in different market structures. A price ceiling cuts output in a competitive market but can raise a monopoly's output by making its marginal revenue flat. Regulators of a natural monopoly choose between the socially optimal price (P = MC, efficient but with a loss that needs a subsidy) and the fair-return price (P = ATC, a normal profit). Lump-sum taxes and subsidies change only profit; per-unit ones change marginal cost and so output and price. A minimum wage lowers employment in a competitive labor market but can raise it in a monopsony.
