Unit 6 · Topic 6.3

Public and Private Goods

Goods differ in rivalry (does one person's use leave less for others?) and excludability (can nonpayers be kept out?).

Practice 1: Principles and Models

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Three households share a dark street. A streetlight would cost $70 a year, and together they would gladly pay more than that for it. Yet weeks go by and nobody pays, because whoever pays lights the street for the neighbors too. Each one waits for someone else.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A good is nonexcludable→people can enjoy it without paying
  2. People can enjoy it without paying→each waits for others to pay: free riding
  3. Few pay→a private seller cannot cover its cost, so the market provides too little or none
  4. The total benefit still exceeds the cost→the outcome is inefficient, and government often provides the good, paid for with taxes

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Rival: one person's use leaves less for others. Excludable: nonpayers can be kept out.
  • Private goods are rival and excludable; public goods nonrival and nonexcludable; common resources rival and nonexcludable; club goods nonrival and excludable.
  • Public goods suffer from the free-rider problem: markets underprovide them. A public good is worth providing when the sum of everyone's benefit is at least its cost.
  • Common resources are overused (the tragedy of the commons), because each user ignores the cost to others. Limits, permits or fees per unit reduce overuse.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A public good is any good the government provides.

What actually happens: A public good is defined by two features, nonrival and nonexcludable, not by who provides it. Governments provide many private goods (a public school lunch is rival), and some public goods are funded privately.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. A good is nonrival when

  1. no one can be kept from using it, paid or not
  2. one person's use leaves no less for others
  3. it is provided by the government
  4. its price is set at zero
Show the answer

Rival: my use leaves less for you. Nonrival: it does not.

  • no one can be kept from using it, paid or not: That is nonexcludable.
  • Correct: one person's use leaves no less for others: Right: many can use it at once, like a radio broadcast.
  • it is provided by the government: Who provides it does not define rivalry.
  • its price is set at zero: Price does not define rivalry.

2. A good is excludable when

  1. one person's use leaves less for others
  2. it is sold at a high price
  3. people who do not pay can be kept from using it
  4. it can be used by a single person in its lifetime
Show the answer

Excludability makes it possible to charge for a good.

  • one person's use leaves less for others: That is rivalry.
  • it is sold at a high price: Price alone does not exclude anyone who pays.
  • Correct: people who do not pay can be kept from using it: Right: a ticket gate or a login does this.
  • it can be used by a single person in its lifetime: That is not the definition; excludable is about keeping out nonpayers.

Scenario

Goods sorted by rivalry and excludability

An invented town, Thistlebrook, has: (1) slices of pizza sold at a stand; (2) a fireworks show over the harbor that anyone in town can see; (3) fish in a lake that anyone may fish; (4) a members-only online film library with a monthly fee and plenty of capacity.

3. In the scenario, which item is a public good?

  1. (1) the pizza
  2. (3) the fish
  3. (2) the fireworks show
  4. (4) the film library
Show the answer

A public good is both nonrival and nonexcludable.

  • (1) the pizza: A slice eaten by one person cannot be eaten by another, and the stand can refuse to sell: rival and excludable.
  • (3) the fish: A fish caught by one person is not there for another: rival, so it is a common resource.
  • Correct: (2) the fireworks show: Right: one more viewer does not reduce anyone's view (nonrival), and no one can be stopped from looking (nonexcludable).
  • (4) the film library: Nonpayers can be kept out with a login, so it is excludable: a club good.

4. In the scenario, which item is a common resource?

  1. (3) the fish in the lake
  2. (2) the fireworks show
  3. (4) the film library
  4. (1) the pizza
Show the answer

Common resources are rival and nonexcludable, so they tend to be overused.

  • Correct: (3) the fish in the lake: Right: rival (a fish caught is gone) but nonexcludable (anyone may fish).
  • (2) the fireworks show: Viewing is nonrival, so it is a public good.
  • (4) the film library: It is nonrival but excludable: a club good.
  • (1) the pizza: Pizza is excludable: it is a private good.

5. Why do private markets tend to provide too little of a public good?

  1. Free riders pay nothing, so sellers' revenue falls short
  2. Public goods cost more to produce than most private goods
  3. Each user's benefit is too large to measure
  4. Public goods are rival, so they run out
Show the answer

Because sellers cannot exclude nonpayers, the market quantity is below the efficient quantity. Governments often provide such goods and pay with taxes.

  • Correct: Free riders pay nothing, so sellers' revenue falls short: Right: nonexcludability lets people benefit without paying.
  • Public goods cost more to produce than most private goods: Cost is not what sets them apart.
  • Each user's benefit is too large to measure: The problem is collecting payment, not measuring benefit.
  • Public goods are rival, so they run out: Public goods are nonrival.

6. Which of these is a public good? Select all that apply.

  1. National defense
  2. A city bus ride paid by fare on a half-empty bus
  3. A seat on a crowded commuter train
  4. A ticketed concert in a stadium
  5. Groundwater pumped freely from a shared aquifer
Show the answer

A public good must be both nonrival and nonexcludable.

  • Correct: National defense: Right: nonrival and nonexcludable.
  • A city bus ride paid by fare on a half-empty bus: The fare box excludes nonpayers: at most a club good while the bus has room.
  • A seat on a crowded commuter train: Rival: one rider's seat is not available to another.
  • A ticketed concert in a stadium: Excludable: there is a ticket gate.
  • Groundwater pumped freely from a shared aquifer: Rival and nonexcludable: a common resource.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Goods differ in rivalry (does one person's use leave less for others?) and excludability (can nonpayers be kept out?). Private goods are rival and excludable and markets handle them well. Public goods are nonrival and nonexcludable: people can free ride, so markets provide too little, even when the sum of everyone's benefit exceeds the cost, and governments often provide them with taxes. Common resources are rival but nonexcludable, so they are overused. Club goods are excludable but nonrival.
