Curves That Cross and Areas

Where a downward curve and an upward curve cross, one price and one quantity lie on both curves: the intersection.

Practice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A box of strawberries sells for $5 at a farmers market because, at $5, the number of boxes shoppers want is the number farmers bring. On a graph that is the one point where the buyers' curve and the sellers' curve cross. Much of this course is finding that crossing point, seeing where it moves, and measuring the triangles and rectangles that form around it.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. A downward curve and an upward curve sit on the same graph→they cross at exactly one point
  2. You read the crossing point→you get one price and one quantity that lie on both curves
  3. One curve shifts→the crossing point slides along the curve that did not move to a new price and quantity
  4. You compare the old and new crossing points→you state which way the price and the quantity each changed

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The intersection (crossing point) of two curves is the one point on both. Read its price and quantity with dashed lines to both axes.
  • When one curve shifts, the crossing point slides along the curve that stayed put. Label the old point E1 and the new one E2.
  • Area of a rectangle = base × height. Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height.
  • Read a base along the horizontal axis and a height along the vertical axis, from the shape's own edges.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: The area of the triangle under D and above $5, out to 50 boxes, is 50 × 5 = 250.

What actually happens: That is the rectangle around the triangle. The triangle is half of it: ½ × 50 × 5 = 125.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What is true at the point where two curves on a graph cross?

  1. The two curves have the same slope there
  2. The price is at its highest value on the graph
  3. The point lies on both curves, so both share its price and quantity
  4. The quantity is 0
Show the answer

An intersection is the one point that sits on both curves at once, so both curves give the same price and quantity there.

  • The two curves have the same slope there: A downward curve and an upward curve have opposite slopes, even where they cross.
  • The price is at its highest value on the graph: The crossing point can be at any height.
  • Correct: The point lies on both curves, so both share its price and quantity: Right: one point, on both curves.
  • The quantity is 0: The crossing point usually sits well away from the vertical axis.

  1. $5 and 50 boxes

Show the answer

Follow the dashed lines from E: across to $5 on the price axis and down to 50 boxes on the quantity axis.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

Where a downward curve and an upward curve cross, one price and one quantity lie on both curves: the intersection. Read it with dashed lines to both axes. When one curve shifts, the crossing point slides along the other curve, and you compare the old and new points to say what happened to price and quantity. Areas on graphs are rectangles (base × height) and triangles (½ × base × height), with the base read on the horizontal axis and the height on the vertical axis.
