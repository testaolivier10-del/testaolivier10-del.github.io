Unit 4 · Topic 4.4

Monopolistic Competition

In monopolistic competition, many firms sell differentiated products and entry is easy.

Practice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A new ramen shop opens and has a line out the door for months. Then three more ramen shops open within a few blocks, each a little different. A year later the first shop is still busy, but its owner is earning only what she could earn elsewhere. Many sellers, slightly different products and easy entry: that is monopolistic competition.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Firms in the market earn economic profit→new firms with similar products enter (entry is easy)
  2. New firms take some of each firm's buyers→each firm's demand (and MR) shifts left and becomes more elastic
  3. Demand keeps shifting left→it becomes tangent to ATC, where P = ATC
  4. Economic profit is zero→entry stops; the firm produces where ATC is still falling, with excess capacity and P > MC

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, easy entry and exit. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve with MR below it.
  • Short run: produce where MR = MC, price from demand; profit or loss is (P−ATC)×Q(P - \text{ATC}) \times Q, as for a monopolist.
  • Long run: entry (or exit) shifts each firm's demand until it is tangent to ATC: P = ATC, zero economic profit.
  • At the tangency P > MC (not allocatively efficient) and output is below minimum ATC: excess capacity (not productively efficient). The trade: more variety.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: In the long run a monopolistically competitive firm ends up at the minimum of ATC, like a perfectly competitive firm.

What actually happens: Zero economic profit means P = ATC, and a downward-sloping demand curve can touch the U-shaped ATC only where ATC is still falling. So the firm produces less than the output at minimum ATC: excess capacity.

1. The graph shows a monopolistically competitive firm that maximizes profit in the short run. Which area is its economic profit?

  1. Area b
  2. Area a
  3. Area c
  4. Area d
Show the answer

At the quantity where MR = MC, the price read from demand is above ATC. Profit is the rectangle between the price and ATC, from zero to that quantity: area b.

  • Correct: Area b: Right: (P minus ATC) times Q.
  • Area a: Area a lies above the price, under demand: consumer surplus.
  • Area c: Area c is ATC times Q: total cost.
  • Area d: Area d, between demand and MC past the firm's quantity, is deadweight loss.

2. Entry into this market is easy. What happens next?

  1. New firms enter, and this firm's ATC shifts down until profit is zero
  2. Entry shifts this firm's demand left until profit is zero
  3. Firms exit, and this firm's demand shifts right
  4. Nothing changes, because the firm has market power
Show the answer

Economic profit draws new firms with similar products. Each existing firm loses some customers, so its demand (and MR) shifts left until demand is tangent to ATC and economic profit is zero.

  • New firms enter, and this firm's ATC shifts down until profit is zero: Entry takes away customers; it does not lower this firm's costs.
  • Correct: Entry shifts this firm's demand left until profit is zero: Right: entry takes customers until P = ATC.
  • Firms exit, and this firm's demand shifts right: Firms leave a market with losses, not one with profit.
  • Nothing changes, because the firm has market power: Its market power is small and entry is easy, so the profit is competed away.

3. Which area is the deadweight loss?

  1. Area b
  2. Areas b and d
  3. Area d
  4. Area a
Show the answer

The firm stops where MR = MC, short of the quantity where demand meets MC. The triangle between demand and MC over those missing units is the deadweight loss: area d.

  • Area b: Area b is the profit the firm keeps.
  • Areas b and d: Area b goes to the firm; it is not lost.
  • Correct: Area d: Right: between D and MC, from the firm's quantity to where D meets MC.
  • Area a: Area a is consumer surplus that buyers keep.

  1. Yes, because its price equals its average total cost
  2. Yes, because it earns zero economic profit

Allocative efficiency needs P = MC. Maple Row's price ($8) is above its marginal cost ($5), so buyers value one more sandwich more than it costs to make, and too few are made.

  • Yes, because its price equals its average total cost: P = ATC means zero economic profit; efficiency needs P = MC.
  • Yes, because it earns zero economic profit: Zero profit says nothing about whether P = MC.
  • No, because its price ($8) is below minimum ATC ($6.50): $8 is above $6.50; the gap from minimum ATC is about productive efficiency.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

In monopolistic competition, many firms sell differentiated products and entry is easy. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve, produces where MR = MC and reads its price from demand, so in the short run it can earn a profit or make a loss. Profit draws entry and losses cause exit, shifting each firm's demand until it is tangent to ATC: zero economic profit. At that point price is above marginal cost and output is below the minimum of ATC (excess capacity), the cost of the variety buyers enjoy.
