Unit 6 · Topic 6.2

Externalities

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside a market.

Practice 3: ManipulationPractice 4: Graphing and Visuals

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

A paper mill upriver makes paper that people pay for. It also releases fumes that make families downstream run air filters all summer. The mill pays for wood, workers and power, but not for the filters. Because that cost stays outside its books, the river town gets more paper, and more fumes, than is best.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Producing the good imposes a cost on people outside the market→MSC lies above the firms' MPC (supply)
  2. Firms decide using MPC only→the market produces where MPB = MPC, more than where MSB = MSC
  3. The units beyond the efficient quantity cost society more than they are worth→there is deadweight loss: a market failure
  4. A per-unit tax equal to the external cost is placed on the good→supply shifts up to MSC, and the market produces the efficient quantity

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside the deal. Production externalities show up on the supply side (MSC vs MPC); consumption externalities on the demand side (MSB vs MPB).
  • Negative externalities (MSC above MPC, or MSB below MPB) mean the market makes too much. Positive ones mean it makes too little.
  • Always state both quantities: the market at MPB = MPC, the efficient one at MSB = MSC. The deadweight loss triangle sits between them and points toward the efficient quantity.
  • A corrective (Pigouvian) tax or subsidy per unit equal to the external cost or benefit at the efficient quantity fixes it. A lump-sum tax does not change MC, price or quantity.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A polluting firm will cut its pollution if it has to pay a fixed yearly fee.

What actually happens: A fixed fee is a lump-sum tax: it raises total cost but not the cost of the next unit, so the firm's output, and the pollution that comes with it, stay the same. Only a charge per unit changes the quantity.

1. What is an externality?

  1. A good that is sold to buyers in other countries
  2. A cost that a firm pays to its outside suppliers
  3. A cost or benefit of a deal that falls on people outside it
  4. A benefit a buyer gets from a good she pays for
Show the answer

Because the third party is not part of the deal, the market ignores the effect, and the quantity is not efficient.

  • A good that is sold to buyers in other countries: That is an export.
  • A cost that a firm pays to its outside suppliers: A cost the firm pays itself is a private cost.
  • Correct: A cost or benefit of a deal that falls on people outside it: Right: a third party gains or loses without paying or being paid.
  • A benefit a buyer gets from a good she pays for: That is a private benefit, already in demand.

5. Use the table. What are the market quantity and the socially efficient quantity of paper?

  1. Market 40 tons; efficient 50 tons
  2. Market 50 tons; efficient 40 tons
  3. Market 50 tons; efficient 50 tons
  4. Market 60 tons; efficient 40 tons
Show the answer

The market uses private curves (MPB = MPC); efficiency uses social curves (MSB = MSC).

  • Market 40 tons; efficient 50 tons: The market ignores the fumes, so it makes more, not less.
  • Correct: Market 50 tons; efficient 40 tons: Right: MPB = MPC at 50 ($10), and MSB = MSC at 40 ($12).
  • Market 50 tons; efficient 50 tons: At 50 tons MSC ($14) is above MSB ($10).
  • Market 60 tons; efficient 40 tons: At 60 tons MPC ($12) is above MPB ($8), so the market stops sooner.

6. Use the table. What per-unit tax on paper would lead the market to the efficient quantity, in dollars per ton?

Type a number. A four-function calculator is allowed, as on the exam.

Show the answer

Set the tax equal to the external cost per unit at the efficient quantity: MSC - MPC at 40 tons = $12 - $8 = $4 per ton. Firms then face the full social cost.

7. A town charges a polluting factory a fixed fee of $50,000 a year, whatever it produces. What happens to the factory's output and to the deadweight loss?

  1. Output falls to the efficient quantity, removing the deadweight loss
  2. Output rises, because the factory makes more to cover the fee
  3. Output falls, but the deadweight loss rises
  4. Neither changes, because the fee does not change marginal cost
Show the answer

To correct an externality, the policy must change the marginal decision: a per-unit tax equal to the external cost. A lump-sum tax changes profit, not output.

  • Output falls to the efficient quantity, removing the deadweight loss: Only a per-unit tax changes the cost of the last unit and so the quantity.
  • Output rises, because the factory makes more to cover the fee: A fixed fee is paid whatever the output; it gives no reason to make more.
  • Output falls, but the deadweight loss rises: With MC unchanged, output does not change.
  • Correct: Neither changes, because the fee does not change marginal cost: Right: a lump-sum tax raises total and average cost, but not MC, so output stays where it was.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside a market. With a negative externality, the social cost exceeds the private cost (or the social benefit falls short of the private benefit), so the market, which uses private curves, produces more than the efficient quantity where MSB = MSC; with a positive externality it produces less. The deadweight loss is the triangle between the social and private curves from the market quantity to the efficient quantity. A per-unit tax or subsidy equal to the external cost or benefit at the efficient quantity moves the market there; a lump-sum tax does not, because it leaves marginal cost unchanged.
