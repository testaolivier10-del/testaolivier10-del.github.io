Unit 6 · Topic 6.5

Inequality

The Lorenz curve shows how income is shared: the cumulative percentage of income held by the cumulative percentage of households, from poorest to richest.

Practice 1: Principles and Models

Part 1 · Hook

Why this matters

Two invented countries have the same average income. In one, a nurse, a farmer and a bank manager live within a short walk of each other's standard of living. In the other, half of all income goes to the top fifth of households. An average hides the difference; a single curve shows it.

Part 5 · Step by step

How it works, step by step

  1. Households are ranked from lowest to highest income and their shares added up→each point gives the share of income held by the lowest x percent: the Lorenz curve
  2. Income is shared less equally→the curve bows further below the line of equality
  3. The area between the line of equality and the curve grows→the Gini coefficient, that area as a share of the triangle, rises
  4. Progressive taxes and transfer payments shift income toward lower-income households→the after-tax curve moves toward the line of equality

Part 6 · Key ideas

Key ideas

  • The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative percentage of income against the cumulative percentage of households, poorest first. The diagonal is the line of equality; the further the curve bows below it, the more unequal.
  • The Gini coefficient is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve as a share of the triangle under the line: 0 is perfect equality, 1 the most unequal.
  • Taxes are progressive, proportional or regressive as the share of income they take rises, stays the same or falls with income. Transfer payments give money with no good or service in return.
  • Sources of inequality include differences in skills and training, inherited wealth, discrimination, market power and luck. Income is a yearly flow; wealth is a stock of assets.

Part 7 · Misconception

A common mistake

The wrong idea: A tax is progressive whenever higher-income households pay more dollars of tax.

What actually happens: Look at the share of income, not the dollars. A tax that takes 5 percent from everyone has richer households paying more dollars, but it is proportional. Only a share that rises with income is progressive.

Part 8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Exam-style questions. Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. What does a Lorenz curve show?

  1. How one household's income changes over the course of its lifetime
  2. The tax rate paid at each level of income
  3. Cumulative income shares held by cumulative shares of households
  4. The number of households at each level of income
Show the answer

If every household had the same income, the Lorenz curve would be the diagonal line of equality.

  • How one household's income changes over the course of its lifetime: It is a snapshot across households, not over time for one household.
  • The tax rate paid at each level of income: Tax rates are shown in a tax table; the curve shows shares of income.
  • Correct: Cumulative income shares held by cumulative shares of households: Right: each point says what percent of income the lowest x percent of households hold.
  • The number of households at each level of income: That would be a bar chart of incomes, not cumulative shares.

Show the answer

4. Use the table. At 40 percent of households, how far below the line of equality is Velmora's Lorenz curve?

  1. 32 percentage points: 40 against 8
  2. 0 points: the curve meets the line there
  3. 29 points: 40 on the line, 11 on the curve
  4. 15 percentage points: 40 against 25
Show the answer

On the line of equality each 20 percent of households holds 20 percent of income. The gap shows how much less the lower groups hold.

  • 32 percentage points: 40 against 8: 8 is the second fifth alone; the curve is cumulative, 3 + 8 = 11.
  • 0 points: the curve meets the line there: The curve meets the line only at 0 and 100 percent here.
  • Correct: 29 points: 40 on the line, 11 on the curve: Right: the lowest two fifths hold 3 + 8 = 11 percent, against 40 percent with equal shares.
  • 15 percentage points: 40 against 25: The lowest two fifths hold 11 percent, not 25.

5. Use the table. What kind of tax is Plan A?

  1. Progressive: higher incomes pay more dollars of tax
  2. Regressive: lower incomes pay a larger share
  3. Lump-sum: each household pays a fixed amount
  4. Proportional: every household pays 5 percent of its income
Show the answer

Classify a tax by the share of income it takes (the average tax rate), not by the dollar amount.

  • Progressive: higher incomes pay more dollars of tax: Paying more dollars is not enough; the share of income must rise with income.
  • Regressive: lower incomes pay a larger share: Each household pays the same 5 percent.
  • Lump-sum: each household pays a fixed amount: The amounts differ by income.
  • Correct: Proportional: every household pays 5 percent of its income: Right: $1,000 of $20,000, $2,500 of $50,000 and $5,000 of $100,000 are each 5 percent.

6. Which statement describes a progressive tax?

  1. It takes the same share of income from everyone
  2. It takes a larger share from lower incomes
  3. It charges each household the same dollar amount
  4. It takes a larger share of income as income rises
Show the answer

Progressive, proportional and regressive describe how the share of income taken changes as income rises.

  • It takes the same share of income from everyone: That is a proportional tax.
  • It takes a larger share from lower incomes: That is a regressive tax.
  • It charges each household the same dollar amount: That is a lump-sum tax, which is regressive as a share of income.
  • Correct: It takes a larger share of income as income rises: Right: the average tax rate rises with income.

7. The Gini coefficient of one invented country rises from 0.30 to 0.40. What does that mean?

  1. Income became more equally distributed
  2. Income became less equally distributed
  3. Total income rose by 10 percent
  4. The tax system became more progressive
Show the answer

The Gini coefficient runs from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one household has all the income).

  • Income became more equally distributed: A lower Gini coefficient would mean more equality.
  • Correct: Income became less equally distributed: Right: a higher Gini coefficient means a larger area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
  • Total income rose by 10 percent: The Gini coefficient measures how income is shared, not how much there is.
  • The tax system became more progressive: That would usually lower after-tax inequality, not raise it.

Part 9 · Summary

Summary

The Lorenz curve shows how income is shared: the cumulative percentage of income held by the cumulative percentage of households, from poorest to richest. The diagonal line of equality means equal shares, and the further the curve bows below it, the more unequal the distribution. The Gini coefficient measures that gap as a share of the area under the line. Taxes are progressive, proportional or regressive by how their share of income changes with income, and progressive taxes and transfer payments move the after-tax Lorenz curve toward equality. Inequality comes from differences in skills, wealth, discrimination, market power and luck.
