AP® Microeconomics

Graphs and formulas you must know

The exam gives you no formula sheet. These are the calculations and the graphs it expects you to know, in our own words: what each means, when to use it, and the trap that costs points. The ones students most often get wrong are flagged "Easy to forget".

Easy to forget

These 10 formulas and 7 graphs cost the most points every year. Each is flagged below.

  • Opportunity cost per unit, read off a PPC or an output table. Comparative advantage goes to the lower opportunity cost, not the larger output (that is absolute advantage).
  • Marginal utility per dollar. Compare MU divided by price, not MU alone, and stop when the budget runs out.
  • The price elasticity of demand. Quantity on top, price on the bottom; read the absolute value: above 1 is elastic, below 1 inelastic.
  • The cross-price elasticity of demand. Positive means substitutes, negative means complements; the price is the other good's.
  • Consumer surplus under a straight demand curve. A triangle needs the one half; use the quantity actually bought.
  • What a per-unit subsidy costs the government. The whole rectangle between the price sellers receive and the price buyers pay, out to the new, larger quantity.
  • Deadweight loss, a triangle. Between demand (MSB) and supply (MC, MSC), from the quantity traded to the efficient quantity; do not forget the one half.
  • Marginal cost. Divide the change in total cost by the change in output, which is not always 1.
  • Average fixed cost. Divide fixed cost by output, not by workers; TFC is the same at every output, including zero.
  • Marginal revenue product. Marginal product times marginal revenue, never MR times P.
  • Supply and demand. The tax shifts supply up by the tax. Tax revenue b + d on the quantity after the tax (buyers pay b, sellers d), deadweight loss c + e.
  • Supply and demand. The subsidy shifts supply down by the subsidy. Its cost is the whole rectangle b + c + e + f + g out to the new quantity; deadweight loss g.
  • Cost curves. MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minimums; the gap between ATC and AVC (AFC) narrows as output grows.
  • Perfect competition: market and firm. Market on the left, firm on the right, the price carried across. The firm's demand is horizontal: d = MR = AR = P; it produces where MR = MC. Profit is area a.
  • Monopoly. Quantity where MR = MC, price up on demand. Consumer surplus a, profit b, deadweight loss d between demand and MC.
  • Monopsony. Hire where MRP = MFC, then read the wage down on labor supply, not on MRP.
  • Externality. A negative production externality: MSC above MPC, so the market overproduces. Deadweight loss is area a, from the efficient to the market quantity.

The graphs you must draw

Label every axis and curve exactly as here, show each shift with a new curve (D2) and the new equilibrium, and shade the whole area a question names.

Habits that save points

  • Label everything. Every axis, every curve and every point the question names (PM, QM, PF, QF). A mislabeled graph loses the graph point and usually the next one. Axes, Variables and Points
  • MC through the minimums. Draw MC so it crosses AVC and ATC at their lowest points, every time. Short-Run Production Costs
  • Price from demand. In monopoly and monopolistic competition, find the quantity where MR = MC, then go straight up to the demand curve for the price. Monopoly
  • Shade the whole area. Partial shading loses the point; shade the full triangle or rectangle the question names. Curves That Cross and Areas
  • Both comparisons. A dominant-strategy claim needs both comparisons from the matrix, with the numbers. Oligopoly and Game Theory
  • Show your work. "Calculate" needs the formula or the numbers used, not just the answer. Short-Run Production Costs

On exam day

  • Four-function calculator. Bluebook has one built in (add, subtract, multiply, divide, square root, percent) for both sections; no scientific or graphing calculator.
  • No formula sheet. Nothing on this page is given on exam day.
  • Graphs by hand. Free responses are handwritten in a paper booklet, graphs drawn by hand.
