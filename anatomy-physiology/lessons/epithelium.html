Epithelial tissue

A&P IanatomyRead the notes

1Why this matters

Dana, 52, has had burning acid reflux most nights for ten years. A camera passed down her esophagus shows a salmon-pink patch near her stomach. A tiny sample shows the usual many-layered lining of flat cells has been replaced there by a single layer of tall cells with mucus-filled cells among them. Her doctor calls it Barrett esophagus and schedules regular checks, because the new lining is more likely to turn cancerous. To see why acid would swap one lining for another, you need to know what each kind of epithelium is built to do.

5How it works, step by step

  1. Epithelium has no blood vessels of its own (it is avascular).→Its cells get oxygen and nutrients only by diffusion from capillaries below the basement membrane.
  2. Diffusion is fast only over short distances.→In a thick stratified squamous epithelium, the deepest cells, closest to the capillaries, are the best supplied. They are also the layer that divides: the cells anchored on the basement membrane.
  3. The deep cells divide.→Their daughter cells are pushed toward the surface, farther from the supply.
  4. In keratinized epithelium, the rising cells fill with keratin and die by a built-in program.→The surface becomes a tough, dry layer of dead cells that flakes off and is replaced from below.

7A common mistake

The wrong idea: Pseudostratified epithelium has several layers of cells, because you can see several rows of nuclei.

What actually happens: Every cell in pseudostratified epithelium touches the basement membrane, so it is a single layer. The cells are different heights, and only the tall ones reach the surface, so their nuclei sit at different levels. That is why it only looks stratified (pseudo- = false). In a truly stratified epithelium, the upper cells rest on other cells and never touch the basement membrane.

8Check yourself

Anything you miss goes into your review queue.

1. An epithelial cell's apical surface faces which direction?

  1. Toward the basement membrane
  2. Toward the capillaries that supply it
  3. Toward a lumen or the outside of the body
  4. Toward neighboring cells along its sides
Show the answer

The apical surface (apex = tip) is the free face of an epithelial cell. It looks into a lumen, such as the inside of the trachea, or out to the environment. Microvilli and cilia sit on this face.

  • Toward the basement membrane: The face on the basement membrane is the basal surface, the opposite of the apical surface.
  • Toward the capillaries that supply it: The capillaries lie below the basement membrane, on the basal side. Nutrients reach the cell through its basal surface.
  • Correct: Toward a lumen or the outside of the body: Correct. The apical surface faces the open space or the outside world.
  • Toward neighboring cells along its sides: The sides of the cell face neighbors and carry the tight junctions and desmosomes. They are the lateral surfaces, not the apical surface.

2. Under a microscope you see one layer of flat cells lining a small blood vessel. What is this tissue called?

  1. Mesothelium
  2. Transitional epithelium
  3. Simple cuboidal epithelium
  4. Endothelium
Show the answer

One layer of flat cells is simple squamous epithelium. The simple squamous lining of the heart and blood vessels has its own name: endothelium (endo- = within).

  • Mesothelium: Mesothelium is also simple squamous, but it is the surface layer of a serous membrane such as the pleura, not a vessel lining.
  • Transitional epithelium: Transitional epithelium is many layers thick and lines the urinary passages, not blood vessels.
  • Simple cuboidal epithelium: Cuboidal cells are about as tall as they are wide. The cells here are flat.
  • Correct: Endothelium: Correct. Endothelium is the simple squamous lining of the heart and blood vessels.

3. In a slide of airway lining, nuclei sit at three different heights, but every cell touches the basement membrane. How should you classify it?

  1. Ciliated stratified columnar epithelium
  2. Pseudostratified columnar epithelium
  3. Transitional epithelium
  4. Simple cuboidal epithelium
Show the answer

The test for layers is contact with the basement membrane. If every cell touches it, the epithelium is one layer. Cells of different heights put their nuclei at different levels, so the sheet looks stratified but is not.

  • Ciliated stratified columnar epithelium: Rows of nuclei are not layers of cells. In a stratified epithelium, the upper cells do not touch the basement membrane, and here every cell does.
  • Correct: Pseudostratified columnar epithelium: Correct. Every cell touching the basement membrane means one layer; the varied heights make it look layered.
  • Transitional epithelium: Transitional epithelium is truly stratified and lines the urinary passages. Its surface cells do not touch the basement membrane.
  • Simple cuboidal epithelium: The name follows the cells that reach the surface. Here those cells are tall, so the tissue is columnar.

4. A long-term smoker coughs up mucus every morning. A biopsy shows his tracheal lining has lost its cilia in many places. Which process has failed?

  1. Diffusion of oxygen across the tracheal lining
  2. Sweeping of mucus up and out of the airway
  3. Release of mucus by goblet cells
  4. Stretching of the airway lining during breathing
Show the answer

In respiratory epithelium, goblet cells release mucus that traps particles, and cilia beat in waves that carry the mucus toward the throat. Without cilia, the mucus pools, and coughing becomes the only way to clear it.

  • Diffusion of oxygen across the tracheal lining: Gas exchange happens across the thin lining of the air sacs, not across the trachea.
  • Correct: Sweeping of mucus up and out of the airway: Correct. Cilia move the mucus sheet; lose them and mucus builds up.
  • Release of mucus by goblet cells: Goblet cells still make mucus, often more of it in smokers. The problem is moving it, not making it.
  • Stretching of the airway lining during breathing: The trachea is a fairly rigid tube and does not rely on stretchy epithelium. Transitional epithelium, which stretches, lines the urinary passages.

5. A slide of an empty urinary bladder shows a lining six cells thick with rounded, dome-shaped surface cells. The same tissue from a full bladder looks only two or three cells thick with flat surface cells. What explains the difference?

  1. The surface cells flake off as the bladder fills
  2. The full bladder sample came from another region
  3. The cells rearrange and flatten as the wall stretches
  4. Cells in the full bladder lose water to the urine
Show the answer

Transitional epithelium stretches. As the bladder fills, its cells slide past one another and flatten, so the sheet thins without tearing. When the bladder empties, the cells round up again.

  • The surface cells flake off as the bladder fills: Losing cells every time the bladder filled would strip the lining. The cells stay in place and change shape.
  • The full bladder sample came from another region: The ureters, bladder and first part of the urethra are all lined by the same transitional epithelium. The change comes from stretch.
  • Correct: The cells rearrange and flatten as the wall stretches: Correct. The cells flatten and rearrange as the wall stretches.
  • Cells in the full bladder lose water to the urine: The surface cells seal tightly and block water movement between urine and the wall. The change is in shape, not water content.

6. Which lining would you expect in a kidney tubule, where cells pull salts and nutrients back out of the fluid passing through by active transport?

  1. Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium
  2. Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium
  3. Transitional epithelium
  4. Simple cuboidal epithelium
Show the answer

Active transport needs ATP and many transport proteins. Cube-shaped cells have room for mitochondria and other organelles, and microvilli add membrane area. One layer keeps the path short.

  • Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium: A dry, dead, many-layered surface would block transport. It suits the skin, not a tubule.
  • Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium: This lining moves mucus with cilia in the airways. It does not do the absorbing a tubule needs.
  • Transitional epithelium: Transitional epithelium stretches and seals in urine; it is not built for absorbing.
  • Correct: Simple cuboidal epithelium: Correct. Simple cuboidal cells, many with microvilli, line the kidney tubules.

9Summary

Your body has four primary tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous. Epithelium is a sheet of tightly joined cells with an apical surface facing a lumen or the outside and a basal surface on a basement membrane. It has no blood vessels, so it depends on diffusion from below. You name an epithelium by its layers (simple or stratified) and the shape of its surface cells (squamous, cuboidal or columnar). Thin simple squamous sheets handle diffusion, cuboidal and columnar cells secrete and absorb, pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium sweeps the airways, stratified squamous epithelium resists wear, and transitional epithelium stretches.
