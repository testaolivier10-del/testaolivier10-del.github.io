Newborn adjustments and lactation

A&P IIphysiologyRead the notes

1Why this matters

Baby Lina is born at 30 weeks. Within minutes she is breathing 80 times a minute and grunting, and the skin between her ribs sinks in with every breath. The team slips a small mask over her nose to hold her airways under steady pressure and gives surfactant down a tube into her trachea. Two floors up, a full-term baby born the same hour has already cried, turned pink and latched onto his mother's breast. Both babies face the same tasks in their first days: open the lungs, reroute the blood, stay warm and start feeding. This page explains how each happens, and what goes wrong when one does not.

5How it works, step by step

  1. At birth, rising CO2, falling O2, cold, touch and noise drive the brainstem breathing centers.→The baby takes its first breaths; air opens the alveoli, and surfactant keeps them from collapsing completely after each breath.
  2. Air and oxygen in the lungs relax the pulmonary arterioles.→Pulmonary vascular resistance falls, lung blood flow rises about eightfold, and far more blood returns to the left atrium.
  3. Clamping the cord removes the low-resistance placenta and stops return through the umbilical vein.→Systemic resistance and aortic pressure rise, and right atrial pressure falls below left.
  4. Left atrial pressure exceeds right, and aortic pressure exceeds pulmonary pressure.→The foramen ovale is pressed shut, and flow through the ductus arteriosus reverses.
  5. Blood oxygen rises and prostaglandin E2 falls.→The ductus arteriosus constricts within days and later becomes the ligamentum arteriosum, leaving the two circuits in series.

7A common mistake

The wrong idea: Prolactin makes milk flow out of the breast when the baby suckles.

What actually happens: Two hormones do two jobs. Prolactin, released when suckling lowers dopamine, makes the alveolar cells produce milk for the next feed. Oxytocin, released through the milk ejection reflex, contracts the myoepithelial cells and squeezes out the milk already made, within a minute. A mother can have full breasts yet no flow if stress blocks oxytocin, and a drug that acts like dopamine stops production without touching ejection.

1. A term baby takes its first breaths, and the cord is clamped. Predict the change in each variable over the first few minutes, compared with just before birth.

VariableChange
Pulmonary vascular resistance—
Blood flow through the lungs—
Left atrial pressure—
Right atrial pressure—
Right-to-left flow through the foramen ovale—
Oxygen in the baby's arterial blood—
Show the answer

Breathing lowers pulmonary resistance, and clamping the cord raises systemic resistance and cuts umbilical return. Left-sided pressures rise above right, which closes the foramen ovale and reverses flow in the ductus arteriosus, while oxygen from the lungs raises arterial oxygen.

  • Pulmonary vascular resistance: down. Air stretches the lungs open, and rising oxygen relaxes the pulmonary arterioles, helped by nitric oxide.
  • Blood flow through the lungs: up. With resistance falling, far more of the right ventricle's output passes through the lungs instead of the ductus arteriosus.
  • Left atrial pressure: up. Much more blood returns from the lungs through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium.
  • Right atrial pressure: down. Clamping the cord stops the flow returning from the placenta through the umbilical vein, so less blood enters the right atrium.
  • Right-to-left flow through the foramen ovale: down. Left atrial pressure now exceeds right, which pushes the septum primum against the opening and closes it.
  • Oxygen in the baby's arterial blood: up. Blood now picks up oxygen in the lungs, and the shunts that mixed oxygen-poor blood into the aorta close.

2. A week after birth, a baby's ductus arteriosus is still open. Predict the change in each variable, compared with a baby whose ductus has closed.

VariableChange
Blood flow through the lungs—
Volume returning to the left atrium and ventricle—
Aortic diastolic pressure—
Pulse pressure—
Show the answer

After birth the pressures are reversed, so an open ductus shunts blood left to right, from the aorta into the lungs. The lungs and left heart carry extra volume, and runoff from the aorta in diastole widens the pulse pressure.

  • Blood flow through the lungs: up. Aortic pressure is now higher than pulmonary pressure, so blood flows from the aorta through the ductus into the pulmonary trunk and adds to the right ventricle's output.
  • Volume returning to the left atrium and ventricle: up. The extra blood sent to the lungs returns through the pulmonary veins to the left heart.
  • Aortic diastolic pressure: down. During diastole, blood keeps draining from the aorta into the lower-pressure pulmonary circuit through the ductus.
  • Pulse pressure: up. Diastolic pressure falls while the larger stroke volume keeps systolic pressure up, so the gap widens and pulses feel bounding.

3. What closes the foramen ovale within minutes to hours of birth?

  1. Fibrous tissue grows across the opening
  2. Rising oxygen makes a ring of smooth muscle around the opening contract
  3. Left atrial pressure rises above right and presses the flap shut
  4. Falling prostaglandin E2 lets the opening constrict
Show the answer

More blood returns from the lungs to the left atrium, and less returns to the right atrium once the cord is clamped. The higher left pressure pushes the septum primum against the rim of the opening, like a door pushed shut. Fusion comes months later.

  • Fibrous tissue grows across the opening: Fusion of the flap to the wall happens over months, and in about one adult in four never happens. The quick closure is a pressure effect.
  • Rising oxygen makes a ring of smooth muscle around the opening contract: Oxygen-driven contraction closes the ductus arteriosus, a muscular vessel. The foramen ovale is a flap valve with no ring of muscle.
  • Correct: Left atrial pressure rises above right and presses the flap shut: Correct. A pressure reversal between the atria presses the flap closed.
  • Falling prostaglandin E2 lets the opening constrict: Falling prostaglandin E2 helps close the ductus arteriosus, not the foramen ovale.

4. A baby born at 29 weeks begins breathing 80 times a minute within an hour of birth. She grunts with each breath, her nostrils flare, and the skin between her ribs sinks in as she breathes in. What is the most likely cause?

  1. Too little surfactant, so alveoli collapse after each breath
  2. An open ductus arteriosus flooding the lungs with extra blood
  3. Aspiration of amniotic fluid stained with meconium before birth
  4. Cold stress from being left wet
Show the answer

At 29 weeks, the type II cells have not yet made enough surfactant. Surface tension collapses alveoli at the end of each breath, so every breath must reopen them: respiratory distress syndrome.

  • Correct: Too little surfactant, so alveoli collapse after each breath: Correct. Surfactant shortage causes respiratory distress syndrome in preterm babies.
  • An open ductus arteriosus flooding the lungs with extra blood: An open ductus can add to lung problems later, but distress within the first hour in a very preterm baby points first to surfactant shortage.
  • Aspiration of amniotic fluid stained with meconium before birth: Meconium in the fluid is a problem of term and post-term babies under stress, not of a baby born at 29 weeks.
  • Cold stress from being left wet: Cold stress raises oxygen use and can worsen breathing, but it does not cause grunting and collapsing alveoli by itself.

5. At 1 minute after birth, a baby's heart rate is 90 a minute, breathing is slow and irregular, the limbs are partly bent, the baby grimaces when the feet are flicked, and the body is pink with blue hands and feet. What is the Apgar score?

  1. 5
  2. 7
  3. 4
  4. 6
Show the answer

Each sign scores 1: color (body pink, hands and feet blue), pulse (below 100), grimace (a grimace only), activity (some bending) and respiration (slow, irregular). The total is 5, a moderately abnormal score.

  • Correct: 5: Correct. Five signs at 1 point each gives 5.
  • 7: A 7 would need two signs scoring 2, for example a heart rate of 100 or more and a strong cry.
  • 4: A 4 would need one sign scoring 0, such as absent breathing or no response to stimulation.
  • 6: A 6 would need one sign scoring 2, for example a heart rate of 100 or more.

6. A newborn is left wet and uncovered under a cold air vent for 20 minutes. Its temperature falls, and a blood test then shows low glucose. What links the cold to the low glucose?

  1. Cold makes the newborn shiver hard, and muscle burns the glucose
  2. Cold stops the liver from releasing glucose
  3. Sympathetic nerves make brown fat burn fuel, using oxygen and glucose
  4. Cold stimulates insulin release
Show the answer

Cold skin signals the hypothalamus, sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine onto brown fat, and the brown fat burns fuel in mitochondria that release the energy as heat. That nonshivering thermogenesis uses oxygen and glucose fast, so a cold baby can become low in glucose and oxygen.

  • Cold makes the newborn shiver hard, and muscle burns the glucose: Newborns shiver weakly and get little heat from it. Their main heat source is brown fat.
  • Cold stops the liver from releasing glucose: Sympathetic activity in cold raises glucose release from the liver; it does not stop it. The fall comes from use outrunning supply.
  • Correct: Sympathetic nerves make brown fat burn fuel, using oxygen and glucose: Correct. Nonshivering thermogenesis costs oxygen and glucose.
  • Cold stimulates insulin release: Cold and sympathetic activity hold insulin release down, not up.

7. Five days after birth, Ms. Ruiz's milk has still not come in, and she has had heavy bleeding. An ultrasound shows a piece of placenta left in her uterus. How does the retained tissue explain the delay?

  1. It keeps progesterone high, which blocks prolactin's action on milk cells
  2. It releases dopamine, which blocks prolactin
  3. It uses up the oxytocin that would make milk
  4. Bleeding has lowered her blood volume too far to make milk
Show the answer

Full milk production starts when progesterone falls after the placenta is delivered. Retained placental tissue keeps making progesterone, which keeps blocking prolactin's action, so the milk does not come in until the tissue is removed.

  • Correct: It keeps progesterone high, which blocks prolactin's action on milk cells: Correct. Placental progesterone keeps the brake on milk production.
  • It releases dopamine, which blocks prolactin: Dopamine comes from the hypothalamus, not the placenta.
  • It uses up the oxytocin that would make milk: Oxytocin ejects milk; it does not make it. Prolactin, blocked by progesterone, is what is missing here.
  • Bleeding has lowered her blood volume too far to make milk: Heavy bleeding can slow milk production, but the retained tissue explains the delay directly, through progesterone.

8. A mother anxious and in pain after a difficult birth finds that her full breasts do not release milk when her baby suckles. Once her pain is controlled and she relaxes, milk flows. What was blocked?

  1. Oxytocin release, and so milk ejection
  2. Prolactin release from the anterior pituitary
  3. Milk production by the alveolar cells
  4. Dopamine release from the hypothalamus
Show the answer

Her breasts were full, so milk had been made. Pain, fear and stress inhibit oxytocin release through the hypothalamus, so the myoepithelial cells did not contract and the milk was not ejected.

  • Correct: Oxytocin release, and so milk ejection: Correct. Stress blocks the oxytocin that ejects milk.
  • Prolactin release from the anterior pituitary: Prolactin makes milk, and her breasts were already full. The problem was getting the milk out.
  • Milk production by the alveolar cells: Full breasts show that the alveolar cells had been making milk.
  • Dopamine release from the hypothalamus: Less dopamine would raise prolactin; blocking dopamine would not stop milk from flowing.

9Summary

At birth the lungs, already draining, open with a hard first breath, and surfactant keeps them open. Expanded, oxygenated lungs lower pulmonary resistance while clamping the cord raises systemic resistance, so left-sided pressures rise above right: the foramen ovale is pressed shut, and the ductus arteriosus constricts as oxygen rises and prostaglandin E2 falls, becoming the ligamentum arteriosum. A patent ductus shunts blood from the aorta into the lungs. Surfactant shortage causes respiratory distress syndrome in preterm babies. The Apgar score rates color, heart rate, reflex response, tone and breathing at 1 and 5 minutes. Newborns pass meconium within two days and keep warm mainly by burning brown fat. After the placenta is delivered, falling progesterone lets milk come in; suckling lowers dopamine so prolactin makes the next feed, and releases oxytocin, which ejects milk now. Colostrum is rich in IgA, which gives passive immunity to the baby's gut.
