abdominal quadrants
The four areas made by a midsagittal line and a transverse line crossing at the navel: right upper, left upper, right lower and left lower, named for the patient's own sides. quadrans (Latin) a fourth part Taught in Body regions and abdominal quadrants.
abdominopelvic cavity (ab-DOM-ih-noh-PEL-vik)
The part of the ventral body cavity below the diaphragm: the abdominal cavity above and the pelvic cavity below, with only an imaginary boundary between them. abdomin/o abdomen · pelv/i basin, pelvis Taught in Body cavities and serous membranes.
abdominopelvic regions (hy-poh-KON-dree-ak, ep-ih-GAS-trik)
The nine areas made by two vertical and two horizontal lines across the abdomen: right and left hypochondriac with the epigastric between them, right and left lumbar with the umbilical between them, and right and left iliac with the hypogastric between them. hypo- below · chondr/o cartilage · epi- upon, over · gastr/o stomach · umbilicus (Latin) navel Taught in Body regions and abdominal quadrants.
abnormal filtrate (pro-teen-YOOR-ee-uh; hee-muh-TYOOR-ee-uh)
Filtrate containing what a healthy filter holds back. Proteinuria is protein, usually albumin, in the urine above the normal trace; hematuria is red blood cells in the urine, from the glomeruli or anywhere further down the urinary tract. -uria in the urine · hemat- blood Taught in Glomerular filtration.
ABO blood groups
The blood group system set by sugars added to the H antigen on red cells: type A carries the A antigen, type B the B antigen, type AB both, type O neither. From infancy, plasma holds antibodies against whichever of A and B the red cells lack. Taught in Blood types and transfusion.
absolute and relative refractory periods
The absolute refractory period is the time, about 1 ms from threshold through most of the falling phase, when no stimulus can start another action potential. The relative refractory period follows it, when only a stronger-than-normal stimulus can. refractarius stubborn (Latin) · absolutus complete (Latin) Taught in The action potential in detail.
absorptive state
The fed state, about four hours after a meal, when nutrients are being absorbed. Insulin is high and glucagon low, glucose is the main fuel, and the liver, muscle and adipose tissue store the extra as glycogen, protein and fat. ab- away, from · sorb- suck in Taught in Fed and fasting states.
accessory structures of the eye (LEE-vay-tor PAL-peh-bree soo-peer-ee-OR-iss)
The parts around the eyeball that protect and move it: the eyelids, the conjunctiva lining the lids and covering the white of the eye, the lacrimal gland and the ducts that drain tears into the nose, and the six extraocular (extrinsic eye) muscles. The lateral rectus is supplied by the abducens nerve, the superior oblique by the trochlear nerve, and the other four and the levator palpebrae superioris by the oculomotor nerve. palpebr- eyelid · lacrim- tear · extra- outside · ocul- eye · rectus straight Taught in Vision.
acid
A substance that releases hydrogen ions (H+) when it dissolves in water, raising the H+ concentration. A strong acid releases nearly all of them; a weak acid releases only a small share. acid- sour Taught in Acids, bases, pH and buffers.
acidosis and alkalosis (definitions) (as-ih-DOE-sis; al-kuh-LOE-sis)
Acidemia is blood pH below 7.35 and alkalemia is blood pH above 7.45. Acidosis and alkalosis are the processes that push pH down or up; in everyday speech the words are often used for the blood state itself. -osis condition, process · -emia blood condition Taught in Acids, bases, pH and buffers.
action potential
A rapid, all-or-none reversal of the membrane potential: sodium rushes in (to about +30 mV), then potassium flows out, returning the membrane to rest. It travels along the membrane without shrinking; in a neuron it is also called a nerve impulse. Taught in Electrical signals: graded potentials and action potentials.
active and passive immunity
Active immunity is made by your own immune system, after infection or a vaccine; it is slow to start, leaves memory and lasts long. Passive immunity is borrowed antibody, from mother to baby or by injection; it works at once but fades in weeks to months. vacc- cow (from cowpox) · immun- exempt Taught in B cells and antibodies.
active transport
Movement of a substance across a membrane that requires the cell to supply energy, usually from ATP. It usually moves the substance against its gradient; vesicle transport also counts. activus doing, acting Taught in Active transport and vesicles.
ADH and aquaporins in the collecting duct
ADH binds its receptor protein on principal cells, raises cAMP and activates protein kinase A, which moves stored aquaporins into the apical membrane. Water then leaves the collecting duct by osmosis into the salty medulla; without ADH the duct is nearly waterproof. anti- against · diuresis passing urine · aqua- water · por- pore, passage Taught in Concentrating the urine.
ADH in blood pressure control (vay-zoh-PRESS-in)
The role of antidiuretic hormone in blood pressure: it makes the kidneys keep water, and at the high levels a large fall in volume produces, it also constricts arterioles. Its other name, vasopressin, reflects that effect. anti- against · di- through · -uretic relating to urine · vas/o vessel Taught in Long-term blood pressure regulation.
adrenal androgens (dee-hy-droh-ep-ee-an-DROSS-ter-ohn)
Weak androgens, mainly DHEA, made by the zona reticularis under ACTH's drive. Tissues convert them into stronger androgens; they matter most in women, supporting pubic and underarm hair and sex drive. andr- male · -gen producing Taught in Adrenal glands.
adrenal cortex (ah-DREE-nul)
The outer 80 to 90 percent of each adrenal gland, which makes steroid hormones from cholesterol in three zones: the zona glomerulosa (aldosterone), the zona fasciculata (cortisol) and the zona reticularis (adrenal androgens). ad- near · ren- kidney · cortex bark, outer layer · glomerul- little ball · fascicul- little bundle · reticul- little net Taught in Adrenal glands.
adrenal disorders (KUSH-ing)
Addison disease is destruction of the adrenal cortex, with low cortisol and aldosterone, high ACTH and darker skin. Cushing syndrome is the effect of long-term glucocorticoid excess, usually from medication: central fat, thin skin, high blood glucose and raised blood pressure. Taught in Adrenal glands.
adrenal medulla (uh-DREE-nul meh-DUL-uh; kroh-MAF-in)
The inner part of each adrenal gland. Its chromaffin cells are modified sympathetic postganglionic neurons without axons: stimulated directly by preganglionic fibers, they release epinephrine (about 80 percent) and norepinephrine into the blood. ad- near · ren- kidney · medulla inner part, marrow · chrom- color · affin- attracted to Taught in Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.
aerobic respiration (air-OH-bik)
The complete breakdown of fuel using oxygen: glycolysis, then the citric acid cycle in the mitochondrion, then the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where oxygen accepts electrons and most ATP is made. aer- air · bio- life · citr- lemon Taught in How cells make ATP.
afferent pathway (AF-er-ent)
The route, usually a nerve, that carries information from the sensory receptor toward the control center. af- (ad-) toward · fer to carry Taught in Homeostasis and feedback loops.
afterload
The pressure a ventricle must overcome to open its semilunar valve and eject blood, set mainly by aortic pressure for the left ventricle. A higher afterload leaves more blood in the ventricle after ejection and lowers stroke volume. Taught in Cardiac output and its control.
agglutination (uh-gloo-tih-NAY-shun)
The clumping of cells when antibodies, each with two or more binding sites, bind antigens on several cells and cross-link them. It is an immune process, not clotting, and is often followed by hemolysis. agglutinare to glue together Taught in Blood types and transfusion.
agranulocytes (LIM-foh-site; MON-oh-site)
Leukocytes without visible granules: lymphocytes (a large round nucleus almost filling the cell; mostly the cells of specific immunity, from the lymphoid branch) and monocytes (the largest, with a kidney-shaped nucleus; they leave the blood and become macrophages). a- without · mono- one Taught in White blood cells and platelets.
airway defenses (MYOO-koh-SIL-ee-air-ee)
The ways the airway keeps particles and microorganisms out of the alveoli: hairs in the nasal vestibule, sticky mucus with lysozyme, defensins and IgA, the mucociliary escalator (cilia that move the mucus sheet toward the pharynx to be swallowed), and the cough reflex (vagus nerve) and sneeze reflex (trigeminal nerve), which blast irritants out. muc- mucus · cili- eyelash Taught in Nose, pharynx and larynx.
airway resistance (AZ-muh)
The opposition to airflow through the airways, which rises steeply as airway radius falls (resistance depends on 1 ÷ r⁴). Bronchoconstriction (smooth muscle contraction, from acetylcholine, histamine or leukotrienes) raises it; bronchodilation (relaxation, from epinephrine on beta-2 receptor proteins) lowers it. Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease in which bronchoconstriction, wall swelling and mucus narrow the airways. bronch- windpipe · con- together · strict- draw tight · dilat- widen · asthma panting Taught in Pulmonary ventilation.
aldosterone (al-DOSS-ter-ohn)
The main mineralocorticoid, a steroid from the zona glomerulosa. Released when plasma potassium rises or angiotensin II arrives, it makes the kidney reabsorb sodium, with water following, and secrete potassium. mineral- mineral (here sodium and potassium) · cortic- cortex · -oid like Taught in Adrenal glands.
alimentary canal and accessory organs (al-ih-MEN-tuh-ree)
The alimentary canal (GI tract) is the continuous tube food passes through: mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine, ending at the anus. The accessory digestive organs (teeth, tongue, glands that make saliva, liver, gallbladder, pancreas) help from outside it; food never passes through them. aliment- nourishment · gastr- stomach · intestin- gut Taught in Organization and regulation of the digestive system.
alpha adrenergic receptor
Adrenergic receptor proteins of the alpha family. Alpha-1 raises calcium in smooth muscle, so vessels in skin and gut constrict, the pupil's dilator muscle contracts and sphincters tighten. Alpha-2, often on the varicosity itself, lowers cyclic AMP and turns down norepinephrine release. adren- adrenal gland · erg- work Taught in Autonomic neurotransmitters and receptors.
alveolar ventilation (al-VEE-oh-lar)
The volume of fresh air that reaches the alveoli each minute: (tidal volume − dead space) × respiratory rate, about 4.2 L/min at rest. Shallow breathing lowers it even when minute ventilation stays the same. alveol- small cavity · -ar pertaining to Taught in Lung volumes and ventilation rates.
alveoli (in detail) (al-VEE-oh-lye)
The tiny air sacs, about 0.2 mm across and numbering hundreds of millions, where gases cross between air and blood. They cluster in alveolar sacs. Thin type I alveolar cells form the wall; cuboidal type II cells secrete surfactant, a film that lowers surface tension, and replace type I cells; alveolar macrophages engulf particles; alveolar pores link neighbors. alveol- small cavity Taught in Trachea, bronchial tree, alveoli and lungs.
amino acid metabolism
The handling of amino acids beyond protein building. Transamination moves each amino group onto glutamate; deamination in the liver releases it as ammonia; the carbon skeleton is burned or made into glucose (glucogenic amino acids) or into ketone bodies or fat (ketogenic amino acids). trans- across · de- remove · -genic producing Taught in Lipid and protein metabolism.
anaerobic metabolism (AN-air-OH-bik)
Making ATP without using oxygen: glycolysis followed by conversion of pyruvate to lactate, which regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue. It yields only 2 ATP per glucose. an- without · aer- air · bio- life · lact- milk Taught in How cells make ATP.
anaphylaxis (an-uh-fuh-LAK-sis)
A severe, rapid, whole-body type I reaction in which mast cells and basophils everywhere release histamine and leukotrienes: vasodilation and leaky capillaries drop MAP (anaphylactic shock, a distributive shock) and the airways narrow. Intramuscular epinephrine is the first treatment. ana- against · phylaxis protection Taught in Immune disorders.
anastomosis (uh-nas-tuh-MOH-sis)
A connection between two blood vessels. Where branches of two arteries join, blood can reach tissue by a second route, called collateral circulation. ana- again · stom- mouth Taught in Blood flow through the heart and the two circuits.
anatomical position
The reference posture for describing the body: standing upright and facing forward, feet flat and pointing forward, arms at the sides with the palms facing forward and the thumbs pointing away from the body. Every directional term assumes this posture. Taught in Anatomical position and directional terms.
anatomy (uh-NAT-uh-mee)
The study of the body's structures: their shape, size, parts and position. Gross anatomy covers what you can see unaided, microscopic anatomy what needs a microscope, regional anatomy one body part at a time, and systemic anatomy one organ system at a time. ana- apart, up · -tomy cutting Taught in Levels of organization and organ systems.
anemia (uh-NEE-mee-uh)
Too little hemoglobin to carry a normal load of oxygen, from blood loss (hemorrhagic anemia), too little production (iron-deficiency, pernicious and aplastic anemia, or too little erythropoietin) or early destruction of red cells (sickle cell disease, thalassemia). an- without · -emia blood condition Taught in Red blood cells and hemoglobin.
ANP in blood pressure control (nay-tree-yoo-RET-ik)
The role of atrial natriuretic peptide in lowering blood pressure: released when the atria are stretched, it makes the kidneys excrete sodium and water, lowers renin, aldosterone and ADH, and relaxes arterioles. natri- sodium · -uretic relating to urine Taught in Long-term blood pressure regulation.
anterior and posterior
Anterior means toward the front of the body; posterior means toward the back. Ventral (belly side) and dorsal (back side) mean the same in a standing human. ante- before · post- after, behind · venter (Latin) belly · dorsum (Latin) back Taught in Anatomical position and directional terms.
antibody classes
The five kinds of antibody, set by the heavy chain: IgG (main blood antibody, crosses to the fetus), IgA (in secretions), IgM (first made, a pentamer), IgE (on mast cells) and IgD (on naïve B cells). Class switching changes a B cell's class while keeping its variable region. Ig immunoglobulin · penta- five Taught in B cells and antibodies.
antibody structure (im-yoo-noh-GLOB-yoo-lin)
An antibody (immunoglobulin) is a Y of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains joined by disulfide bonds. The variable regions at the arm tips form two antigen-binding sites; the constant regions, including the Fc region of the stem, set the class and bind Fc receptor proteins and complement. immun/o immune · globulin globular protein · Fc fragment, crystallizable Taught in B cells and antibodies.
anticoagulants (an-tee-koh-AG-yoo-lunts)
Substances that slow or stop coagulation: the body's own antithrombin, protein C and the heparin-like molecules on the vessel lining, and drugs such as heparin (boosts antithrombin; works in minutes) and warfarin (blocks vitamin K recycling; works over days). anti- against Taught in Hemostasis and clotting.
antidiuretic hormone (an-tee-dy-yoo-RET-ik)
Antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin): a peptide made by hypothalamic neurons and released from the posterior pituitary when plasma osmolarity rises or blood volume falls sharply. It makes kidney cells insert aquaporins so less urine is made, and at high levels it constricts small arteries. anti- against · diuresis passing urine · vaso- vessel · press- pressure Taught in Hypothalamus and pituitary gland.
antigen and antibody (basic) (AN-tih-jen)
An antigen is a molecule the immune system recognizes as a specific target, usually a protein or glycoprotein on a surface. An antibody is a Y-shaped plasma protein, released by cells descended from lymphocytes, that binds one antigen; with two or more binding sites it can link and clump cells. anti- against · -gen producing Taught in White blood cells and platelets.
antigen in full (EP-ih-tope)
Any molecule that a lymphocyte's antigen receptor or an antibody binds specifically. Each antigen receptor binds one small part of it, an epitope (antigenic determinant), and a large antigen carries many epitopes. anti(body) antibody · -gen producing · epi- upon · -tope place Taught in Antigens, lymphocytes and antigen presentation.
antigen-presenting cell
A cell that takes in antigen, breaks it into peptides and loads them onto MHC class II (antigen processing), then displays them for T cells (antigen presentation): dendritic cells, which start naive T cell responses, macrophages and B cells. dendr/o tree Taught in Antigens, lymphocytes and antigen presentation.
antimicrobial proteins (LY-soh-zime)
Proteins made in advance that kill microbes directly: lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus that breaks bacterial cell walls, and defensins, small proteins that punch holes in microbial membranes. lys/o breaking apart · -zyme enzyme Taught in Innate immunity.
apoptosis (ap-op-TOH-sis)
Programmed cell death: an orderly self-destruction in which the cell's own enzymes dismantle it into membrane-wrapped pieces that neighboring cells engulf, without spilling its contents. apo- away · -ptosis falling Taught in Cell division and differentiation.
appetite regulation (GREL-in)
The control of hunger and eating by the hypothalamus. Ghrelin from the empty stomach raises hunger; stomach stretch and gut hormones produce satiety, the fullness that ends a meal; leptin from fat reports long-term stores. sati- enough Taught in Energy balance and body temperature.
aquaporin (ak-wuh-POR-in)
A membrane protein that forms a water channel, letting water molecules cross in single file far faster than through the bare bilayer. Water still moves in the direction osmosis sets. aqua water · porus passage Taught in Passive transport across membranes.
arrhythmia (uh-RITH-mee-uh; tak-ih-KAR-dee-uh; bray-dee-KAR-dee-uh)
Any heart rhythm that is abnormal in rate, regularity, starting point or conduction; also called dysrhythmia. Examples are tachycardia (above 100 per minute at rest), bradycardia (below 60), heart block, atrial fibrillation and ventricular fibrillation, which is treated by defibrillation. An artificial pacemaker fires when the heart's own rate is too slow. a- without · dys- abnormal · tachy- fast · brady- slow · fibrill- small fiber · de- undo Taught in The electrocardiogram.
arteries of the abdomen and pelvis (SEE-lee-ak; mez-en-TAIR-ik; ILL-ee-ak)
The branches of the abdominal aorta: three single gut arteries (celiac trunk, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric) and paired renal and gonadal arteries, ending in the common iliac arteries, which split into internal iliacs for the pelvis and external iliacs for the legs. The celiac trunk splits into the left gastric artery, the splenic artery and the common hepatic artery. celi- belly · mes- middle · enter- gut · ren- kidney · gon- seed · gastr- stomach · hepat- liver Taught in The major systemic arteries.
arteries of the head and neck (bray-kee-oh-seh-FAL-ik; kuh-ROT-id)
The brachiocephalic trunk (right side only), the common carotid arteries, which split into external carotids for the face and scalp and internal carotids for the brain, and the vertebral arteries from the subclavians. The carotid sinus is the pressure-sensing bulge at the start of each internal carotid. brachi- arm · cephal- head · karot- stupor (Greek) · sinus hollow, pocket Taught in The major systemic arteries.
arteries of the limbs (sub-KLAY-vee-un; pop-lih-TEE-ul; dor-SAY-lis PEE-dis)
In the arm, one artery renamed at landmarks: subclavian, axillary, brachial, then the radial and ulnar arteries, which join in the palmar arches. In the leg: femoral (with its deep femoral branch), popliteal, then the anterior tibial, which becomes the dorsalis pedis, and the posterior tibial. sub- under · clavi- collarbone · axilla armpit · brachi- arm · poplit- back of the knee · dorsum back, top · ped- foot Taught in The major systemic arteries.
arteriole (ar-TEER-ee-ohl)
The smallest artery, under about 0.1 mm across, with one to a few layers of smooth muscle. Arterioles are the main resistance vessels; their steady partial contraction, vascular tone, sets how much blood enters each capillary bed. arteri- artery · -ole small Taught in Structure of blood vessels.
artery, vein and capillary (basic)
The three kinds of blood vessel. An artery carries blood away from the heart, a vein carries blood back toward the heart, and a capillary is the thinnest vessel, where materials pass between blood and cells. capillaris (Latin) hairlike Taught in Levels of organization and organ systems.
ascending and descending tracts
The bundles of axons in the spinal cord's white columns. Ascending tracts carry sensory information up to the brain; descending tracts carry motor commands down. Their names give origin then destination, and most cross the midline somewhere along their path. ascend- climb up · descend- climb down · tractus a drawing out Taught in The spinal cord.
ascending sensory pathways (dee-kuh-SAY-shun, lem-NIS-kus)
Chains of neurons that carry sensory signals up the spinal cord and brainstem. Conscious pathways use a first-order neuron (sensory receptor to CNS), a second-order neuron (crosses the midline, a decussation, and rises to the thalamus) and a third-order neuron (thalamus to cortex). The dorsal column system (fasciculus gracilis and cuneatus, then the medial lemniscus) carries fine touch, vibration and proprioception and crosses in the medulla; the spinothalamic tract carries pain and temperature and crosses in the spinal cord; the spinocerebellar tracts carry unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum. decuss- cross like an X · lemniscus ribbon · gracil- slender · cune- wedge Taught in Sensory receptors and ascending pathways.
atherosclerosis (ATH-er-oh-skleh-ROH-sis)
The buildup of plaques of fat, foam cells and fibrous tissue in the tunica intima of large and medium arteries. Plaques narrow the lumen and can rupture, triggering a thrombus that blocks the artery. ather/o gruel, paste · scler/o hard · -osis condition Taught in Hypertension, heart failure and shock.
atomic number, mass number and isotopes (EYE-so-tope)
The atomic number is an atom's proton count, which defines the element; the mass number is protons plus neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons, and radioisotopes are isotopes whose unstable nuclei give off radiation. iso- equal · top/o place Taught in Atoms, ions and chemical bonds.
atomic structure
The smallest unit of an element that still behaves like that element: a nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons arranged in shells around it. a- not · tom/o cut Taught in Atoms, ions and chemical bonds.
ATP (uh-DEN-o-seen try-FOS-fate)
Adenosine triphosphate: a nucleotide of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups. Splitting off the outer phosphate by hydrolysis leaves ADP (adenosine diphosphate) and releases energy that cells use for work. tri- three · di- two Taught in Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.
ATP as the energy currency (fos-for-ih-LAY-shun)
The splitting of ATP by water into ADP and phosphate, which releases energy because the products are more stable than ATP. An ATPase is an enzyme that does this; phosphorylation is attaching a phosphate group to another molecule, which changes its energy and shape. hydro- water · -lysis splitting · -ase enzyme Taught in Energy, metabolism and enzymes.
ATP yield of glucose
The ATP made from one glucose broken down fully with oxygen: about 30–32, using about 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2. Only 4 are made directly; the rest come from oxidative phosphorylation. Older texts give 36–38. Taught in Carbohydrate metabolism in detail.
atrial natriuretic peptide (nay-tree-yoo-RET-ik)
Atrial natriuretic peptide, a hormone released by stretched muscle cells of the heart's upper chambers. It makes the kidneys excrete more sodium and water, lowers renin, aldosterone and ADH release, and dilates vessels, so blood volume and pressure fall. natri- sodium · -uretic relating to urine Taught in Other endocrine organs.
atrial reflex (BAYN-bridj)
A reflex in which stretch of the atria and nearby large veins, from more venous return, raises heart rate through the cardiovascular center. Also called the Bainbridge reflex. atri- entrance hall Taught in Short-term blood pressure regulation.
atrioventricular node (ay-tree-oh-ven-TRIK-yoo-lur)
The atrioventricular node: autorhythmic tissue in the lower interatrial septum, and the only normal electrical doorway from atria to ventricles. It conducts slowly, causing the AV nodal delay of about 0.1 second, during which the atria finish contracting. atrio- atrium · ventricul- ventricle Taught in The conduction system and pacemaker cells.
atrioventricular valves (MY-trul; KOR-dee ten-DIN-ee-ee)
The valves between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve on the right and the mitral (bicuspid) valve on the left. Chordae tendineae tie their cusps to papillary muscles, which keep the cusps from flipping into the atria when the ventricles contract. tri- three · bi- two · cusp point · chord- string · tendin- tendon · papilla nipple Taught in Heart valves, septa and internal structure.
auditory pathway
The route from the cochlea to the cortex: cochlear nerve, cochlear nuclei, superior olivary nuclei (where the two ears are first compared), inferior colliculus, medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, and primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. Each ear reaches both hemispheres. genicul- little knee Taught in Hearing and equilibrium.
autoimmune disease (LOO-pus air-ih-thee-muh-TOH-sus)
An adaptive immune response against self antigens after self-tolerance fails; autoimmune disease is the damage it causes. It can be organ-specific, as in type 1 diabetes, or systemic, as in rheumatoid arthritis (inflamed synovial membranes) and systemic lupus erythematosus (antibodies against nuclear contents forming immune complexes). auto- self · arthr- joint · -itis inflammation · lupus wolf · erythemat- red Taught in Immune disorders.
autonomic nervous system (basic) (aw-toh-NOM-ik)
The part of the motor division that controls cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands, mostly without conscious control. Its sympathetic division (fight-or-flight) dominates in stress and exercise; its parasympathetic division (rest-and-digest) dominates at rest. auto- self · nom- law · sym- together · path- feeling · para- beside Taught in Organization of the nervous system.
autonomic tone (VAY-gul)
The steady background firing of autonomic neurons at rest, which lets one division change an effector in both directions. Vagal tone holds the resting heart well below its own rate of about 100 beats a minute; sympathetic tone keeps most blood vessels partly constricted. tonus tension · vag- wandering (vagus) Taught in Autonomic control and visceral reflexes.
autoregulation of GFR
The kidney's own control that keeps GFR nearly constant between a mean arterial pressure of about 80 and 180 mm Hg by adjusting the afferent arteriole: the myogenic mechanism (stretch makes it constrict) and tubuloglomerular feedback (more sodium chloride at the macula densa makes it constrict). auto- self · my/o muscle · -genic produced by Taught in Glomerular filtration.
autorhythmic cell (aw-toh-RITH-mik)
A specialized cardiac muscle cell, also called a pacemaker cell, that depolarizes to threshold on its own with no outside stimulus. About 1 percent of heart cells are autorhythmic; they start and conduct each heartbeat's signal and contract only weakly. auto- self · rhythm- regular beat · -ic relating to Taught in The conduction system and pacemaker cells.

B cell
A lymphocyte that matures in the red bone marrow and binds intact antigen with a membrane-bound antibody as its antigen receptor; when activated, its descendants release antibodies. Taught in Antigens, lymphocytes and antigen presentation.
baroreceptor reflex
The negative feedback loop that corrects fast changes in blood pressure: baroreceptor signals reach the cardiovascular center, which adjusts sympathetic and vagal output to the heart, arterioles and veins. Also called the baroreflex; on standing it is the orthostatic reflex. bar/o pressure, weight · re- back · flex bend Taught in Short-term blood pressure regulation.
baroreceptors (BAIR-oh-ree-SEP-terz)
Stretch-sensitive sensory receptors in the walls of the carotid sinuses and the aortic arch (the arch group is sometimes called the aortic sinuses, a name anatomists give to the bulges at the root of the aorta). They fire faster when arterial pressure stretches the wall more, and they reset to a new pressure within a day or two. bar/o pressure, weight Taught in Short-term blood pressure regulation.
basal nuclei (KAW-dayt; pyoo-TAY-men; GLOH-bus PAL-ih-dus)
Masses of gray matter deep in the cerebrum (the caudate and putamen, together the striatum, and the globus pallidus), working with the subthalamus and substantia nigra. They help select wanted movements and suppress unwanted ones; the older name is basal ganglia. cauda tail · putamen shell · globus pallidus pale globe · substantia nigra black substance Taught in Regions of the brain.
base (chemistry) (AL-kuh-line)
A substance that takes up hydrogen ions, lowering a fluid's H+ concentration, either directly or by releasing hydroxide ions (OH−) that join H+ to form water. Also called alkaline. alkal- ashes of burned plants Taught in Acids, bases, pH and buffers.
basement membrane
The thin mat of proteins under every epithelium that anchors it to the tissue below. It has two parts: the basal lamina, made by the epithelial cells, and the reticular lamina, made by the tissue beneath. lamina thin sheet · reticul- little net Taught in Epithelial tissue.
beta adrenergic receptor
Adrenergic receptor proteins of the beta family, which raise cyclic AMP. Beta-1 speeds the heart and strengthens its contraction. Beta-2, reached mainly by circulating epinephrine, relaxes smooth muscle (widening the small airways and dilating vessels in skeletal muscle) and makes the liver release glucose. adren- adrenal gland · erg- work Taught in Autonomic neurotransmitters and receptors.
beta oxidation (BAY-tuh)
The breakdown of a fatty acid inside mitochondria, two carbons at a time, into acetyl CoA; each turn also makes one FADH2 and one NADH. It needs oxygen, because the carriers must be reoxidized by the electron transport chain. beta second letter of the Greek alphabet: the carbon being oxidized Taught in Lipid and protein metabolism.
bile
A yellow-green, slightly alkaline fluid made by hepatocytes: water, bicarbonate, bile salts, bilirubin, cholesterol and phospholipids. Its bile salts emulsify fat into tiny droplets; it has no digestive enzymes. It drains through the hepatic ducts and the common bile duct, and its bile salts are reabsorbed in the ileum and reused (the enterohepatic circulation). emulgere (Latin) to milk out · enter- intestine · hepat- liver Taught in Liver, gallbladder and pancreas.
bleeding around the brain (hee-muh-TOH-muh)
