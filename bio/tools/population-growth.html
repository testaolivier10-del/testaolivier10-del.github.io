Population growth

Watch a population grow until it fills its space, and see what slows it down.

About this tool

Grow a population and watch what limits it. Choose exponential growth (unlimited resources) or logistic growth (a carrying capacity, K), set the starting size and rmax, then add a flood or a drought at the time you choose. Read the size, the growth rate and the growth rate per individual at any moment. The questions below use the same model.

How it works

A population's growth rate, dN/dt, is how many individuals it gains per unit of time: births minus deaths (this model has no migration). N is the population size and t is time, here in years.

  1. Exponential growth, dN/dt = rmaxN. rmax is the maximum per-capita growth rate: what each individual adds per year when nothing is in short supply. Because each individual adds the same amount, the more individuals there are, the faster the population grows, and the curve of N over time curves upward more and more steeply (a J shape).
  2. Logistic growth, dN/dt = rmaxN(K − N)/K. K, the carrying capacity, is the largest population the environment can support. As N rises toward K, individuals compete for food, space and other limited resources, so births per individual fall and deaths per individual rise. The factor (K − N)/K is the share of K still unused: close to 1 when N is small, 0 when N = K, negative above K. N over time makes an S shape.
  3. The per-capita growth rate is dN/dt divided by N, the growth per individual. In exponential growth it stays at rmax. In logistic growth it falls in a straight line, from rmax when N is near 0 to 0 at K.
  4. So in logistic growth dN/dt is largest when N = K/2, at rmaxK/4: below that there are few individuals adding young; above it each individual adds few. The graph of dN/dt against N is a hump.

The model's rules:

  • Steps (Euler's method). Time moves in steps of Δt. At the start of each step the model works out dN/dt from the current N, then sets N(t + Δt) = N(t) + (dN/dt) × Δt. A smaller Δt follows the smooth curve of the equation more closely; with Δt = 1 year, exponential growth gives N = N0(1 + rmax)t, a little below the smooth curve.
  • N is a real number, the expected size, so tables round it to whole individuals. There is no chance: the same settings always give the same numbers.
  • Density-independent events (a flood, a fire, a hard frost) remove the same share of the population whatever its size: N becomes N × (1 − share), at the first step at or after the time you set. The model treats K as unchanged by them.
  • A change in K (a drought, a new food source) applies from the time you set. Above the new K, dN/dt is negative and the population shrinks toward it.
  • An exponential run stops once N passes 1,000,000, where the graph would no longer be readable.
