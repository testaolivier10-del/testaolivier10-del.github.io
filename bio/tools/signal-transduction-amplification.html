Signal transduction and amplification

Follow one hormone signal into a cell and see how it grows, then stops.

About this tool

Add epinephrine (adrenaline) to a liver cell and follow the signal from the receptor on the surface to the response inside: glucose units broken off glycogen. Count the active molecules at each step to see where the signal grows, wash the hormone away to watch the signal switch off, and block single steps with a drug or a toxin. Then answer the questions below about the same model.

How it works

The pathway, one step at a time (each effect is the cause of the next):

  1. Reception. Epinephrine binds a G protein-coupled receptor. Binding is reversible: the fraction of receptors with epinephrine bound moves toward [L] / ([L] + Kd), with Kd = 10 nM (half of the receptors are bound at 10 nM). After washout, epinephrine leaves the receptors within a few seconds.
  2. G protein. A bound receptor changes shape and makes G proteins release GDP and pick up GTP, which switches them on. One receptor keeps doing this, about 10 G proteins per second, so this step amplifies. An active G protein switches itself off by hydrolyzing its GTP to GDP (on average after 4 seconds). A few G proteins switch on by themselves now and then.
  3. Adenylyl cyclase. An active G protein (its α subunit) binds and switches on one adenylyl cyclase. One-to-one binding passes the signal on but does not amplify it.
  4. cAMP. Each active adenylyl cyclase makes about 100 cAMP molecules per second from ATP (amplifies). Phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down to AMP, 10% of the cAMP each second.
  5. PKA. cAMP binds protein kinase A (PKA): four cAMP molecules bind each PKA (two on each regulatory subunit), which frees its two active (catalytic) parts. Binding passes the signal on but does not multiply it, and most of the cAMP in the cell is not bound at any moment, so the model has about 1 active PKA for every 200 cAMP. That ratio is a count of molecules, not how many cAMP each PKA binds: active PKA = PKAtotal × C² / (C² + Kc²).
  6. Kinase cascade. Each active PKA phosphorylates about 1 phosphorylase kinase per second, and each active phosphorylase kinase phosphorylates about 2 glycogen phosphorylase molecules per second (both amplify). Phosphatases remove the phosphates, 20% of the active molecules each second.
  7. Response. Each active glycogen phosphorylase breaks about 10 glucose units off glycogen per second (amplifies).

The drugs and toxins (each acts from time 0):

  • Receptor antagonist: binds the receptor without activating it and competes with epinephrine. At the dose used, Kd behaves as if it were 21 times higher (210 nM), so more epinephrine can overcome it.
  • G protein locked on: the G protein cannot hydrolyze its GTP, so once it has GTP it stays active, with or without epinephrine. Cholera toxin does this to the stimulatory G protein of intestinal cells; the cAMP it raises opens chloride channels and water follows the ions out of the cells.
  • G protein locked off: the G protein cannot exchange GDP for GTP, so a bound receptor cannot switch it on. (Pertussis toxin locks a different, inhibitory G protein this way; the model applies the lock to the stimulatory one.)
  • Phosphodiesterase inhibitor: 90% less cAMP breakdown.
  • PKA inhibitor: blocks the active site of PKA. cAMP still binds PKA, but no target gets phosphorylated.

Simplifications: counts are molecules in one cell, with made-up but realistic proportions. The model takes steps of 0.05 s and starts from a resting cell. Real cells also switch receptors off after long exposure, have several kinds of G protein, and use PKA to switch off glycogen synthesis at the same time. Each trial you run adds random scatter (standard deviation 3%), like measurement error in a lab; the curves show the model without scatter.
