Osmosis and water potential

See which way water moves when a cell sits in a salty or sugary solution.

About this tool

Put a potato core, a red blood cell or a dialysis bag into a solution. Compare the water potential inside and outside, see which way water moves, and watch the mass change. Run trials across a range of concentrations to build the classic graph, then answer the questions below.

How it works

Water potential ψ = ψs + ψp (in bars). Solute potential ψs = −iCRT, where i is the number of particles each solute unit makes in water (sucrose 1, NaCl 2), C is the molar concentration (mol/L), R = 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K) and T is the temperature in kelvins (°C + 273). Pure water in an open beaker has ψ = 0; adding solute makes ψ negative. Water moves from higher (less negative) to lower (more negative) water potential, and net movement stops when the two are equal.

The model’s assumptions:

  • Water crosses the membrane; the solutes inside the cell or bag and the solute in the beaker do not. Dialysis tubing does let sucrose through slowly: over 30 minutes the effect is small, but over hours the bag’s mass change would shrink.
  • The beaker holds far more solution than the cell, so its concentration does not change. An open beaker has ψp = 0.
  • NaCl splits completely into two ions (i = 2). Real NaCl behaves as about 1.9 particles because some ions pair up.
  • Plant cells (the potato core) have a cell wall. Once the cell is full, the wall pushes back: pressure potential ψp rises as the cell takes in a little more water, which raises its ψ until no more water enters (the cell is turgid). In a strong solution the cell loses water, ψp falls to 0, and the membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis).
  • Animal cells (red blood cells) have no wall, so ψp stays 0. In a mildly hypotonic solution they swell until the water potentials match; in a strongly hypotonic one (or pure water) they swell until they burst (lyse), here when they reach 1.55 times their normal size (mass and volume change together).
  • Water flows at a rate proportional to the difference in water potential, so changes are fast at first and slow down near equilibrium. Mass change counts only water; part of each cell’s mass (proteins, starch, walls) does not change.
  • The numbers are illustrative: real potatoes differ with variety and storage. Each run adds random scatter (standard deviation 0.8 percentage points), like measurement error.
