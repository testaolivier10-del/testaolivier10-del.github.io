Lac and trp operons

See how bacteria switch genes on and off depending on the sugar they have.

About this tool

Switch a bacterium's genes on and off. Choose the sugars or the amino acid in the medium, break a gene or a DNA site, and see where the repressor sits, whether RNA polymerase can bind, and how much mRNA and enzyme the cell makes. Then change the medium and follow the mRNA and the enzyme minute by minute. The questions below use the same model.

How it works

An operon is a group of bacterial genes read from one promoter into one mRNA. Between the promoter and the genes sits the operator, a short DNA site where a repressor protein can bind. A bound repressor keeps RNA polymerase from transcribing the genes.

The lac operon is inducible (off unless something turns it on):

  1. The lacI gene, outside the operon, makes the lac repressor all the time. On its own, the repressor binds the operator, so the genes for using lactose (lacZ, β-galactosidase; lacY; lacA) are barely transcribed.
  2. When lactose is present, some of it is turned into allolactose, the inducer. Allolactose binds the repressor at a site away from its DNA-binding part, the repressor changes shape and lets go of the operator.
  3. When glucose is scarce, the cell's cAMP level rises. cAMP binds CAP (catabolite activator protein); CAP–cAMP binds a site next to the promoter and helps RNA polymerase bind, so transcription becomes much faster. With glucose present, cAMP stays low and CAP does not bind.

The trp operon is repressible (on unless something turns it off): its five genes make the enzymes that build the amino acid tryptophan. The trp repressor, made by trpR, cannot bind the operator by itself. When tryptophan is plentiful, tryptophan binds the repressor (it is the corepressor), the repressor changes shape, binds the operator and blocks transcription.

The model's rules:

  • Rates (relative, a fully on wild-type operon = 100): repressor on the operator 1 (a trickle still gets through); repressor off and CAP–cAMP bound 100; repressor off but no CAP (glucose present) 10. The trp operon: repressor bound 1, not bound 100.
  • Inputs act at once at 0 min: lactose gives allolactose and glucose lowers cAMP immediately. The model treats lactose as giving allolactose in every cell. (Real cells need a little β-galactosidase to make allolactose, so a cell with no working lacZ cannot make it; experiments on such cells use a lab-made inducer instead. The model skips this.) Tryptophan in the medium is the corepressor; the model ignores the tryptophan the cell makes itself.
  • Mutations: lacI⁻ makes no working repressor. lacIˢ makes a super-repressor that cannot bind allolactose, so it stays on the operator. Oᶜ is an operator with a changed DNA sequence that no repressor can bind. lacZ⁻ still makes mRNA but no working β-galactosidase. trpR⁻ makes no trp repressor. A trp Oᶜ operator binds no trp repressor.
  • Time course: the cells grow in the starting medium long enough for every level to settle, then move to the new medium at 0 min. mRNA is broken down quickly (half-life 2 min), so its level settles within about 10 min. The enzymes are stable and are only diluted as the cells grow and divide (one doubling every 40 min), so the enzyme level changes slowly. Both are relative: steady state in a fully on wild-type cell = 100.
  • The model has no randomness: the same settings always give the same numbers.
