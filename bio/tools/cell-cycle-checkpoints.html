Cell cycle checkpoints

See how checkpoints stop damaged cells from dividing, and what cancer mutations break.

About this tool

Grow a dish of 1,000 human cells for three days. Change the growth factor in the medium, damage their DNA, give them mutations found in cancer cells or block the spindle, and watch what fraction of cells sits in each phase, how much DNA each cell holds, how fast they divide and whether cells with damaged DNA get through. Then answer the questions below about the same model.

How it works

Every cell moves through G1, S (DNA replication), G2 and M (mitosis and cytokinesis), or rests in G0. Three checkpoints decide whether it may go on:

  1. G1 checkpoint. A growth factor binds its receptor, a signaling protein like ras passes the signal on, and the cell makes cyclin D. Cyclin D–CDK phosphorylates Rb, Rb releases the transcription factor (E2F) it was holding, and the genes needed for S phase are switched on. In the model, the fraction of cells that pass equals the growth factor level (0-100%); the rest go into G0, which they leave at 10% per hour times the growth factor level. A cell with damaged DNA and working p53 is held here: p53 makes p21, and p21 blocks cyclin–CDK.
  2. G2 checkpoint. A cell with damaged DNA is held before mitosis. Other checkpoint proteins detect the damage, so this hold starts with or without p53, but p53 keeps it in place.
  3. M (spindle) checkpoint. Anaphase waits until every chromosome is attached to spindle microtubules from both poles.

The model’s rules:

  • Phase lengths follow a 24-hour human cell cycle: G1 about 9 h, S 10 h, G2 4.5 h, M 0.5 h. G1 varies from cell to cell: at least 5 h, then cells leave at random, 4 h later on average. Ages are tracked in half-hour steps.
  • DNA damage: each hour the chosen percentage of undamaged cells, in any phase, gets damaged DNA. A held cell repairs its DNA at 25% per hour and then goes on. With working p53, a held cell that is not repaired dies by apoptosis at 5% per hour. Damage that happens after the G2 checkpoint is not checked again, so a few damaged cells divide even with working p53. Daughter cells keep their parent’s damage.
  • p53 lost: no hold at the G1 checkpoint, no apoptosis, and the G2 hold is not kept (50% of held cells move on into M each hour, still damaged).
  • Rb lost: nothing holds the S-phase genes off, so every cell passes the G1 checkpoint with or without growth factor. (The model keeps the p53 hold of damaged cells working; in real cells losing Rb also weakens it.)
  • Cyclin D–CDK overactive: Rb is phosphorylated without growth factor, and cells reach the checkpoint sooner (late G1 lasts 1 h on average instead of 4 h).
  • Ras-like protein stuck on: the growth factor signal is on all the time, as if the dish always had full growth factor.
  • Spindle poison: no spindle forms, so no chromosome is attached and no cell leaves M. (Real cells held this long eventually die or leave mitosis without dividing; the model does not show that.)
  • DNA content: a G1 or G0 cell holds 2 units of DNA, a cell in S between 2 and 4, and a cell in G2 or M 4. A G2 cell is still diploid (2n): it has the same chromosomes, each made of two sister chromatids, so some books call these peaks 2n and 4n.
  • The dish starts with 1,000 cells that have been growing with full growth factor. Cell numbers are averages, so the model has no randomness: the same settings always give the same numbers, and a run records them.
