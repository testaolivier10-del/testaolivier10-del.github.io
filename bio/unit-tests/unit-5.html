Kernels on an ear of corn

Each kernel on an ear of corn is a seed, the offspring of one fertilization. A true-breeding plant grown from purple, smooth kernels was crossed with a true-breeding plant grown from yellow, wrinkled kernels. Every F1 kernel was purple and smooth. F1 plants were then self-pollinated, and students counted the F2 kernels on one ear. Kernel color and kernel texture are each controlled by one gene, on different chromosome pairs.

1. Which alleles are dominant?

  1. The purple allele and the smooth allele
  2. The yellow allele and the wrinkled allele
  3. The purple allele and the wrinkled allele
  4. The yellow allele and the smooth allele
Show the answer

The F1 kernels were heterozygous for both genes and showed purple and smooth, so those alleles are dominant.

  • Correct: The purple allele and the smooth allele: A heterozygote shows the dominant phenotype: the F1 were purple and smooth.
  • The yellow allele and the wrinkled allele: Yellow and wrinkled disappeared in the F1 and returned in the F2: they are recessive.
  • The purple allele and the wrinkled allele: Wrinkled was hidden in the heterozygous F1, so it is recessive.
  • The yellow allele and the smooth allele: Yellow was hidden in the heterozygous F1, so it is recessive.

2. The F2 counts are close to a 9:3:3:1 ratio. Which explanation connects that ratio to events in the F1 plants' meiosis?

  1. The two genes' chromosome pairs line up independently at metaphase I, so PS, Ps, pS and ps gametes form in equal numbers and combine at random.
  2. Homologs pair and swap pieces in each meiosis, which makes the four kernel types appear in a 9:3:3:1 ratio.
  3. The purple and smooth alleles are dominant, so they go into 9 out of 16 gametes and the recessive alleles into the rest.
  4. Each F1 plant makes two kinds of gamete, PS and ps, and their random combination at fertilization gives 9:3:3:1.
Show the answer

Each F1 is PpSs. Segregation gives each gamete one allele of each gene, and because the two pairs assort independently, the four combinations are equally likely (1/4 each). Random fertilization of 4 × 4 gamete types gives 16 equally likely boxes: 9 P_S_, 3 P_ss, 3 ppS_, 1 ppss.

  • Correct: The two genes' chromosome pairs line up independently at metaphase I, so PS, Ps, pS and ps gametes form in equal numbers and combine at random.: Independent assortment makes four equal gamete types; 4 × 4 random combinations give 9:3:3:1.
  • Homologs pair and swap pieces in each meiosis, which makes the four kernel types appear in a 9:3:3:1 ratio.: The ratio comes from independent assortment of separate chromosome pairs; crossing over affects genes on the same chromosome.
  • The purple and smooth alleles are dominant, so they go into 9 out of 16 gametes and the recessive alleles into the rest.: Dominance decides what the offspring look like, not which alleles enter gametes; each allele goes into half the gametes.
  • Each F1 plant makes two kinds of gamete, PS and ps, and their random combination at fertilization gives 9:3:3:1.: Only two gamete types would give a 3:1 pattern of purple smooth to yellow wrinkled, with no purple wrinkled or yellow smooth kernels.

3. A student grows a plant from one purple, smooth F2 kernel and wants to know its genotype. Which cross and result would show that it is PpSs?

  1. Cross it with a plant from a yellow, wrinkled kernel; find the four phenotypes in about equal numbers.
  2. Cross it with a plant from a purple, smooth kernel; find purple, smooth offspring and no other kind.
  3. Cross it with a plant from a yellow, wrinkled kernel; find purple, smooth offspring and no others.
  4. Self-pollinate it; find purple, smooth and yellow, wrinkled kernels in a 3:1 ratio.
Show the answer

A testcross to ppss means every offspring gets p and s from that parent, so the offspring show directly which alleles the tested plant's gametes carried. PpSs makes PS, Ps, pS and ps equally, giving a 1:1:1:1 ratio.

  • Correct: Cross it with a plant from a yellow, wrinkled kernel; find the four phenotypes in about equal numbers.: In a testcross the offspring phenotypes mirror the tested plant's gametes: four equal classes mean PpSs.
  • Cross it with a plant from a purple, smooth kernel; find purple, smooth offspring and no other kind.: Another purple, smooth parent can hide recessive alleles, so the result would not reveal the genotype.
  • Cross it with a plant from a yellow, wrinkled kernel; find purple, smooth offspring and no others.: Offspring that are purple and smooth every time would mean the plant is PPSS, not PpSs.
  • Self-pollinate it; find purple, smooth and yellow, wrinkled kernels in a 3:1 ratio.: Selfing PpSs gives four phenotypes in a 9:3:3:1 ratio, not 3:1.

4. On one beach, nests have been incubating at about 28 °C. If warmer summers raise nest temperatures to about 30 °C, predict the effect on the hatchlings.

  1. The share of females rises from about 18% to about 86%.
  2. The share of females falls from about 86% to about 18%.
  3. The sex ratio stays the same, because sex is set by the hatchlings' genes.
  4. Each hatchling becomes female, because warming above 28 °C gives females.
Show the answer

Reading the graph: 18% female at 28 °C, 86% at 30 °C. A 2 °C rise near the pivotal temperature shifts the sex ratio strongly toward females.

  • Correct: The share of females rises from about 18% to about 86%.: 28 °C gives about 18% females and 30 °C about 86%: a large shift toward females.
  • The share of females falls from about 86% to about 18%.: This reverses the direction: warmer eggs give more females in this species.
  • The sex ratio stays the same, because sex is set by the hatchlings' genes.: In this species sex is set by temperature, so the ratio changes with warming.
  • Each hatchling becomes female, because warming above 28 °C gives females.: At 30 °C the mean is 86% female, not 100%; some males still hatch.

5. Why did the researchers divide the eggs of each nest among all seven incubators?

  1. So nest-to-nest genetic differences were spread across temperatures, leaving temperature as the one difference.
  2. So that each incubator held the same number of eggs, which made the incubators warm up evenly.
  3. So that eggs from the same mother would hatch as the same sex in each incubator they were placed in.
  4. So that the researchers could later tell which of the ten nests each hatchling came from just by looking at its sex.
Show the answer

If each nest went to one incubator, a nest whose eggs were, for genetic reasons, more likely to become female would be confused with the effect of its temperature. Splitting every nest across all temperatures controls for that.

  • Correct: So nest-to-nest genetic differences were spread across temperatures, leaving temperature as the one difference.: Spreading each nest's eggs across every temperature stops nest differences from being mistaken for temperature effects.
  • So that each incubator held the same number of eggs, which made the incubators warm up evenly.: Equal numbers help, but the purpose of splitting nests is to control for differences between nests.
  • So that eggs from the same mother would hatch as the same sex in each incubator they were placed in.: Sex is expected to differ with temperature, even among eggs from one mother.
  • So that the researchers could later tell which of the ten nests each hatchling came from just by looking at its sex.: Sex cannot identify a nest; the nests' eggs would be labeled directly.

6. Do the error bars support a real difference in the percentage of females between 31 °C and 32 °C?

  1. No; 97 ± 3 reaches 100, so the two intervals overlap.
  2. Yes; 100% is higher than 97%, so the difference is real.
  3. Yes; the error bars at 32 °C are zero, so any difference is real.
  4. No; percentages are not compared using error bars.
Show the answer

At 31 °C the interval is 94% to 100%, which includes the 100% seen at 32 °C. The difference is not shown to be real.

  • Correct: No; 97 ± 3 reaches 100, so the two intervals overlap.: The 31 °C interval reaches 100%, so it overlaps the 32 °C value.
  • Yes; 100% is higher than 97%, so the difference is real.: Means can differ by chance; overlapping ± 2 SE intervals mean the difference is not shown to be real.
  • Yes; the error bars at 32 °C are zero, so any difference is real.: A zero-width bar at 32 °C does not stop it overlapping the 31 °C interval.
  • No; percentages are not compared using error bars.: Means of percentages from several nests can be compared with ± 2 SE intervals like any other mean.

7. A dog's skin cells have 78 chromosomes. How many chromosomes does a dog sperm cell have? Give a whole number.

Type a number in chromosomes.

Show the answer

Skin cells are diploid (2n = 78). Sperm are made by meiosis and are haploid: n = 78 ÷ 2 = 39.

  • Answer: 39 chromosomes

8. A species has 2n = 6, and each parent's two homologs of every pair differ. Ignoring crossing over, how many different chromosome combinations could one pair of parents produce in their offspring? Give a whole number.

Type a number in combinations.

Show the answer

n = 3, so each parent makes 2³ = 8 kinds of gamete. Random fertilization pairs any of the 8 eggs with any of the 8 sperm: 8 × 8 = 64.

  • Answer: 64 combinations

9. In snapdragons, red (CRCR) × white (CWCW) gives pink offspring. What ratio of flower colors is expected when two pink plants are crossed?

  1. 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white
  2. 3 pink : 1 white
  3. 3 red : 1 white
  4. 1 red : 1 white
Show the answer

CRCW × CRCW gives 1 CRCR : 2 CRCW : 1 CWCW. With incomplete dominance each genotype has its own color, so the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio.

  • Correct: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white: Each genotype looks different, so 1:2:1 genotypes give 1:2:1 colors.
  • 3 pink : 1 white: Pink is the heterozygote, so it cannot breed true or be a dominant class.
  • 3 red : 1 white: 3:1 would need red to be dominant, but the heterozygote is pink, not red.
  • 1 red : 1 white: Pink plants are heterozygous, so they produce pink offspring as well as red and white.

10. In a man's liver cell in G2, a biologist compares the two copies of chromosome 7 with each other, and the two sister chromatids of one copy with each other. Which statement is correct?

  1. The sister chromatids carry identical DNA; the two chromosome 7s have the same genes but may carry different alleles.
  2. The two chromosome 7s carry identical DNA, because they are copies of the same chromosome.
  3. The two sister chromatids came from different parents, one from his mother and one from his father.
  4. The two chromosome 7s carry different genes, one set inherited from each of his parents.
Show the answer

Sister chromatids are made by copying one DNA molecule in S phase, so they are identical. The two chromosome 7s are homologs, one from each parent: same genes in the same order, but possibly different alleles.

  • Correct: The sister chromatids carry identical DNA; the two chromosome 7s have the same genes but may carry different alleles.: Sister chromatids are copies; homologs come from different parents and may differ in alleles.
  • The two chromosome 7s carry identical DNA, because they are copies of the same chromosome.: Homologs are not copies of each other: one came from the mother and one from the father.
  • The two sister chromatids came from different parents, one from his mother and one from his father.: Both sister chromatids were copied from the same chromosome, so they come from the same parent.
  • The two chromosome 7s carry different genes, one set inherited from each of his parents.: Homologs carry the same genes in the same order; what can differ is the version (allele) of each gene.

11. A plant has identical alleles on the two homologs of every pair, at every gene. Its pollen fertilizes its own eggs. Predict how the offspring compare with the parent and with each other.

  1. Identical to the parent and to each other, since meiosis just reshuffles alleles that are the same.
  2. They differ from one another, because crossing over makes new combinations of alleles in each meiosis.
  3. They differ, because independent assortment gives 2ⁿ different gametes in any plant.
  4. They have half the parent's chromosome number, because just one parent was involved.
Show the answer

Crossing over and independent assortment create variety only by mixing different alleles. If both homologs of every pair are the same, every gamete carries the same alleles, and so does every zygote.

  • Correct: Identical to the parent and to each other, since meiosis just reshuffles alleles that are the same.: With nothing different to shuffle, every gamete, and so every offspring, is the same.
  • They differ from one another, because crossing over makes new combinations of alleles in each meiosis.: Swapping matching pieces between identical homologs produces nothing new.
  • They differ, because independent assortment gives 2ⁿ different gametes in any plant.: 2ⁿ arrangements are possible, but if the homologs are identical every arrangement gives the same gamete.
  • They have half the parent's chromosome number, because just one parent was involved.: Egg and pollen are each haploid; their fusion restores the diploid number, even from one parent.

12. In a cross AaBb × Aabb (genes on different chromosome pairs; A and B dominant), what proportion of offspring are expected to show both dominant traits? Give a decimal to three places.

Type a number.

Show the answer

Aa × Aa: dominant phenotype (AA or Aa) with probability 3/4. Bb × bb: Bb with probability 1/2. Product rule: 3/4 × 1/2 = 3/8 = 0.375.

  • Answer: 0.375
