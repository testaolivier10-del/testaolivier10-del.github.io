Every cell is mostly water, and almost every reaction in this course happens in it. To understand why water is so useful to living things, you only need one idea: water molecules attract each other. This page builds that idea one step at a time.

A water molecule is polar

A water molecule is one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms. Each joint is a covalent bond: the two atoms share a pair of electrons. The sharing is not equal. Oxygen pulls on shared electrons much more strongly than hydrogen does, so the electrons spend more of their time near the oxygen.

That uneven sharing gives the oxygen end of the molecule a small negative charge, written δ− ("partial negative"), and each hydrogen a small positive charge, δ+. The molecule is also bent, not straight, so the two hydrogens sit on one side. A molecule with a partly negative end and a partly positive end is called a polar molecule.

Hydrogen bonds hold water together

Opposite charges attract. The partly positive hydrogen of one water molecule is pulled toward the partly negative oxygen of a neighbor. That attraction is a hydrogen bond (Figure 1).

A hydrogen bond is much weaker than a covalent bond. In liquid water the bonds break and re-form constantly, trillions of times a second. But there are so many of them that, together, they give water its unusual properties.

Do not mix them up. The covalent bond is inside one molecule (oxygen to its own hydrogen). The hydrogen bond is between two molecules (a hydrogen of one to the oxygen of another).

Cohesion, adhesion and surface tension

Cohesion is water sticking to water. Because of it, the surface of a pond behaves like a thin, stretchy skin: this is surface tension, and it lets a water strider stand on the surface without sinking.

Adhesion is water sticking to other polar or charged surfaces, such as glass or the walls of the narrow tubes inside a plant stem. In a thin tube, adhesion pulls water up the walls, and cohesion drags the rest of the water column along behind it. That is part of how water climbs from the roots to the leaves of a tall tree.

Water resists changes in temperature

Specific heat is the energy needed to warm one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius. Water's specific heat is high, about 4.18 joules per gram per degree. When you add heat to water, much of that energy first goes into breaking hydrogen bonds, and only the rest speeds the molecules up. So water warms slowly, and it cools slowly too.

This is why a lake stays cool on a hot afternoon and why the inside of an organism, which is mostly water, does not swing in temperature every time the air does.

Evaporation cools

Heat of vaporization is the energy needed to turn one gram of a liquid into a gas. For water it is very high, about 2,260 joules per gram, because a molecule must break all of its hydrogen bonds to escape. The molecules that escape are the fastest ones, and they carry their energy away with them. The water left behind is cooler. Sweating works this way: each gram of sweat that evaporates removes a large amount of heat from the skin.

Water as a solvent

A solvent is the substance other substances dissolve in. When salt or sugar enters water, water molecules crowd around each charged or polar particle: their negative oxygen ends face positive charges and their positive hydrogen ends face negative ones. The particle is surrounded and carried into solution. Because so many of the substances in cells are polar or charged, water is the solvent for most of the chemistry of life.

What to remember

Water is polar, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other. Cohesion, adhesion, surface tension, a high specific heat, a high heat of vaporization and its power as a solvent all follow from that one fact. On the exam, when a question asks why water behaves in a surprising way, start from the hydrogen bonds.
