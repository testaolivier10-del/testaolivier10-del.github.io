Factual claims in an AP Biology video (Unit 1: elements of life, intro to macromolecules), including what illustrations show.
1. A bean plant grown in a pot of soil gains far more mass than the dried soil loses; the soil loses only a tiny amount (roughly a pinch, a gram or less), as in van Helmont's willow experiment.
2. An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom (carbon, oxygen, gold are elements).
3. What makes an atom carbon is its number of protons; every carbon atom has 6 protons.
4. Most carbon has 6 neutrons (carbon 12); some has 7 (carbon 13); a little has 8 (carbon 14).
5. Versions of an element with different numbers of neutrons are isotopes.
6. Some isotopes are unstable and give off radiation as they break down (radioactive isotopes), e.g. carbon 14, phosphorus 32, sulfur 35.
7. Chemistry depends mainly on electrons; an isotope (neutral atom) has the same number of electrons as the normal atom, so isotopes behave almost identically in chemistry.
8. A cell uses radioactive phosphorus the same way as normal phosphorus, so scientists can track where those atoms go (a tracer).
9. Living things cannot make atoms; every atom in an organism was taken in from its surroundings.
10. Photosynthesis: plants use light energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen.
11. Cellular respiration: nearly every cell, including plant cells, breaks sugar down with oxygen, releasing carbon dioxide and water and capturing energy in ATP.
12. Atoms cycle; energy enters as light, is stored in chemical bonds for a while, and leaves as heat. "Matter cycles, energy flows."
13. Most of a plant's (dry) mass is built from carbon dioxide from the air plus water, not from soil.
14. The small mass the soil loses is minerals taken up by the roots.
15. Glucose is C6H12O6: 6 C, 12 H, 6 O, 24 atoms. In photosynthesis the carbon in glucose comes from CO2 and the hydrogen from water.
16. About 25 elements are found in living things; six (C, H, O, N, P, S) make up roughly 98% of a cell's mass. Students remember them as CHONPS.
17. Human body by mass: about 65% oxygen, 18% carbon, 10% hydrogen, 3% nitrogen, 1% phosphorus, a tiny bit (about 0.3%) of sulfur; much of the O and H is in water.
18. Carbon is the skeleton of every organic molecule. H and O are in nearly all biological molecules and in water.
19. Nitrogen is in every protein (every amino acid) and in DNA.
20. Phosphorus is in DNA, ATP, and the phospholipids that make up cell membranes.
21. Sulfur is in a couple of the amino acids (cysteine and methionine), so it ends up in many proteins.
22. Plant food labels list N, P, K = nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium; these are nutrients a plant cannot get from air or water (it takes them from soil).
23. A plant with light, air and water but almost no nitrogen can still make sugar (sugar contains only C, H, O), but cannot build proteins properly and cannot grow normally.
24. Molecules built on carbon are organic; water, carbon dioxide and salts are inorganic.
25. Each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds; carbons link into long chains, branched chains and rings; two carbons can share two pairs of electrons in a double bond. The carbon framework is the carbon skeleton.
26. A molecule of only C and H is a hydrocarbon; candle wax (paraffin) is a hydrocarbon. C-H bonds are nonpolar, so hydrocarbons do not mix with water.
27. Glucose and fructose both have the formula C6H12O6 but their atoms are bonded differently (different structures and shapes); fructose tastes much sweeter than glucose.
28. Molecules with the same formula but a different structure are isomers. In biology, shape decides what a molecule can do.
29. Ethanol (drinking alcohol) and dimethyl ether (a gas used as an aerosol spray propellant) both have the formula C2H6O; they are isomers with different structures and properties.
30. Functional groups are clusters of atoms attached to the carbon skeleton; each behaves about the same whatever molecule it is on.
31. Hydroxyl is -OH, polar, hydrogen bonds with water and makes a molecule more soluble. Carbonyl is C=O, polar. Both are found in sugars.
32. Carboxyl is -COOH and is an acid: it releases H+ (becoming -COO-). Amino is -NH2 and is a base: it picks up H+ (becoming -NH3+). Every amino acid has both.
33. Phosphate is acidic and negatively charged; found in DNA (nucleic acids) and ATP.
34. Sulfhydryl is -SH; two -SH groups can bond to each other (disulfide bridge), linking two parts of a protein.
35. Methyl is -CH3, nonpolar; methyl groups on DNA can change how a gene is read (expressed).
36. Cysteine: HS-CH2-CH(NH2)-COOH carries a sulfhydryl, an amino and a carboxyl group; elements C, H, O, N, S; without sulfur a cell cannot make cysteine or proteins that contain it.
37. Four families of biological molecules: carbohydrates (sugars and larger molecules built from them, like starch in bread; C, H, O); lipids (fats and oils, like butter; mostly C and H with a little O; hydrophobic); proteins (chains of amino acids, e.g. in cheese; every amino acid has C, H, O, N, a few have S); nucleic acids (DNA and relatives, store genetic information; C, H, O, N, P).
38. Every cell in bread (wheat), cheese, and beans had DNA. (Check: cheese contains microbial cells and some milk cells; bread is baked: is it fair to say "every cell in that bread, cheese and beans had" nucleic acids, meaning the living cells they came from?)
39. ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the energy carrier and has a chain of three phosphate groups.
40. Cells fed sulfur 35 show the label almost entirely in proteins; carbohydrates and DNA contain no sulfur.
41. Macromolecules are very large molecules; most are polymers ("many parts"): long chains of small, similar monomers ("one part") linked by covalent bonds.
42. A polymer can be one kind of monomer repeated or many kinds in a particular order.
43. Carbohydrates (the large ones), proteins and nucleic acids are polymers; lipids are not true polymers (not long chains of repeating monomers).
44. Dehydration synthesis: one monomer gives up -OH, the other -H; they form H2O which is released; the monomers are joined by a new covalent bond. Also called condensation.
45. Each bond formed releases one water; a straight chain of 50 monomers has 49 bonds and releases 49 waters; n monomers release n - 1.
46. Hydrolysis is the reverse: water is split across the bond, H to one monomer, OH to the other, the bond breaks. Hydrolysis means "splitting with water".
47. Digestion is mostly hydrolysis: large molecules in food are cut into monomers small enough to be absorbed into the blood.
48. Cells then use dehydration synthesis (simple model) to build their own polymers from those monomers.
49. Uncatalyzed hydrolysis of a polymer in water can take years; enzymes (proteins) speed up chemical reactions. (Note: some enzymes are RNA; is "enzymes, proteins that speed up reactions" acceptable at AP level?)
50. Each kind of polymer has its own enzymes; salivary amylase cuts starch and does nothing to proteins.
51. Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose; the linkages are angled differently (alpha vs beta); starch is digestible food, cellulose is tough fiber humans cannot digest.
52. Hydrolysis products weigh more than the polymer, because water's atoms are added for each bond broken.
53. Some of the carbon in exhaled breath (CO2) came from food eaten an hour earlier; exhaled CO2 can be taken up by plants in photosynthesis.

Practice questions (answer these yourself):
Q1. Glucose is C6H12O6. How many atoms of each element are in one molecule, and where does a plant get the carbon and the hydrogen to build it?
Q2. A bean plant has plenty of light, air and water, but its soil has almost no nitrogen. Can it still make sugar? Can it make proteins?
Q3. Two molecules both have the formula C2H6O. One is the alcohol in drinks, the other is a gas used in spray cans. How can they be different molecules, and what is the word for them?
Q4. Cysteine is HS-CH2-CH(NH2)-COOH. Name its three functional groups and the elements a cell needs to make it.
Q5. Cells are fed sulfur 35, a radioactive tracer. Which family of molecules does the radioactive sulfur show up in?
Q6. A cell links ten sugar monomers into one straight chain. How many water molecules are released? When the chain is hydrolyzed again, do the monomers weigh more or less than the chain?
