## Four levels of training
[l1] We'll start with who shows up. People who give care before the hospital in the United States are trained to one of four national levels. Each level includes everything in the level below it and then adds more, so you can picture them as four steps on a staircase.
[l2] The first step is the emergency medical responder, or EMR. An EMR is often the first trained person on scene, and many of them are police officers or firefighters. An EMR calls in more help and gives basic care for the problems that kill fastest. That means opening the airway, which is the path air takes to the lungs, stopping heavy bleeding, and starting CPR with an AED, a machine that can shock a heart out of a deadly rhythm.
[l3] The second step is the emergency medical technician, the EMT, and that's the level you're training for. In most places, it's the minimum certification for staffing an ambulance. An EMT assesses the patient and gives basic care for illness and injury. That care includes oxygen and a short list of medications, and some of those are the patient's own prescriptions. Then the EMT transports the patient to the hospital.
[l4] So what does the third step add? That step is the advanced EMT, or AEMT. An advanced EMT does everything that an EMT does, and then adds a few advanced skills. An AEMT can start an IV, which is a line into a vein, or an IO, which is a line into a bone, so fluids can go straight in. An AEMT can also place certain advanced airway devices and give several more medications.
[l5] The top step is the paramedic. That's the highest of the four levels. A paramedic has all the skills of the EMT and of the advanced EMT. On top of those, a paramedic adds advanced assessment and decision making, like reading a tracing of the heart's electrical signals and choosing from a much longer list of drugs.
[l6 STORY] Those four steps are about to arrive at Walt's store. Deputy Ruiz, from the sheriff's office, gets there first. He's an EMR, so he sits Walt down, keeps him calm, and sets an AED within reach. Priya's ambulance pulls up next. She tells Walt her name, and she starts her assessment while her partner Marcus, who's an advanced EMT, gets an IV ready. A paramedic named Joan is already driving out to meet them. So Walt gets four people at four levels, and each one adds something the last one couldn't.

## What an EMT is responsible for
[j1] So what is an EMT actually responsible for on a call? The list has an order, and the order matters. Your own safety comes first, and I know that can sound selfish. But an injured EMT can't help anybody, and now the scene has two patients. Your crew comes next, for the same reason. The patient comes after that, and then the bystanders.
[j2] Once the scene is safe, assessment always comes before care, because you can't treat what you haven't found. After care, you lift and move the patient safely, then you transport, and then you transfer care at the hospital. You never leave a patient before that handoff is complete. And through all of it, you speak up for what the patient needs, which is called being the patient's advocate.
[j3] Running through that list isn't enough by itself, though, because the job also takes judgment. To think like an EMT, you gather information from the scene, from your assessment, and from the patient, and then you put all of that together to decide. When you don't have enough information, you ask more questions or examine the patient further before you act.
[j4 STORY] Priya is doing exactly that with Walt. Before she reaches for the aspirin, she asks about allergies and bleeding problems. Aspirin makes blood clot less, which can help the heart but is dangerous for someone who already bleeds easily. Then she has to think about where Walt should go. A heart attack happens when an artery that feeds the heart gets blocked. The nearest hospital is eight minutes away, but it doesn't have the lab that can open that artery with a thin tube. A heart center that does is twenty five minutes away. The closest hospital isn't always the right one... so Priya's crew heads for the heart center, just as their local rules direct.
[j5] And what does the job ask from you, the person doing it? A commonly cited benchmark is being able to lift and carry about a hundred and twenty five pounds. You need good eyesight, including color vision, since lips or skin turning pale or blue is a real clue. A good EMT also stays calm under pressure, listens without judging, and treats every patient fairly.
[p2q] This one's for you to try. You pull up to a car crash on a dark highway. The driver is slumped over the wheel, fuel is leaking, and traffic is still passing. What's your first responsibility? And once you reach a patient, which one should usually come first, assessment or care?
[p2a] Your first responsibility is your own safety, so you deal with the traffic and the fuel before you go to the driver. The trap is running straight to the patient, which is the instinct almost everyone has. Then, as a general rule, you assess before you treat, because you can't treat what you haven't found.

## Who stands behind the EMT
[m1] That brings us back to the question from the start. Who says Priya can give Walt anything at all? Every EMS system operates under the authority of a physician, who is called the Medical Director. The Medical Director has the final responsibility for patient care in that system. This physician writes the protocols, which are the written plans for treating each kind of problem, and also oversees the training and helps review the care. When an EMT treats a patient, that EMT is acting on the physician's authority.
[m2] That authority reaches you in two ways, and together they're called medical direction. The first is off line medical direction, which means standing orders. Those are written instructions that let you act without calling anyone, so nobody has to be on the line with you. The second is on line medical direction, where a physician on duty gives you an order in real time, over the phone or the radio.
[m3 STORY] Here's how both kinds look with Walt. Priya's protocols include a standing order for chest pain that might be coming from the heart. Once she's checked for allergies and bleeding, she hands him four small aspirin tablets to chew. She doesn't have to ask anyone... because the physician already gave that order in writing. Walt also carries his own nitroglycerin tablets. Nitroglycerin can drop a patient's blood pressure, so in Priya's system, the protocol says to call first. She gets a physician on the radio, reports what she's found, and hears the order before she helps Walt take one.
[m4] The Medical Director also checks how the care went. After a call, the EMT writes a report, and the Medical Director's team reviews reports like that one to find problems and fix them. So the physician you rarely see is there before the call, in the protocols, and after the call, in the review.
[p3q] It's your turn again. Naloxone is a drug that reverses an opioid overdose. Your protocol says that you may give it to a patient with a suspected overdose, and you don't need to call anyone. Which kind of medical direction are you using? And if you radio a physician for an order instead, which kind is that?
[p3a] The naloxone is off line medical direction, because it's a standing order. The radio call is on line medical direction, since the order comes from a physician in real time. This is where people slip. They think that off line means no doctor is involved. The physician is involved, and simply gave the order ahead of time, in writing.

## The whole system in one picture
[rc] We can put the whole system in one picture. There are four levels, the emergency medical responder, the EMT, the advanced EMT, and the paramedic, and each one adds to the level below. On a call, your safety comes first, and assessment comes before care. And a physician called the Medical Director stands behind everything you do, through standing orders, which are off line, and through real time orders, which are on line.
