# Claims to check (yt-nremt-02, US EMS, written for EMT students). Sources to prefer: the 2019 National EMS Scope of Practice Model (NHTSA / ems.gov), National EMS Education Standards (2021), nremt.org, current AHA guidelines, standard EMT textbooks.
1. In the US, prehospital providers are trained to four nationally recognized levels: EMR, EMT, AEMT, paramedic, and each level includes the skills of the level below.
2. An EMR (emergency medical responder) is often the first trained person on scene; many are police officers or firefighters; they call for more help and give basic care for immediate life threats: opening an airway, stopping heavy bleeding, CPR with an AED.
3. The EMT is, in most places, the minimum certification for staffing an ambulance.
4. An EMT assesses patients, gives basic medical and trauma care including oxygen and a short list of medications, and transports.
5. An AEMT does everything an EMT does and adds: starting IV (vein) and IO (bone) access to give fluids, placing certain advanced airway devices (supraglottic), and several more medications.
6. The paramedic is the highest prehospital level; adds advanced assessment and decision making, including interpreting an ECG (heart tracing), and a much longer drug list.
7. Story: a sheriff's deputy trained as an EMR sits a chest pain patient down, keeps him calm and sets an AED within reach (is that within EMR scope and sensible?). Under the national model aspirin is not an EMR level medication (it starts at EMT). Is that right?
8. Starting an IV is not in the EMT's scope under the national model; it starts at AEMT.
9. An EMT's responsibilities in order: personal safety first, then crew, patient, bystanders; patient assessment before care; then lifting and moving, transport, transfer of care; never abandon the patient before the handoff; patient advocacy.
10. "Think like an EMT" (critical decision making): gather information from the scene, assessment and patient, synthesize, and if information is not enough, ask more questions or examine further before acting.
11. A drug that helps one patient can harm another; before aspirin an EMT asks about allergy and bleeding problems (correct contraindication screening for aspirin in suspected ACS?).
12. The closest hospital is not always the right destination; a hospital without a cardiac catheterization lab cannot open a blocked coronary artery (PCI); for a heart attack (STEMI) a longer transport to a PCI capable center may be better (story: 8 minutes vs 25 minutes; is 25 min a reasonable bypass?).
13. An EMT should be able to lift and carry about 125 pounds (57 kg).
14. Color vision matters because changes in the color of skin and lips are assessment clues.
15. Every EMS system operates under the authority of a physician Medical Director, who has ultimate responsibility for patient care: writes protocols, oversees training, takes part in quality review.
16. An EMT treating a patient acts on the Medical Director's authority.
17. Off line medical direction = standing orders (written protocols) that let the EMT act without contacting a physician. On line medical direction = a real time order by phone or radio from an on duty physician.
18. Story: in many EMS systems an EMT may give aspirin (chewed) for chest pain of suspected cardiac origin under a standing order; picture shows four chewable low dose tablets (4 x 81 mg = 324 mg). Dose and route correct?
19. Story: an EMT may assist a patient with the patient's own prescribed nitroglycerin; in some systems this requires on line medical direction first (script says "in Priya's system, the protocol says to call first"). Plausible and accurate?
20. National Registry (NREMT) certification renews every two years; state certification or license periods vary, roughly two to four years.
21. Recertification requires continuing education.
22. The NREMT (National Registry of Emergency Medical Technicians) sets a national standard and registers EMRs, EMTs, AEMTs and paramedics.
23. To gain National Registry certification as an EMT you pass the computer based cognitive (knowledge) exam and your psychomotor skills are verified by the state or the training program (no separate NREMT run practical exam for EMTs).
24. Reciprocity = transfer of certification from one state to another; national registration helps with it.
25. Quality improvement (QI) = a system's continuous self review to find and fix problems; EMTs contribute through accurate complete reports, critiquing their own performance, maintaining equipment (also continuing education, feedback).
26. The Medical Director (or the QI process) may review patient care reports.
27. Naloxone for suspected opioid overdose under a standing order is an example of off line medical direction (and naloxone is within EMT scope).
28. Public health = protecting the health of a whole population (clean water, vaccination). EMS takes part: spotting fall hazards in homes, car seat clinics, and call volume trends as early warning (overdoses, influenza).
29. The wider role is called mobile integrated health care (MIH), also called community paramedicine.
30. Evidence based practice: treatments supported by scientific evidence rather than tradition; the measure that matters is patient outcome (long term), not only survival to the hospital.
31. Peer reviewed research has been checked by other experts before a journal publishes it.
32. For decades nearly every chest pain patient was given oxygen; trials (for example AVOID 2015, DETO2X-AMI 2017) found supplemental oxygen does not help heart attack patients with normal oxygen saturation, and excess oxygen may harm. Current guidance withholds oxygen when saturation is normal (state the current AHA threshold).
33. Story: a pulse oximeter on the finger reads 97 percent, so the EMT gives no oxygen. Correct under current guidance?
34. New evidence is evaluated together with the Medical Director and protocols change if the evidence supports it.
35. Story: a paramedic reads (interprets) the 12 lead ECG and alerts the heart center ahead of arrival (prehospital STEMI alert). Accurate to paramedic scope and practice?
36. Visual: an IO needle is drawn in the shin bone (proximal tibia) as a typical site. OK?
37. Visual: skin and lip colour swatches labelled normal, pale and blue (cyanosis). OK?

# Practice questions (answer them yourself before reading anything else; give your own answers)
P1. A patient needs an IV started so she can get fluids. What is the lowest level of provider who can start it? Which level is the usual minimum for staffing an ambulance?
P2. You pull up to a car crash on a dark highway. The driver is slumped over the wheel, fuel is leaking and traffic is still passing. What is your first responsibility? Once you reach the driver, which comes first, assessment or care?
P3. Your protocol says you may give naloxone to a patient with a suspected opioid overdose without calling anyone. Which kind of medical direction is that? If you radio a physician for an order instead, which kind is that?
P4. A study in a peer reviewed journal finds that a treatment your service has used for years does not improve how patients do. Should the service keep using it because that is how it has always been done? Who helps decide?
