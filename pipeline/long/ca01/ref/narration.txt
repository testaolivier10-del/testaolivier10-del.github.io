## Chapter: What a function is
[f1] A function is a rule that takes an input and gives back exactly ONE output. We write f of x for the output when the input is x. So f of x equals x squared minus three x means: square the input, then subtract three times the input. The x is just a placeholder, an empty slot waiting for a number.
[f2] To evaluate, put the input into every slot, and put it in parentheses. f of negative two is negative two, in parentheses, squared, minus three times negative two. That's four plus six, so ten. Without the parentheses, negative two squared reads as negative four. One missing pair of parentheses, and the answer is wrong.
[f3] The input can be a whole expression, too. What's f of a plus one? Every x becomes a plus one, in parentheses. Expand it, collect the terms, and you get a squared minus a minus two. Notice that's not f of a, plus one. The function acts on the whole input at once.
[f4] Some inputs a rule can't take. The domain is the set of inputs it accepts. The range is the set of outputs it actually makes. In this course, two things shrink a domain. A denominator can never be zero. And a square root needs a number that's zero or more.
[f5] Try h of x equals the square root of x plus four, over x minus one. The root needs x plus four to be at least zero, so x is at least negative four. The bottom can't be zero, so x can't be one. Domain: x at least negative four, and x not equal to one. And negative four itself is fine. The square root of zero is just zero.
[f6 STORY] Marco's jar is a function too. The input is time since he fed it, in hours. The output is the height of the starter. At any one moment, the jar has exactly ONE height. It can't be four centimeters and six at the same time. And the domain? It starts at zero, the feeding. Before that, this batch didn't exist yet. So h of zero equals four, and h of two is what he's waiting to find.
[p1q (pause 6s for the viewer to try)] Your turn, two quick ones. Let g of x equal x squared plus two x. Find g of negative three. Then find the domain of k of x equals the square root of x minus two, over x minus five.
[p1a] For the first one: negative three, in parentheses, squared is nine. Plus two times negative three is minus six. So g of negative three is three. The trap is squaring without the parentheses. That gives negative nine minus six, negative fifteen. For the domain: the root needs x minus two at least zero, so x is at least two. The bottom needs x not equal to five. So x at least two, and x not five.

## Chapter: Reading graphs and piecewise functions
[g1] The graph of f is every point x comma f of x. So f of a is a HEIGHT. Go to a on the horizontal axis, then go up or down to the graph, and read how high it is there.
[g2] Graphs in calculus often come in pieces, so watch the dots. A filled dot is a point ON the graph. An open circle marks a point the graph comes close to, but doesn't include. Here, the open circle at one comma three point five is not on the graph. The filled dot at one comma five is. So f of one is five.
[g3] Three more readings you'll use all year. To solve f of x equals k, draw the flat line y equals k, and read the x values where it meets the graph. f is positive where the graph is above the x axis. And the range runs from the lowest height to the highest, minding any open circles at the ends.
[g4 STORY] Marco keeps a chart of his starter taped to the wall. Here's last night. Before the feeding, the old starter had risen up to nine centimeters. At nine p.m., he poured most of it out and topped it up with fresh flour and water. The height dropped to four in an instant. On the chart, that's an open circle at the old height, and a filled dot at the new one. At the moment of feeding, the jar is at four, not nine.
[g5] A rule that switches formulas like that is a piecewise function. Here's one: x squared when x is less than two, and three x minus one when x is two or more. To evaluate, first ask which piece the input belongs to, then use only that formula. The boundary goes wherever the or equal to sign is. So f of two uses three x minus one, and gives five. But f of one point nine uses x squared, three point six one. Calculus cares a LOT about boundaries like this one, so slow down there.
[p2q (pause 6s for the viewer to try)] Your turn. f of x is x plus one when x is less than three, and two x when x is three or more. Find f of three, and f of two point nine. Then: is the point three comma four on the graph?
[p2a] Three goes with the or equal to sign, so f of three is two times three, six. Two point nine is less than three, so it uses x plus one: three point nine. And three comma four? That's where the first piece is heading, but it stops just short. It's an open circle, so no, it's not on the graph. The trap is plugging three into x plus one, just because it's the formula written first.

## Chapter: Lines, composites and moving graphs
[l1] Lines show up everywhere in calculus, so let's sharpen them. The slope of a line is rise over run: y two minus y one, over x two minus x one. Subtract in the same order, top and bottom. Through one comma four and three comma negative two, that's negative six over two. A slope of negative three, so the line goes down to the right.
[l2] Calculus writes lines in point slope form. y minus y one equals m, times x minus x one. Here, y minus four equals negative three, times x minus one. Why this form? Because calculus usually knows exactly one point and the slope there. And the exam accepts it as it is. No need to rearrange.
[l3] A composite function feeds one function's output into another. f of g of x means: do g first, then do f to the result. Work from the inside out. With f of x equals x squared and g of x equals three x minus one, f of g of x is three x minus one, all squared. But g of f of x is three x squared, minus one. Different! The order matters.
[l4] Going backward matters just as much. The square root of x squared plus one is a composite. The inside is x squared plus one. The outside is the square root. In Unit 3, you'll split functions into inside and outside like this all the time.
[l5] Now, moving graphs. f of x, plus k, moves the graph up k. Negative f of x flips it over the x axis. And f of x minus h moves it RIGHT h. That one feels backward, doesn't it? Here's a way to see why.
[l6 STORY] Say Marco feeds tomorrow's starter at eleven, two hours later than tonight. It grows the same way, so it reaches every height two hours later. The whole curve slides right by two. To read the new jar at time t, read the old curve at t minus two. That's h of t minus two: a minus sign, and a move to the right. And if he sets the jar on a two centimeter stand, every height goes up two. That's h of t, plus two.
[l7] One more kind of function before we practice. Firing up the oven costs Marco forty dollars, and each loaf adds two dollars of flour. So the cost per loaf is two n plus forty, over n. One polynomial over another is called a rational function. Bake more loaves, and the cost per loaf gets closer and closer to two dollars, the ratio of the leading numbers. That flat line is a horizontal asymptote. And where the bottom is zero but the top isn't, you get a vertical asymptote. When top and bottom are BOTH zero... something more interesting happens. That's Unit 1.
[p3q (pause 6s for the viewer to try)] Your turn. Let f of x equal x squared, and g of x equal x minus four. Find f of g of one, and g of f of one. Then: how does the graph of x plus three, squared, sit compared with x squared?
[p3a] Inside first. g of one is negative three, and f of negative three is nine. The other way, f of one is one, and g of one... is negative three. Nine versus negative three. Order matters. And x plus three is x minus negative three, so the graph moves LEFT three. The trap is reading the plus sign as a move to the right.

## Chapter: Exponent rules
[e1] Calculus has one short rule that handles every power of x. But it only sees powers. So before you use it, you rewrite fractions and roots as exponents. Here's that toolkit. An exponent counts repeated multiplication. x cubed is x times x times x.
[e2 STORY] Marco's starter shows exactly that. Every four hours, it doubles. After one stretch of four hours, it's two times as tall. After two stretches, two times two. After three stretches, two times two times two: two cubed, eight times as tall. The exponent counts the doublings.
[e3] Every rule comes from counting factors. x cubed times x squared: three factors, then two more, five in all. So you add the exponents. x to the fifth over x squared: two factors cancel, three are left. So you subtract. x cubed, all squared, is two groups of three, six factors. So you multiply. And anything nonzero to the power zero is one.
[e4] Now, why should x to the negative two mean one over x squared? Take x cubed over x to the fifth. Canceling leaves one over x squared. The subtraction rule says x to the three minus five, x to the negative two. Both are right, so they're the same thing. And a negative exponent never makes a number negative. Two to the negative three is one eighth, a small positive number.
[e5 STORY] In the jar, a negative exponent is just time running backward. Four hours before the feeding, there was no starter to measure. But if this batch had been growing then, it would have been two to the negative one times as tall. Half. Back up one doubling, and you divide by two.
[e6] The slip that costs the most points here is moving too much. An exponent applies only to the base it touches. Three x to the negative two is three over x squared. Only the x goes down. But three x, in parentheses, to the negative two is one over nine x squared. So five over x cubed, minus one over two x, becomes five x to the negative three, minus one half x to the negative one. The two is just a number, so it stays as one half.
[e7] Now fractional exponents. What should x to the one half be? Square it: x to the one half times two is x to the one. So x to the one half is the number whose square is x. That's the square root of x. In a fraction exponent, the bottom is a root, and the top is a power. Take the root first, so the numbers stay small. Eight to the two thirds: the cube root of eight is two, and two squared is four.
[e8 STORY] And now Marco's question. Two hours is half of one doubling, so the starter has grown by two to the one half, the square root of two. That's about one point four one. Not one and a half. Four times one point four one... is about five point seven centimeters. He checks the jar. Five point seven, almost exactly. Halfway in TIME is not halfway in height.
[e9] Last, the slips. Exponent rules work on products. They do NOT work across a plus sign. x plus y, squared, is not x squared plus y squared. Test it: one plus two, squared, is nine, not five. The square root of x squared plus nine is not x plus three. Try x equals four: the square root of twenty five is five, not seven. And negative three squared is negative nine, because the square touches only the three. Testing with a number takes ten seconds, and it catches most of these slips.
[p4q (pause 6s for the viewer to try)] Your turn. Write six over x squared, plus the square root of x, as a sum of powers of x. Then evaluate twenty seven to the two thirds.
[p4a] Six over x squared is six x to the negative two. The six stays on top. The square root of x is x to the one half. For twenty seven to the two thirds, take the root first: the cube root of twenty seven is three, and three squared is nine. The traps: writing six x to the negative two as six x, all to the negative two, or multiplying twenty seven by two thirds to get eighteen.

## Chapter: Recap
[rc] So here's the whole toolkit in one picture. A function gives one output per input. Parentheses around every input, and watch the domain. On a graph, f of a is a height. Filled dots count, open circles don't. Composites work inside out, and f of x minus h moves right. And exponents: negative means one over, a fraction means a root, and nothing crosses a plus sign.
