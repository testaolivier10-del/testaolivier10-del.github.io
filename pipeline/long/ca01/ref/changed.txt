[hook] It's eleven at night, and Marco is alone in the bakery. On the bench sits a jar of sourdough starter. He fed it at nine, when it stood four centimeters tall. His rubber band marks eight, double that. When it reaches the band, the morning's dough can be mixed. His notes say this starter doubles about every four hours. It's been two. So how tall should it be right now? Halfway to the band? Not quite.
[f4] Some inputs a rule just can't take. Which ones? The domain is the set of inputs it accepts, and the range is the set of outputs it actually makes. For now, two things shrink a domain. Logarithms will add a third later. A denominator can never be zero. And a square root needs zero or more.
[f6] Marco's jar is a function too. Call it s, for starter. The input is the time since he fed it, in hours. The output is the height. At any one moment, the jar has exactly ONE height. And the domain? It starts at zero, the feeding. Before nine, this batch didn't exist yet, so the situation itself limits the domain. So s of zero is four, and s of two is what he's waiting to find.
[g4] Marco keeps a chart of his starter taped to the wall, against the clock. Here's tonight. Before the feeding, the old starter had risen to nine centimeters. At nine, he poured most of it out and topped it up with fresh flour and water. The height dropped to four in an instant. On the chart, that's an open circle at the old height, and a filled dot at the new one: at nine o'clock, the jar is at four.
[l6] Say he had fed the starter at eleven instead of nine, two hours later on the clock. It grows the same way, so it reaches every height two hours later. The whole curve on his chart slides right by two. To read the new jar at clock time t, read the old curve two hours earlier, at t minus two. A minus sign, and a move to the right.
[l7] One more kind of function. Firing up the oven costs Marco forty dollars, plus two dollars of flour per loaf. For n loaves, the cost per loaf is two n plus forty, over n. One polynomial over another: a rational function. Bake more loaves, and the forty spreads thinner, so the cost closes in on two dollars. That flat line is a horizontal asymptote. At n equals zero, the bottom is zero but the top isn't: a vertical asymptote. And when top and bottom are BOTH zero... something more interesting happens. That's Unit 1.
[p3q] Your turn. Let f of x equal x squared, and g of x equal x minus four. Find f of g of two, and g of f of two. Then: how does the graph of x plus three, all squared, sit compared with x squared?
[p3a] Inside first. g of two is negative two, and f of negative two is four. The other way, f of two is four, and g of four is zero. Four versus zero. Order matters. And x plus three is x minus negative three, so the graph moves LEFT three. The trap is reading the plus sign as a move to the right.
[e2] Marco's starter shows exactly that. His rough rule: every four hours, it doubles. After one stretch, it's two times as tall. After three stretches, two times two times two: two cubed, eight times as tall. The exponent counts the doublings. Real starters start slowly and level off near their peak, but steady doubling is a good first model.
[e3] Every rule comes from counting factors. x cubed times x squared. How many factors? Three, then two more. Five. So you add the exponents. x to the fifth over x squared: two factors cancel, three are left. So you subtract. x cubed, all squared, is two groups of three. Six factors, so you multiply. And x cubed over x cubed is one, but the subtraction rule says x to the zero. So any nonzero number to the zero is one.
[e7] Now fractional exponents. What should x to the one half be? Square it, and you double the exponent. One half times two is one, so you get plain x. So x to the one half is the number whose square is x: the square root of x, for x at least zero. In a fraction exponent, the bottom is a root, and the top is a power. Root first keeps the numbers small. Eight to the two thirds: the cube root of eight is two, and two squared is four.
[e8] And now Marco's question. Two hours is half of one doubling. Say two hours multiplies the height by some number r. Then four hours multiplies it by r times r, and that has to be two. So r is the square root of two, two to the one half. About one point four one four. Halfway would be six centimeters. But four times one point four one four... is about five point seven. He checks the jar. Five point seven, almost exactly. Halfway in TIME... is not halfway in height.
[e9] Now, where people trip. Exponent rules work on products. They do NOT work across a plus sign. x plus y, squared, is not x squared plus y squared. Test it: one plus two, squared, is nine, not five. The square root of the whole thing, x squared plus nine, is not x plus three. Try x equals four: the square root of twenty five is five, not seven. Testing with a number takes ten seconds, and it catches most of these slips.
[p4a] Six over x squared is six x to the negative two. The six stays on top. The square root of x is x to the one half. For twenty seven to the two thirds, take the root first: the cube root of twenty seven is three, and three squared is nine. The traps: putting the six under the power too, which means one over thirty six x squared. Or multiplying twenty seven by two thirds to get eighteen.
