One idea (AP Microeconomics, marginal analysis and consumer choice, CED 1.6; site page microeconomics/notes/marginal-analysis.html): what a thing is worth in a trade follows its marginal utility, what ONE MORE unit adds, not how useful the good is in total. Plentiful water: the next bottle adds little. Scarce diamonds: the next one adds a lot. Lost in a desert, the next bottle of water is worth everything.

## Narration (vo.txt, exactly this; about 520 characters)
The diamond water paradox, in one trade. He's been lost in the desert for two days, and a stranger wants his diamond ring for one bottle of water. Would you take it? Back home, a diamond costs thousands and a bottle of water costs about a dollar, even though water keeps you alive. Economists answered that with one word... MARGINAL. What you'll pay follows what the NEXT unit adds. At home there's always more water, so one more bottle adds almost nothing. Out here, it's the only one. He takes the trade. Right now, the cheapest thing in the world is worth more than a diamond.

## Facts (checked)
- The paradox of value: Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), water very useful but cheap, diamonds of little use but dear. Resolved in the 1870s by marginal utility (Jevons, Menger, Walras): exchange value tracks marginal utility, not total utility.
- Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit of a good adds less satisfaction than the one before; plentiful goods have low marginal utility at the margin.
- Prices: a bottle of water about a dollar in the US; a diamond ring thousands. Keep claims to these round words.
- Do not claim people die after exactly two days without water; "two days lost" is the story's situation only. Show thirst honestly (cracked lips, squint, dusty clothes), not collapse.
