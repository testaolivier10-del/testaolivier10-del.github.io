Search phrase: "Great Smog of London" (spoken in the first sentence; on screen in the first 3 s as title text: THE GREAT SMOG OF LONDON).
One idea (AP Environmental Science 7.3, thermal inversion): normally warm smoke rises through cooler air and drifts away; in a thermal inversion a layer of warm air sits above the cold air at the ground like a lid, so the smoke stops rising and stays where people breathe.

## Narration (vo.txt, exactly this)
In the Great Smog of London, this bus conductor has to guide his own bus on foot, with a flare. The driver can't see the road. So where is it all coming from? The city's own coal fires, on a cold, still week. Normally smoke is warmer than the air above it, so it rises and drifts away. But this week a layer of warm air has settled over London... like a LID. The smoke climbs, meets air warmer than itself, and stops. It's called a thermal inversion. For five days, London breathes its own smoke. At least four thousand people die. And he's still out in front, guiding the bus home.

## Facts (checked; every drawing must match)
- The Great Smog: London, 5 to 9 December 1952 (five days). A high pressure system (anticyclone) sat over a cold, windless city and caused a temperature inversion: cool still air at the ground trapped under a layer of warmer air. Coal smoke from homes and power stations could not disperse.
- Visibility fell to a few metres. Conductors walked ahead of buses with torches and flares to guide the drivers; most road transport stopped.
- Deaths: the government's count at the time was about 4,000; later research estimates 10,000 to 12,000. It led to the Clean Air Act of 1956. (On screen you may show "about 4,000 counted, later estimates up to 12,000". Do not show any other number.)
- The physics to draw: smoke rises only while it is warmer (lighter) than the air around it. Normal day: air gets colder with height, smoke keeps rising and spreads away high up. Inversion: cold air at the ground, a WARM layer above it; the smoke rises through the cold air, reaches the warm layer, cannot rise further, spreads sideways under it and fills the cold layer down to the street. Draw the warm layer as a warm coloured band (amber or rose) ABOVE a cold blue grey layer, with labels WARM AIR (above) and COLD AIR (below). Never draw the warm layer at the ground.
- Period details: a 1950s London double decker bus (red, half cab front, round headlamps, destination blind; use an invented route number and no real operator name), gas style street lamps, brick terraces with chimney pots, a conductor in a dark uniform coat and peaked cap with a ticket machine strap. The flare burns with a hard orange white light and its own smoke. No modern things.
