## w1025-01-power-lift (NREMT, lifting and moving)
Narration: "She bends at the waist to lift the jump bag. That's the classic way EMTs hurt their backs. Rewind. Legs straight, the bag hangs way out in front of her spine. Her lower back works like a lever, and the farther out the weight, the harder those back muscles have to pull. So, power lift. Feet apart, bag close, back locked, and squat. Now the weight sits near her spine, and her big leg muscles do the lifting. Heavier bag next time. Pull it in closer, or lift faster?"
Claims: (1) bending at the waist with straight legs (stoop lift) with the load far from the body is a classic cause of EMS back injury; back injuries/strains are among the most common EMS worker injuries. (2) Lower back acts as a lever: torque about the low back = weight x horizontal distance; back extensor muscles have a short moment arm (about 5 cm) so they pull many times the load; farther load = more muscle force and spinal load. (3) The power lift (NREMT/Emergency Care): feet shoulder width apart, keep the load close, back locked/straight, squat by bending the knees, lift with the legs. (4) Intended answer to the end question: pull it in closer (lifting faster increases peak forces).
Motion list: 1) frame 0: EMT (traced cartoon paramedic, side view) bends at the waist with nearly straight knees (real motion capture CMU 26_09 "bend, pick up") reaching a heavy jump bag on the floor about a forearm's length in front of her toes; 2) at ~1.8 s a red glow pulses at her lower back and she winces/freezes; red X; 3) rewind effect; 4) frozen stoop pose: dashed horizontal line from her lower back (L5 region) to the bag shows the lever arm; a "back load" meter fills high; 5) she stands, steps close so the bag is between/just in front of her feet, feet apart, squats with bent knees and a straight, inclined back (CMU 69_70 squat pick up), grips, stands up using her legs, bag held close to her body; lever arm line is now short and the meter is low; 6) end card with two answer chips "PULL IT CLOSER" and "LIFT FASTER".

## w1025-02-free-throw (AP Statistics, probability, law of large numbers)
Narration: "Five missed free throws in a row. So the next one's due, right? Nope, that's the gambler's fallacy. If her shots are independent, the ball has no memory. She's a seventy percent shooter, so this one's still seventy percent. The law of large numbers is real, it's just slow. Over ten shots her percentage bounces all over. Over hundreds, it settles near seventy. Not because makes catch up. The old misses just get drowned out. Five makes in a row. On fire, or still seventy? Free practice for this on levlprep dot com."
Claims: (1) Believing a make is "due" after a run of misses is the gambler's fallacy. (2) If shots are independent with constant p = 0.7, P(make on the next shot) = 0.7 regardless of past results. (3) Law of large numbers: the running proportion of makes approaches p as the number of trials grows; with few trials it varies a lot. (4) LLN does not work by compensation: the absolute count of misses relative to expectation does not get "paid back"; earlier deviations are diluted by many later trials. (5) Is this in the AP Statistics course (2026-27 revised CED, check College Board)? (6) Caveat: research on the "hot hand" in basketball (Gilovich 1985 vs Miller & Sanjurjo 2018) — is the phrasing "if her shots are independent" enough to be accurate, and is the end question fair?
Motion list: 1) a girl at the free throw line on an outdoor court shoots (real motion capture CMU 124_04 free throw), ball flies on a physically computed parabola and clanks off the rim; tally of red Xs reaches 5; 2) "70%" shown for the next shot; 3) a live code-drawn graph: running make percentage vs number of shots, from a simulated sequence of independent shots with p = 0.7, wild for the first ~10 shots, settling toward a dashed 70% line over 300 shots; a counter shows shots taken; 4) end chips "ON FIRE" / "STILL 70%".

## w1025-03-dead-zone (AP Environmental Science, eutrophication)
Narration: "He fertilized his lawn right before a storm. Downhill, the lake is about to choke. Rain washes the extra nitrogen and phosphorus into the lake, and algae explode into a green bloom. Then they die and sink. Bacteria break them down and use up the oxygen, so the fish suffocate. That's eutrophication. Big ones leave dead zones. The fix is kind of boring. Use less, skip it before rain, and leave a strip of plants along the shore. Lawn mowed to the water, or a wild plant strip. Which one saves the fish?"
Claims: (1) Lawn fertilizer N and P run off in rain into lakes (nonpoint source). (2) Excess nutrients cause algal blooms. (3) Algae die, sink, decomposed by aerobic bacteria that consume dissolved oxygen, causing hypoxia and fish kills. (4) That process is eutrophication (cultural eutrophication); large hypoxic areas are called dead zones. (5) Fixes: apply less fertilizer, don't fertilize before heavy rain, vegetated riparian buffer strips reduce nutrient runoff. (6) Answer: wild plant strip.
Motion list: 1) man walks across a sloped lawn by a lake, tossing fertilizer granules from a bag by hand; dark clouds and rain start; 2) rain carries a tinted runoff stream down the slope into the lake; 3) camera moves down into the water: underwater scene, sunlight rays; green algae spread from the surface and thicken, light dims; 4) algae turn brown, sink to the bottom; a dissolved oxygen meter falls toward near zero as decomposition happens at the bottom; 5) fish slow, gasp near the surface, then float belly up; 6) payoff: same lake with a strip of tall plants along the shore catching the runoff; 7) chips "MOWED TO THE WATER" / "PLANT STRIP".

## w1025-04-head-rush (A&P, short term blood pressure regulation, orthostatic)
Narration: "He stands up fast, and his vision goes dark. Gravity just pulled about half a liter of blood down into his legs. Less blood gets back to his heart, so his blood pressure dips, and his brain runs short for a few seconds. Pressure sensors in his neck catch it. They tell his heart to speed up and his vessels to squeeze, and the pressure bounces back. That's why you stand up slower, or flex your calves first. Hot shower or cold room. Where's his next head rush worse?"
Claims: (1) On standing, roughly 500 mL (often quoted 300 to 800 mL) of blood shifts to the legs and lower abdomen. (2) Venous return falls, stroke volume and cardiac output fall, blood pressure dips briefly (initial orthostatic hypotension); brain perfusion drops for seconds causing lightheadedness / greying of vision. (3) Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus (neck) and aortic arch detect the drop; the brainstem raises heart rate and causes vasoconstriction (and venoconstriction); BP recovers within seconds to tens of seconds. (4) Standing up slowly and tensing/flexing calf or leg muscles first (calf muscle pump, counter maneuvers) help. (5) Intended answer: a hot shower is worse (heat dilates skin vessels, so more blood pools). Also: should the narration mention the aortic arch, or is "sensors in his neck" acceptable simplification?
Motion list: 1) young man sitting on a tall kitchen stool stands up quickly (real motion capture CMU 13_01 sit on high stool, stand up); edges of the frame darken (vision greying), he sways and catches the counter; 2) a round lens opens from his neck to a flat illustrated body diagram: dark red venous blood fills and widens the leg veins (more volume low), less flows up to the heart; a blood pressure trace dips; 3) at the carotid in the neck, a sensor glows and a signal travels to the brainstem; the heart beats faster (rate counter rises), vessel walls squeeze; the pressure trace recovers to baseline over several seconds; 4) back to him: vision clears, he stands steady, then flexes his calves; 5) chips "HOT SHOWER" / "COLD ROOM".
